# A Quantitative Evaluation of Constant-Time Analysis Tools
> To appear at the 42nd Annual Computer Security Applications Conference (ACSAC 2026; ePrint lists it as "Minor revision"), public version on IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive 2026/2682. University of Melbourne (Australia), Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy (Germany)
> 2026

Constant-time programming, which keeps a cryptographic implementation's running time independent of secret values, is the standard defense against timing side channels, but it is hard to get right in practice. More than 50 tools for checking it have been published, yet earlier comparisons have mostly relied on qualitative observations and case studies. This paper builds AutoBench-CT, a benchmark for comparing constant-time analysis tools quantitatively, and evaluates seven tools against the same criteria. It was received by ePrint on 2026-10-29 and approved for publication on 2026-10-31.

Source: https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2682

### Contribution
- Builds AutoBench-CT, a modular benchmark covering 64 cryptographic tasks, with ground truth manually verified for each individual violation.
- Introduces what the authors describe as the first unified metrics for Operational Performance, Detection Quality, and Usability, and applies them to seven tools.
- Claims to explain quantitatively why CT-grind, first released in 2010, is still the most widely used tool in practice.
- Identifies limitations that recur across tools and proposes a composite pipeline that combines tools to get the strongest constant-time guarantees in practice.

### Evaluation
- Seven constant-time analysis tools are evaluated on 64 cryptographic tasks with manually verified ground truth.
- Metrics fall along three axes: operational performance, detection quality, and usability. The public abstract page, however, names only CT-grind among the seven tools and gives neither per-tool figures such as precision and recall nor the exact metric definitions.
- Reliability of the metrics: verifying ground truth by hand at the level of individual violations makes the comparison more trustworthy. On the other hand, results can depend heavily on which libraries and primitives make up the 64 tasks, and that composition could not be checked this time.

#### Comment
- This is a rare quantitative comparison of constant-time checkers framed around what actually works in practice. Teams that maintain cryptographic libraries or want to add constant-time checks to CI should find it directly useful.

- Venue: ACSAC is a long-running applied security conference with a stable reputation. ePrint marks the paper as "Minor revision," so the final version may change.
- The authors are affiliated with the University of Melbourne and the Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy; this is not a paper from a Chinese institution.

# From if to Oops: Turning a conditional into a key-recovery oracle
> IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive 2026/2542 and arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed). École Polytechnique, Inria, CNRS (France); Eindhoven University of Technology (Netherlands)
> 2026

Isogeny-based key exchange in the CSIDH family often relies on randomness to hide which operations depend on the secret key. Deterministic variants that strip out that randomness, such as Houben's deterministic CSIDH and OSIDH-LD, are cleaner to implement and reproducible. This paper argues that determinism itself opens a new path for fault attacks: every secret-dependent step happens at the same public position in every execution, so an attacker can target the same step again and again with precision. Earlier attacks went after random point sampling instead.

The attack forces to zero the mask that decides which of two lanes computes the next isogeny; skipping a single instruction is enough. Feeding the outcome into a binary search over thresholds on the secret exponents recovers the full key with one fault per execution.

Source: https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2542 (arXiv ID 2610.39910, listed on 2026-10-30)

### Contribution
- Shows concretely that determinism in CSIDH-family implementations creates a fault-injection attack surface that earlier attacks did not address.
- Presents an attack that disables a single mask controlling a conditional and turns it into a key-recovery oracle, together with a binary-search recovery procedure that keeps the number of queries low.
- Argues that fault resistance requires giving up determinism and proposes countermeasures based on randomization.

### Evaluation
- Query count: with CSIDH-512-sized keys, the key is recovered with at most 297 oracle queries for OSIDH-LD and at most 222 for deterministic CSIDH.
- Full OSIDH-LD keys were recovered from the reference implementation in a software fault simulation.
- Hardware experiment: on a ChipWhisperer-Lite, the target instruction was skipped with probability at least 0.94 at an isolated fault position.
- Reliability of the metrics: the query counts are upper bounds from the algorithm's analysis and are fairly clear-cut. Full key recovery was shown in simulation, while the hardware experiment reports only the instruction-skip success rate, so the abstract alone does not tell us whether full key recovery on a real board was demonstrated.

#### Comment
- The paper matters because it shows that the recent trend of removing randomness for the sake of constant-time behavior and determinism can backfire against fault injection. It is a case where side-channel countermeasures and fault countermeasures pull in opposite directions.
- The threat model assumes an attacker with physical access and precise fault injection, so it is most relevant to embedded devices and smart cards.

# Stop Calling Them Provers: The Case for Calling ProVerif and Tamarin Symbolic Protocol Analyzers
> IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive 2026/2683 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Symbolic Software
> 2026

Security protocol papers routinely say a design was "proved secure with ProVerif" or "verified with Tamarin." This position paper argues that calling these tools "provers" is misleading. For each tool, the author examines the theory, software, models, and security properties that sit behind a "proved secure" claim, and proposes calling them "symbolic protocol analyzers" and reporting results together with the models, assumptions, and evidence behind them. It was received by ePrint on 2026-10-29 and approved for publication on 2026-10-31.

Source: https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2683

### Contribution
- Audits the published soundness arguments for both tools and reports false statements, missing proofs, and gaps between the procedures justified on paper and those actually implemented.
- Reproduces previously reported bugs that cause Tamarin to accept false claims about submitted models.
- Lays out the difference in what each tool asks users to trust: ProVerif provides no certificate for its full analysis, and Tamarin rechecks its proof tree with its own solver, so that solver's correctness has to be trusted.
- Points out limits of the models themselves: they can leave out parser and state-machine behavior, secrecy queries can still allow leaks, and cryptographic equations need separate justification before they say anything about computational security.

### Evaluation
- There is no quantitative evaluation. The main evidence is an audit of the soundness literature and the reproduction of known bugs.
- Reliability of the evidence: the specific bug reproductions and the details of the audit mentioned in the abstract were outside what could be checked this time.

#### Comment
- The author acknowledges that both tools remain useful for finding attacks and establishing symbolic properties, including for unbounded sessions. The paper is best read as a challenge about how verification results should be reported, not as an attack on the tools.
- It can serve as a useful checklist for practitioners who cite protocol verification results or rely on formal verification in standards work.
- Limitations: it is a single-author position paper and has not been peer reviewed. Responses from the tool developers and any follow-up discussion are worth reading alongside it.
