# MetaScanner: An Empirical Study and Proactive Defense against Deceptive UI Attacks in Social VR
> Accepted at Computers & Security (authors' accepted manuscript), arXiv preprint, code released. Chungbuk National University, Kwangwoon University, ETRI
> 2026

Because anyone can build and upload worlds and avatars on social VR platforms, a malicious content creator can easily plant seemingly harmless scripts and interactive objects to deceive users. The authors present four new deceptive UI attacks on VRChat: Layered Click Redirection, where an invisible overlapping object captures clicks and redirects them to ads or videos; Raycasting Phishing, where an invisible wall blocks interaction and a fake UI is shown; In-World Input Hijack, where a transparent duplicate keypad captures input such as card numbers or passwords and stores it in a hidden room; and Avatar Quishing, where a QR code attached to an avatar covers the legitimate one. As a defense they propose MetaScanner, which statically analyzes world assets (scripts and objects) for suspicious elements before users are exposed to them.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25608
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25608
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25608
- DOI (Computers & Security): https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2026.105251
- Author code repository (MetaScanner): https://github.com/cnslab-cbnu/MetaScanner
- Archived version used in the paper (Zenodo): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24151657
- Earlier poster "Illusion Worlds: Deceptive UI Attacks in Social VR" (IEEE VR 2025 poster, arXiv): https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09199

### Contribution
- Frames the social VR ecosystem with content creators as adversaries and adapts deception techniques such as web clickjacking to the visual and spatial properties of virtual worlds, implementing four attacks.
- Shows that the input hijack collects data in a hidden in-world room with no external network communication, so network-based monitoring cannot detect it.
- Releases MetaScanner, a lightweight static analyzer that can encode platform-specific policies (for example, VRChat forbids opening URLs directly from scripts).

### Evaluation
- User study: in an IRB-approved study with 30 participants (16 with VR experience, 14 without), 83.3% did not notice the difference between the two buttons in click redirection, and 86.7% did not notice the invisible wall in raycasting phishing. No participant noticed anything unusual about the keypad in the input hijack, and all 30 failed to notice that an avatar's QR code covered the original. Prior VR experience made no statistically significant difference.
- Defense: MetaScanner detected all threats in test worlds implementing the four attacks and took about 5 seconds on average across 38 collected real-world worlds.
- Reliability: the user study is small, mostly men in their early twenties, and used a fixed order. The defense evaluation centers on detection in the authors' own attack worlds and on processing time; within what was read, no concrete false-positive rate on the 38 real worlds was reported.

#### Comment
- Computers & Security (Elsevier) is a well-established security journal. The paper extends the authors' IEEE VR 2025 poster.
- A Korean team (Chungbuk National University, Kwangwoon University, ETRI). According to the paper, MetaScanner is public on GitHub and the version used is archived on Zenodo (the repository address was read by joining a line break in the PDF footnote).

# Detecting Formation-Preserving GNSS Spoofing of UAV Swarms through Transmitter Parallax in Pseudorange and Doppler
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Jeonbuk National University
> 2026

Drone swarms detect GNSS spoofing by checking reported positions against their known formation, but formation-preserving spoofing such as GNSS-WASP evades the check by placing one transmitter per satellite a few kilometres away and shifting the whole swarm while keeping its shape. The authors observe that because the transmitters are close, each drone sees them under a slightly different angle, and this parallax bends signal arrival times across the swarm regardless of the delays the attacker applies: 20-40 cm across a 90 m swarm for a transmitter at 5 km. They propose a likelihood ratio test that reads this trace from the pseudoranges and Doppler (range rates) every drone already measures, with all clocks and attacker delays unknown and no extra antennas.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26023
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.26023
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26023

### Contribution
- Shows geometrically, and quantifies, that formation-preserving spoofing still leaves a trace below the position level.
- Designs a test that combines the pseudorange and Doppler signatures and shows that, during a formation turn, precise Doppler greatly increases detection power.
- Covers other ways to spoof a whole swarm, including an adaptive attacker who knows the detector and a single-transmitter attack, within the same framework.

### Evaluation
- Simulations with real GPS and Galileo orbits and white receiver noise, matched to the GNSS-WASP evaluation (pseudorange noise 0.3 m, false-alarm rate 2^-9 per window).
- With 16 drones, GNSS-WASP at 5 km was detected in 13-60% of 10 s windows in straight flight and in every window during a formation turn with a precise Doppler receiver. Transmitters at 2 km were always detected. Detection probabilities come from 300 Monte Carlo attack trials, with standard error at most 0.03.
- Comparison: GNSS-WASP's own countermeasure detected at most 0.05 of windows at 5 km, versus 0.14-0.61 for the proposed test in straight flight.
- Reliability: all results are from simulation, with no real flights or receivers. The authors themselves report that time-correlated noise drops straight-flight detection to 0.00-0.12.

#### Comment
- A Korean team (Jeonbuk National University). Following the attack paper's own evaluation conditions makes the comparison fair, and the appendices include a Cramér-Rao bound and other theory.
- Deployment would require relative positions (for example from UWB) and sharing of measurements across the swarm; real-world validation is the next step.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# Passing NPCR Without Diffusion: Exact Signatures, a Detection Benchmark and an Audit of Published Image Ciphers
> Accepted at the Journal of Information Security and Applications (submitted version), arXiv preprint, code and records released. abstract-only summary, full text not read
> 2026

Image cipher papers almost always report NPCR, the share of ciphertext bytes that change after a one-pixel plaintext change, as evidence of diffusion, with 99.61% as the target. The author argues that several mechanisms produce values near this target and the mean alone cannot tell them apart. The paper separates three regimes (no propagation, one round of forward and backward chaining linear over addition or XOR, and independent ciphertexts) and gives an exact count for the second. It then audits 21 published image ciphers.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25561
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25561
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25561
- Code and records (Zenodo, DOI given in the arXiv comment): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22796547
- Code and records (Zenodo record): https://zenodo.org/records/24344520
- Code and records (Zenodo record): https://zenodo.org/records/23069747

### Contribution
- According to the author, after a least-significant-bit change the mean NPCR of one-round chaining asymptotically matches the independent-ciphertext reference, but its spread is about five to twenty times larger, so two-sided rather than one-sided testing is what catches such weak structures.
- Releases a checker that applies these signatures to published tables; the author stresses that it shows what a table measures and does not certify security.

### Evaluation
- Per the abstract, in simulation the one-sided NPCR test of Wu, Noonan and Agaian flags one-round additive, mixed and S-box chaining in only 2-4% of papers, while reading the same five values in both directions flags at least 99.9%.
- Audit: the exact count matched a reimplementation of one published diffusion layer on every trial; two schemes' printed specifications cannot produce their reported values; three report plain-versus-cipher values and fall to two chosen images; ten more change their encryption material with the plaintext or per encryption, so their NPCR mostly reflects that change.
- Because the full text was not read, the datasets, comparison conditions and reliability of the metrics could not be checked.

#### Comment
- Credibility signal: the arXiv comment states acceptance at the Journal of Information Security and Applications (Elsevier) and gives a Zenodo address for code and records. The current arXiv version is the submitted manuscript and will be replaced by the accepted one.
- A useful critical check on a habitual metric in chaos-based image cipher papers.

# Phantom of the Masquerade: Graph Neural Network-Based Detection of Masquerade Attacks in Controller Area Networks
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), code released. abstract-only summary, full text not read
> 2026

CAN lacks built-in security, so masquerade attacks that mimic legitimate traffic are hard for existing intrusion detection systems to catch. The authors describe PhantoMasq, which models CAN traffic as message sequence graphs annotated with lightweight statistical features of signal behavior (the mean and standard deviation of each ID's signals within a window) and classifies each graph with a graph convolutional network.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26094
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.26094
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26094
- Author code repository: https://github.com/nehal-vcu/PhantoMasq

### Contribution
- According to the authors, unlike earlier graph-embedding approaches that separate representation learning from classification, PhantoMasq learns structural dependencies and temporal characteristics jointly.
- They use an evaluation protocol that keeps overlapping windows from spanning training and test data.

### Evaluation
- Per the abstract, on five masquerade attack types from the ROAD dataset, mean AUC-ROC ranged from 0.831 to 0.999, depending on the targeted ID's transmission rate and the size of the induced signal deviation.
- Against a state-of-the-art graph-embedding baseline on the same sliding-window settings, AUC-ROC was higher by 0.13 to 0.36 on all five attack types, with less sensitivity to window configuration.
- Window processing took 2.33-5.94 ms on a CPU (168-429 windows per second).
- Because the full text was not read, the datasets, comparison conditions and reliability of the metrics could not be checked.

- Credibility signal: the arXiv comment gives a code address. Cloning the repository showed code, unit tests and result files for rebuilding the tables and figures; the README notes that the ACTT tool used to create attack captures is not included. There is no paper PDF or TeX.
