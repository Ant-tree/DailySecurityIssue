# Towards Effective Fuzzing of EDK2 Components
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.03854 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Purdue University (USA)
> 2026

edk2 is the de facto reference implementation of UEFI bootloaders and is used by ARM, AMD, Intel, Apple, Microsoft and many other vendors. It has recently produced a string of high-severity vulnerabilities, and the boot environment offers attackers an easy ride because mitigations such as ASLR are largely absent. Earlier fuzzing work mostly targeted post-boot SMI handlers, and the few efforts that covered boot-time interfaces (which edk2 calls "protocols") relied on heavyweight simulators with limited throughput and coverage.

Metis rehosts edk2 drivers as user-space programs and fuzzes them there. The key observation is that a protocol's inputs arrive not only through its interface functions but also through the other protocols it depends on, such as packets handed up by the UDP layer. Metis therefore replaces only the direct dependencies with semantically faithful mocks and injects fuzzer input through those mocks as well. Harnesses and mocks are scaffolded by static analysis and filled in by an LLM that draws on the UEFI specification and source comments, then refined over several rounds using execution feedback. Crashes go through LLM-assisted triage to filter out false positives caused by imprecise mocks. The implementation builds on Intel's Host Based Firmware Analyzer (HBFA).

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03854
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03854
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.03854
- Vulnerability commitment ledger (Zenodo, as cited in the paper): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23749160

### Contribution
- A rehosting strategy built around direct dependencies that treats both protocol interfaces and dependency interactions as input channels, making fast fuzzing of boot-time drivers possible without a simulator.
- Automatic generation of harnesses and mocks by combining static analysis with LLM synthesis, refined through execution feedback. The refinement rounds lift mean driver line coverage from 33.4% to 63.9%.
- Harness generation that follows the UEFI specification, so Metis avoids unrealistic crashes such as passing NULL to arguments the specification forbids from being NULL.
- A public ledger of SHA3-512 hashes for each undisclosed finding, so discovery dates can be verified once the bugs are published.

### Evaluation
- Targets: 60 edk2 drivers covering 162 protocols, plus 14 historical CVEs re-injected by porting the vulnerable code back onto the patched tree. Each driver was fuzzed for 24 hours on a single AFL++ instance with ASan and UBSan enabled. The default LLM is Claude Sonnet 5.5, and six models were compared.
- Coverage: across the 59 drivers with measured coverage, mean line coverage is 73.1% and function coverage 85.5%. On the 54 drivers that both Metis and FuzzUEr, the previous state of the art, could run, Metis averages 72.5% line coverage against 42.1% (median 80.9% vs 43.1%) and wins on 47 drivers. Intel's existing hand-written HBFA harnesses could exercise only PartitionDxe among the evaluated drivers, reaching 15.2% against 62.6% for Metis.
- LLM-only baseline: with nothing but build and crash output as feedback, an LLM produced a working harness for only 25 of the 60 drivers. On those 25 it reaches 79.2% line coverage, slightly above Metis at 76.7%, but averaged over all 60 drivers with failures counted as zero the figures are 33.0% vs 71.9%. Half of the baseline's 32 reported bugs turned out to be harness artifacts that violated firmware contracts.
- Bugs: 5,116 unique AFL++ crashes reduce to 204 reproducible distinct crashes and then to 137 bugs attributed to the target drivers. The authors have reported 26 so far, 12 of them remotely triggerable, and say all reported issues have been acknowledged by the vendors.
- Known CVEs: Metis rediscovered only 4 of the 14, a false-negative rate of 71.4%. The authors analyze the misses openly: some bugs are invisible to a memory-safety oracle (predictable TCP initial sequence numbers, missing TLS host-name verification), the Ip6Dxe harness crashed at startup, and some bugs need inputs the mocks never produce.
- Reliability of the metrics: the 24-hour budget matches the baselines and the setup follows the Klees et al. fuzzing evaluation guidelines. The details of the 137 new bugs are withheld pending disclosure, so for now they can only be checked against the hash ledger. I could not find the number of repeated runs or statistical tests in the paper.

#### Comment
- Highly relevant to UEFI firmware supply-chain security. Remotely triggerable memory errors in network drivers such as Dhcp4Dxe and Dhcp6Dxe deserve attention in PXE boot environments.
- Limitations: only DXE-phase drivers are covered; PEI, SEC, Standalone MM and runtime drivers are out of scope. Logic bugs beyond memory safety are not detected.
- The manuscript still carries ACM template placeholders (conference name, a 2018 copyright line), so it looks like a pre-submission draft. Code and data are only promised for release.
- Credibility: a follow-up from the same group that built FuzzUEr (Aravind Machiry's lab at Purdue), so the comparison with FuzzUEr is made by people who know it well. Not yet peer reviewed.

# InterceptFuzz: Interception-based Stateful Fuzzing of Single-Process Targets
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.03709 (preprint, not peer reviewed). COSIC and DistriNet, KU Leuven (Belgium)
> 2026

Most stateful protocol fuzzers act as a network client and talk to the target over sockets. Platforms such as OpenThread and ns-3, however, simulate every node inside a single process and pass packets in memory, so there is no socket to attach to. Meanwhile, large fuzzing infrastructure such as OSS-Fuzz only supports stateless engines like libFuzzer and AFL++, which makes deep protocol states hard to reach.

InterceptFuzz is middleware that tackles both problems. A single hook placed where the target hands packets between nodes lets it record benign traffic to derive seeds and inject fuzzed packets in its place. Observed packet sequences are organized in a prefix tree, each seed carries a state ID pointing to a tree node, and every execution deterministically replays the recorded prefix before delivering one mutated packet. As the campaign runs, newly discovered sequences extend the tree and redundant states are pruned. The fuzzing engine itself is unmodified stateless AFL++, so the approach fits existing infrastructure.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03709
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03709
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.03709
- Author repository (code and experiment artifacts): https://github.com/KULeuven-COSIC/InterceptFuzz

### Contribution
- Stateful fuzzing of socketless single-process simulation targets with only one change to the target code.
- A design that achieves stateful fuzzing on top of a stateless engine (AFL++), opening a path to infrastructure such as OSS-Fuzz.
- An epoch loop that alternates between extending, exploiting and pruning the prefix tree to keep the state space under control.
- Seven previously unknown crash signatures in OpenThread, all reported and fixed by the maintainers. Code and artifacts are fully open source.

### Evaluation
- Targets: OpenThread's Nexus test framework and the LTE and Wi-Fi models of the ns-3 simulator. Baselines are an interception-based port of AFLNet and Concat, a simple AFL++-based fuzzer that mutates a whole message sequence as one input and splits it at a delimiter.
- Setup: 10 runs per target, 24 hours each on OpenThread and 10 hours on ns-3, with the Mann-Whitney U test and the Vargha-Delaney A12 effect size. Coverage is measured by replaying each final queue on a gcov build.
- Coverage: on OpenThread, the median number of branches covered beyond the benign trace is 4,281 for InterceptFuzz, 3,969 for Concat and 1,689 for AFLNet (p=0.001, A12=0.94 against Concat). On ns-3 Wi-Fi the figures are 867, 755 and 233 (p=0.011). On ns-3 LTE, whose reachable code is small, all three land between 525 and 614 with no significant difference, and AFLNet ends with the highest median.
- Bugs: seven distinct crash signatures in OpenThread (two assertion failures, one segmentation fault, four stack buffer overflows). Concat found the same seven, though some in fewer campaigns, and AFLNet found none. Every signature was manually confirmed to reproduce.
- Reliability of the metrics: repeated runs, statistical tests and min-max ranges are all reported in line with fuzzing evaluation guidelines. The authors also report results that do not flatter their own tool, such as the simple Concat baseline beating AFLNet in every campaign.

#### Comment
- Practically useful: it brings stateful fuzzing at OSS-Fuzz scale to wireless stacks such as Thread that are widely deployed in IoT and smart-home devices.
- Limitations: all three fuzzers mutate packets in both directions, so some crashes may require mutating packets the attacker cannot control (those sent by the victim). The OpenThread attacker model assumes the attacker already holds the network key. ns-3 is not used in production, so its crashes are not real-world vulnerabilities. Fuzzing ran without ASan and only the queue was replayed on an ASan build, so some memory errors may have been missed, as the authors acknowledge.
- Credibility: a preprint from KU Leuven's COSIC group (Bart Preneel), which has a strong reputation in cryptography and security.
- Full text source: official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.

# Malware Clustering in the Wild: Taxonomy, Challenges, and Evaluation Metrics
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.02380 (preprint, not peer reviewed, survey). University of Maryland, Baltimore County (USA)
> 2026

As malware keeps growing in volume and variety, clustering, which groups similar samples without labels, is widely used to infer family boundaries and spot new variants. Yet studies use such different methods, features and datasets that they are hard to compare. This survey organizes malware clustering research into seven families (centroid-based, hierarchical, density-based, distribution-based, mixed, fuzzy and AI-driven) and pulls together the evaluation metrics used, the public malware datasets released since 2020, and the feature representations derived from them.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02380
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.02380
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02380

### Contribution
- Tables for each method family listing representative work, features, datasets and evaluation metrics, giving a single view of the malware clustering literature.
- A mapping between public datasets used since 2021 (EMBER, SOREL-20M, BODMAS and others), feature types (bytes, strings, import tables, opcode n-grams, dynamic behavior) and clustering methods, showing which features tend to pair with which methods.
- A comparison of the families in terms of concept drift, outlier handling, dataset scale and feature representation. K-means variants scale well but need the number of clusters in advance and are sensitive to outliers; density-based methods handle noise and new variants well but can leave many samples unclustered when parameters are off; hierarchical methods are interpretable for family lineage but become expensive at scale.

### Evaluation
- This is a literature survey with no experiments of its own. It tabulates the metrics reported by individual studies (silhouette score, adjusted Rand index, Fowlkes-Mallows index, purity, accuracy and so on).
- Reliability of the metrics: the numbers are copied from the original papers and come from different datasets and settings, so they cannot be compared directly.

#### Comment
- The survey's main concerns matter in practice: many studies still rely on private or custom-collected samples, which hurts reproducibility, and few account for temporal drift or label noise. It also notes that Android malware datasets now appear more often in clustering work.
- LLM-based clustering is described as an early-stage area whose behavior under concept drift and adversarial conditions is largely untested.
- Limitations: despite the title, the discussion of evaluation metrics themselves is shallow, with no comparison or recommendation. The manuscript is a little rough; the abstract lists seven families while the conclusion summarizes six.
- Credibility: a preprint survey (Elsevier format) from a US university group.
- Full text source: official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.

# Private key sieves for improved attacks on RSA
> arXiv math.NT 2611.06427 (preprint, not peer reviewed, short version). NORCE Norwegian Research Centre (Norway)
> 2026

RSA is known to fall apart when the private key is too small or part of it is exposed. Such attacks usually rely on lattice reduction to find small roots of polynomials, but as the theoretical bounds are approached the lattice dimension grows and the attacks become hard to run in practice. This paper adapts the "hyperbolic sieve", originally developed for deterministic integer factorization, to RSA parameters. For each small prime r, a secret value such as p+q can only take roughly half of the possible residues, and combining many small primes through the Chinese Remainder Theorem shrinks the candidate set dramatically.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06427
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06427
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.06427

### Contribution
- Generalizes the hyperbolic sieve from p+q to other secret RSA parameters, including Euler's totient of N and the private key d.
- Modifies the partial key exposure polynomials of Blömer-May and Ernst-Jochemsz-May-de Weger so that sieve candidates can be combined with known bits of d. The author argues this cuts the number of required guesses by a factor almost exponential in the search space, compared with the usual practice of guessing a few bits to shrink the lattice.
- For moduli whose prime factors are close together, improves the best known deterministic runtime bound by a square-root factor, with a fully rigorous proof.

### Evaluation
- This is a theory paper with no experiments. The author states that this is a short version and that implementation details will follow in a later version.
- Results are stated as theorems. For example, with a prime public exponent e no larger than N to the 7/8 and some least significant bits of d known, the paper bounds the number of operations below the naive search space by an almost exponential factor. The lattice-based parts rely on the standard heuristic assumption of this area; only the small prime difference result is proven without assumptions.
- Reliability of the metrics: without an implementation, the practical speed-up at real key sizes such as 2048 bits cannot be assessed.

#### Comment
- Limited practical impact. Properly generated RSA keys are not affected; the paper makes known misconfigurations (small private keys, partial key exposure, primes that are too close) cheaper to exploit. The reduced amount of exposed key bits needed may interest hardware security researchers working on side-channel leakage.
- The primary arXiv category is number theory (math.NT), and the paper's mathematics subject classification is cryptography (94A60).
- Credibility: a short single-author preprint supported by the Research Council of Norway.
- Full text source: official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.
