# Hello Again: Exploiting Replay Protected Monotonic Counters to Brute-Force Windows Hello PINs
> Slightly updated arXiv version of a paper published at ACM CCS 2026 (DOI 10.1145/3830454.3846631). TU Berlin
> 2026

A hardware attack paper that defeats the TPM anti-hammering protection behind short Windows Hello PINs by rolling back firmware TPM (fTPM) state. Windows Hello accepts four-digit numeric PINs, which are only safe because the TPM counts failures and locks out. Most current x86 laptops use an fTPM running on the CPU's security coprocessor (AMD PSP, Intel CSME or Microsoft Pluton), and the fTPM stores its non-volatile state, lockout counters included, encrypted on the mainboard's SPI flash. To stop attackers from writing back an old flash image, newer platforms use flash that supports JEDEC's Replay Protected Monotonic Counters (RPMC), which operate over a physically accessible SPI bus. Using a cheap interposer board and voltage glitching, the authors present the first practical analysis of RPMC flash from three vendors and of Intel's and AMD's non-volatile storage controllers.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18126
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.18126
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18126
- ACM Digital Library (DOI): https://doi.org/10.1145/3830454.3846631
- Authors' artifact repository (interposer firmware, PCB designs, attack scripts, SPI traces): https://github.com/mattifinder/rpmc_attacks
- First author's homepage: https://www.user.tu-berlin.de/cwerling/

### Contribution
- An evaluation of RPMC flash from three vendors. Counters on Winbond, Macronix and GigaDevice wrap from the 32-bit maximum to 0, breaking monotonicity (minor impact); GigaDevice returns forged counter values with valid signatures under glitching (minor impact); and Winbond leaks its internal HMAC key through the status register when glitched (major impact). The Winbond key leak reproduces in about one second, so it can be done on demand.
- An evaluation of Intel and AMD controllers. AMD's controller does not check that an RPMC increment actually took effect, so an interposer can silently suppress increments. On two AMD desktops without RPMC flash (Zen 2 and Zen 5), simply reflashing an old image reset the fTPM lockout counters. Intel's controllers reuse the same key data across all counters and sessions (a weakness).
- End-to-end attacks on real hardware via two paths. On an HP OmniBook 3 (a Copilot+ PC), the AMD unverified-increment flaw allows about 4.14 Windows Hello PIN guesses per minute, so a four-digit PIN falls in at most 40 hours instead of 69 days under the default lockout policy. On a Framework Laptop 13, the Winbond key leak allows 34.1 BitLocker TPM+PIN guesses per minute, so a default six-digit PIN falls in at most 20 days instead of 19 years.
- Interposer firmware, PCB designs, attack scripts, the leaked HMAC keys and boot traces are all released.

### Evaluation
- Targets: nine machines released between 2015 and 2025 (Intel NUC, ASUS, Lenovo IdeaPad, ThinkPad T15, two MSI boards, Framework Laptop 13, HP OmniBook 3, Acer Aspire 14 AI) and several flash chips, with each machine's flash model and RPMC usage listed in a table.
- Metrics: PIN guesses per minute and the resulting worst-case time, and the glitch parameters (MOSFET open delay and timing). Videos show 236 PINs being tried for real; one run took 57 minutes on the AMD laptop and 6 minutes 55 seconds for BitLocker.
- Side observation: Microsoft documents a 10-minute lockout recovery time, but on the tested machines it changed to 2 hours after Hello PIN setup, which stretches naive brute force to 833 days.
- Reliability: the attacks are shown end to end on locked retail hardware and all artifacts are public, so reproducibility is high. They do require physical access, desoldering the flash chip and fitting an interposer, and the authors report bricking one machine when its flash failed during experiments.

#### Comment
- The practical risk is that on a lost or stolen laptop, a single Hello PIN unlocks device-bound keys and enterprise SSO. Microsoft only announced a policy change raising the recommended minimum PIN length to six digits, and Winbond, AMD, Intel and JEDEC have not committed to fixing the root causes. For high-risk devices, banning short numeric-only PINs and lengthening BitLocker PINs is the realistic response.
- The arXiv version updates the CCS version, for example with successful rollbacks on AMD systems without RPMC and a faster BitLocker loop (34.1 instead of 27.2 guesses per minute), so some numbers differ from the CCS paper. This summary follows the arXiv version.
- CCS is a top-tier security venue.

# VellSSLH: Sound Type-Guided Selective Speculative Load Hardening for LLVM IR
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Washington State University
> 2026

Constant-time cryptographic code can still leak secrets during speculative execution (Spectre-style). This paper inserts speculative load hardening (SLH) only where it is needed and mechanically proves the result secure. LLVM's existing SLH protects every load and costs a lot, while existing selective approaches have no proven guarantee at the LLVM IR level. With VellSSLH, the programmer annotates C inputs as Public or Secret; a type system infers which values may become secret during speculation, and the transformation inserts misspeculation-flag operations and protections only there. A checker extracted from Rocq, VellSCT, then confirms that the resulting LLVM IR satisfies speculative constant-time (SCT). To support this, the author extends Vellvm, a verified LLVM semantics, with speculative execution, attacker directives and leakage observations.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18463
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.18463
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18463

### Contribution
- A formalization of SCT at the LLVM IR level on top of Vellvm, with a security type system that handles SSA joins, symbolic values, poison, pointer provenance, observable faults, explicit declassification and trusted external services.
- Rocq proofs that programs accepted by the checker satisfy SCT, and that a reference hardening transformation preserves architectural behavior. The Rocq development adds 337,040 lines beyond the existing Vellvm sources.
- An extracted, proven checker that also rechecks optimized variants.

### Evaluation
- Targets: 24 cryptographic operations from TweetNaCl, libsodium, HACL* and Kyber (Round 3), including MACs, stream ciphers, Secretbox, SHA/SHAKE, X25519 and Kyber512/768. Measured on an Intel Core Ultra 5 335 in a bare-metal Linux setup over nine layout-randomized trials.
- Results: 4.03% lower geometric-mean runtime than LLVM SLH, with 13 of 24 comparisons favoring VellSSLH, 8 favoring LLVM SLH and 3 inconclusive. For X25519 it is 22.69% faster than LLVM SLH with only 1.86% overhead over unprotected code, and it is 19.65% faster than Ultimate SLH in geometric mean. Kyber operations, on the other hand, are 16 to 22% slower than unprotected code, and Secretbox open is 40.79% slower.
- Insertion and checking take a while: 409 s and 127 s for X25519, and 769 s and 339 s for Kyber512 decapsulation.
- Reliability: confidence intervals are reported and unfavorable comparisons are not hidden. The proofs stop at LLVM IR, so backend code generation and hardware behavior remain assumptions, and optimized variants are rechecked for SCT but not covered by the preservation proof, as the author states.

#### Comment
- The direction is clear: security by proof, performance by selectivity. The speedups are modest, but keeping a proven guarantee while matching or slightly beating LLVM SLH is a meaningful result.
- The Kyber implementation is Round 3 v3.0 rather than FIPS 203 ML-KEM, and secrets must be annotated by hand, which are hurdles for practical adoption.
- Single-author paper, released together with the same author's verified decompiler paper (DeCompCert). No code link was found in the paper.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# DeCompCert: A Verified Decompiler for CompCert-Generated AArch64
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed, primary category cs.PL). Washington State University, Syracuse University
> 2026

A decompiler that turns AArch64 assembly produced by the verified CompCert compiler back into C, with a Rocq guarantee that the recovered code means the same thing as the input assembly. Production decompilers such as IDA and Ghidra rely on sophisticated analyses and heuristics but give no formal guarantee that their output matches the original program. DeCompCert reverses CompCert's compilation through nine reverse passes to recover CompCert C. Some passes have directly verified reconstruction algorithms; the others use translation validation, where untrusted OCaml and Datalog (Ascent) analyses propose candidates and proven validators accept only those that satisfy the required semantic correspondence.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18055
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.18055
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18055

### Contribution
- A composed correctness theorem: every defined, safe behavior of an accepted C reconstruction is matched by the input assembly, and under a safety assumption the two programs are observationally equivalent. The trusted base is limited to the Rocq kernel, CompCert's semantics and the assumptions inherited from CompCert.
- A setup that starts from assembly without function signatures and can take proposals from Ghidra or IDA as candidates without changing the verified boundary. Failed searches produce no output, so the tool never emits a wrong decompilation.
- About 52,100 lines of Rocq on top of CompCert 3.17.

### Evaluation
- Targets: 16 applications, about 2.16 million lines of C, including zlib, Lua, libuv, libpng, BearSSL, OpenSSL (libssl and libcrypto), wolfSSL, Mbed TLS, nginx, LibreSSL, Coreutils, curl, BusyBox and SQLite.
- With exact function and call signatures, all 3,173 eligible contexts are reconstructed and recompiled under both unoptimized and optimized pipelines. Starting without signatures, its own inference validates 59.2%, adding Ghidra proposals 73.5%, adding IDA proposals 76.1%, and the combined portfolio 82.4% (2,606 of 3,164). The first inferred signature matches the original 40.9% of the time, against 28.6% for Ghidra.
- Execution checks: C recovered from wolfSSL and OpenSSL libcrypto at -O2 and recompiled with CompCert and Clang passes all 836 OpenSSL and 36 wolfSSL executions. On new versions acquired after the setup was frozen (Lua 5.4.9, zlib 1.3.2), all 49 contexts are recovered at -O0, but only 7 under optimization.
- Limitations: the recovered C has 4.11 times as many tokens as the original and keeps labels and gotos instead of loops and switches. Its median token distance to the source is 0.845, less source-like than Ghidra (0.691).

#### Comment
- The scope is limited to binaries produced by CompCert, so it does not apply as-is to ordinary GCC or Clang binaries, let alone obfuscated ones. It is useful for auditing CompCert artifacts whose source is gone, such as certified software, or wherever the correctness of decompiler output must be guaranteed.
- The approach aims at never producing a wrong result rather than at readability, and using its validators to filter the output of production decompilers is especially practical.
- No code link was found in the paper.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# CANTraj: Recovering Vehicle Trajectories from CAN Signals in Naturalistic Driving
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), data and code released. abstract-only summary, full text not read
> 2026

According to the authors, this paper tests at scale whether dropping GPS from driving data, as open driving releases and usage-based insurance programs often do, actually protects location privacy, by recovering vehicle routes from CAN signals. They argue that existing attacks that match speed, steering and driving events to road networks rely on inconsistent assumptions and metrics and were only tested on small datasets, and they release CANTraj, a naturalistic driving dataset that pairs decoded CAN events with time-aligned GNSS trajectories, together with the CANTraj-Bench benchmark.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18442
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.18442
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18442
- Authors' code repository: https://github.com/WangYuHang-cmd/GPS--CAN
- Dataset (Hugging Face): https://huggingface.co/datasets/HenryYHW/CANTraj

### Contribution
- The authors say they release a dataset of 3,940 drives from 454 drivers and a trajectory recovery benchmark covering 1,380 hours and 85,919 km.
- They re-implement six existing attacks and compare them under explicit information-access protocols, and claim that combining steering signals with road-network constraints gives the largest gain.
- Because methods fail on different windows, they also evaluate an LLM-based selector that picks among candidate routes.

### Evaluation
- Per the abstract, on 776 evaluation drives from 94 unseen drivers, with a known start and heading, adding steering to speed raises the online map filter's five-minute route recovery from 56% to 87%, with a median average displacement error of 43 m versus 921 m for map-free dead reckoning. Offline beam search reportedly recovers 88% of test routes with the heading and 81% without it.
- The best LLM selector configuration reportedly recovers 79% of routes on test and 83% on few-shot over 659 five-minute windows, beating the best baseline on test and matching it on few-shot.
- Because the full text was not read, the datasets, comparison conditions and the reliability of the metrics could not be verified.

#### Comment
- Trust signal: the abstract and arXiv record give public code and data locations (GitHub, Hugging Face). There is no peer-reviewed venue. The supplementary material in the repository carries a "[Experiment, Analysis, and Benchmark]" submission-track label in its title, but authors and affiliations are anonymized there.
- Why the full text was not obtained: title and author searches (extended) returned only arXiv and the Hugging Face paper page, with no venue page or author PDF; the authors' repository was cloned and contains only supplementary material, not the paper. The arXiv download cap for this run (10 papers) had been reached.
- Affiliation: could not be confirmed from searches or the repository (affiliation unconfirmed).
- Location privacy in GPS-free vehicle data bears directly on insurer and vehicle-data release policies. The entry will be upgraded to a full summary once the full text is obtained.
