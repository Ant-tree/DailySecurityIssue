# The Promise of Strategic Agentic Scambaiting
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.08170 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Fordham University, University of Colorado Denver, University of North Carolina Wilmington (USA)
> 2026

Scammers have moved from generating messages automatically to running whole conversations with agentic systems. On the defensive side, "agentic scambaiting" has emerged: autonomous agents pose as potential victims and keep scammers talking long enough to extract threat intelligence such as mule bank accounts and cryptocurrency wallet addresses. The result is a contest in which scamming agents and baiting agents deceive each other while trying to expose the other side's resources. This paper builds a formal model of these agent-to-agent fraud interactions and asks under what conditions each side gains the upper hand.

Each engagement is modeled as a finite-horizon dynamic Bayesian game. Scam conversations move through trust-building, escalation, a payment request and finally disclosure, and the scammer updates its belief about whether the counterpart is a real victim or a baiting agent from noisy conversational signals (green, yellow, red), deciding at each stage whether to escalate or walk away. The useful intelligence only appears at the last stage, so a baiting agent has to survive the earlier screening. A campaign-level layer adds the gradual depletion of the scammer's mule accounts and the defender's email identities over repeated engagements.

- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08170
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08170
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.08170

### Contribution
- Treats agentic scambaiting as an adaptive security interaction rather than an engineering system, with a formal model that captures both the scammer's belief updates and the defender's mimicry quality.
- Extends the single-engagement model to repeated interactions in which both sides' resources erode, enabling analysis of long-run sustainability.
- Uses backward induction and Monte Carlo simulation to identify the deployment scale at which scamming stops paying, the conditions under which scammer screening collapses intelligence yield, and the design properties that keep defensive value as both sides adapt.

### Evaluation
- The experiments are simulations of the model, not real conversations. The stage structure, signal design and payoff ordering follow patterns observed in an earlier large-scale LLM scambaiting deployment, and two cost parameters were calibrated to that deployment's figures (about 31.7% disclosure among matured engagements, a mean of 10.3 turns to disclosure, a 48.7% contact acceptance rate). The remaining transition parameters are modeling assumptions.
- In the baseline setting, with a baiting-agent fraction of 29.2%, the intelligence disclosure rate against bots was 22.9%, the payment rate from genuine victims 53.4% and the abandonment rate 14.8%. Each parameter setting was run as 2,000 independent simulations.
- Main findings: once the share of baiting agents passes a threshold, the scammer's expected profit turns negative. When both sides improve at the same pace, gains in the defender's mimicry outpace equivalent gains in the scammer's detection, so under the baseline parameters the arms race favors the defender. The deployment scale that cuts scammer revenue the most differs from the one that yields the most intelligence, so the best strategy depends on the goal.
- Reliability of the metrics: the authors themselves say the results should be read as trends and tipping points across parameter regimes, not as predictions of real deployment performance. Most numbers rest on calibration and assumptions, so the qualitative conclusions deserve more weight than the exact values.

#### Comment
- A rare attempt to look at LLM-based deception defenses (honeypots, scambaiting) in economic terms, asking how long they stay useful once the attacking side is automated too. Read together with today's paper on honeypots for LLM attack agents, it helps frame the "deception versus deception" setting.
- Limitations: there is no validation with real scammers or real baiting agents, and some settings assume the scammer knows the true share of baiting agents.
- Credibility: a preprint from US universities.

# Clear Human Web: Where a Person Vouches for Every Client
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.06286 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Independent researcher
> 2026

The web cannot tell whether a request comes from a person or a program. Sites have relied on network addresses and CAPTCHAs instead, but automated traffic already exceeds human traffic and CAPTCHAs are solved by paid services and by agents. This paper proposes the Clear Human Web, the part of the public web that opens only to clients a person vouches for.

The construction builds on the EU Digital Identity Wallet. A person's wallet presents their person identification data (PID) to a single issuer, which returns a bounded number of blind-signed tokens: proofs of personhood that carry no attributes. The browser spends one token at each site that runs the gate, and the site learns only that a valid token from a trusted issuer was spent. Technically it reuses Privacy Pass's HTTP challenge and blind RSA tokens, so no field is added to the PID, no new protocol is introduced, and only the issuer has to register as a relying party under the regulation. A site's publisher presents the same proof as its readers.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06286
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06286
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.06286

### Contribution
- Defines the problem through five properties and seven adversaries and tabulates which properties the construction meets against each adversary.
- Grounds the design in EU regulations (2024/1183, 2025/848) and the wallet architecture documents, and lays out the concrete steps between what can be deployed now and the theoretical optimum, in which possession of a PID is shown with a zero-knowledge proof, each site sees one pseudonym, and no issuer is needed.
- Shows that the identities one person can hold on one site in an epoch can be bounded to k, provided the wallet limits each person to one pseudonym at the issuer.
- Compares the design with iris-based personhood (World), account or phone anchors (PACT, MoLE), EU age verification and others by what is attested and what a site learns.

### Evaluation
- This is a design and analysis paper with no implementation or measurements. The evaluation argues, property by property, whether the construction holds against seven adversaries, including the issuer and a network observer.
- Quantitative context comes from cited external sources, such as automated traffic exceeding 53% of web traffic in 2025, CAPTCHA-solving studies and the concentration of age-verification providers.
- Reliability of the metrics: the property-versus-adversary analysis is clearly laid out, but performance, usability and real wallet integration are untested. The author notes that the key condition for the identity bound, one pseudonym per person, is not implemented in any wallet release yet.

#### Comment
- With AI crawlers and agent traffic growing, this proposal tries to prove only that "a person stands behind this client" without revealing anything else, and it is notable for building on the EU identity wallet as a real root of trust.
- Limitations: people without a PID (residents of third countries, people without a device that runs the wallet) are excluded. A token only says that a person vouches for the client, and that person may hand the client to a program. Without Tor, network addresses link sessions, and the issuer sees every refill and its timing. The author also warns that a market for renting identities could weaken the bound.
- Credibility: a single-author preprint by an independent researcher without affiliation. The regulatory and standards citations are specific, but the work has not been peer reviewed.
