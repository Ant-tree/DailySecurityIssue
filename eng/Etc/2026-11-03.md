# Who Swept My ETH? Uncovering EIP-7702-Based Ether Redirection Attacks on Ethereum
> arXiv cs.CR 2610.41153 (preprint, not peer reviewed), School of Software Engineering, Sun Yat-sen University (China), and Ant Group
> 2026

EIP-7702 introduced a transaction type that lets an externally owned account (EOA) delegate execution to arbitrary contract code. It brings conveniences such as batching and gas sponsorship, but it also enables a new attack: once a victim's account is delegated to a malicious "sweeper" contract, any ETH sent to that account is automatically forwarded to the attacker. The paper defines this as the EIP-7702-Based Ether Redirection Attack (EERA) and presents SweeperHunter, which detects it through transaction analysis.

The authors describe three ways EERA differs from earlier attacks: passive triggering, where an incoming transfer alone runs the attack without any action by the victim; a broader victim model; and persistent monetization, which keeps collecting funds until the delegation is revoked. Analyzing about ten months of Ethereum mainnet transactions after EIP-7702 went live (May 2025 to February 2026), they identified 4,668,209 malicious delegations, 20,443 attack transactions and 508 ETH sweeper contracts, forming the first EERA dataset.

### Contribution
- The first systematic definition and workflow analysis of ETH redirection through EIP-7702 delegation.
- SweeperHunter, a rule-based detector that jointly constrains delegation state, value transfer and execution-trace consistency, together with a large dataset.
- Shows that about 70% of observed delegations point to ETH sweeper contracts and that more than 600,000 accounts failed to revoke malicious delegations promptly and remain exposed, and proposes matching mitigations.

### Evaluation
- Data: by late February 2026, more than 1.85 million EOAs had executed more than 6.68 million EIP-7702 delegations; malicious delegations and attack transactions were extracted from these.
- Detection checks: all 508 detected contracts were decompiled with Dedaub and reviewed independently by three blockchain security experts, and 508 sampled transactions were checked against Etherscan traces. All were confirmed as genuine EERA, and the authors report observing no false positives.
- Reliability of the metrics: the data is public blockchain data, so anyone can re-verify it. Rule-based detection tends to score high precision, however, and recall (variants that slip past the rules) was not measured; the authors discuss evasion in a separate section.

#### Comment
- Shows, with real losses, how account abstraction and delegation are changing the wallet security model. Useful when designing how mobile wallet apps should display and warn about delegation signature requests.
- Limitations: limited to Ethereum mainnet and ETH; token sweepers and other EVM chains are not covered.
- Venue: preprint.
- Verification of a Chinese-institution paper: this is joint work by Sun Yat-sen University's School of Software Engineering and Ant Group; the affiliations are stated in the paper, and the authors acknowledge National Natural Science Foundation of China funding. The detector code is in a public repository, and the results rest on public blockchain data, so they can be reproduced. The evaluation reports counts and full manual verification rather than ratio metrics, leaving little room for inflation, so the paper is included. It has no peer-reviewed venue yet.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.

# Beyond Smart Homes: Characterizing IoT Vulnerabilities Across Sectors, Vendors, and Products
> arXiv cs.CR 2610.42032 (preprint, not peer reviewed), University of Twente, Netherlands
> 2026

The Internet of Things has spread well beyond smart homes into industrial systems, healthcare and smart cities, yet vulnerability guidance still leans on statistics that lump all of IoT together. If the dominant weaknesses differ substantially by sector or product, uniform guidance points remediation effort in the wrong direction. Starting from that concern, this paper analyzes more than 15,000 IoT-specific CVEs at the level of sector, vendor and product type.

NVD's CPE metadata lists the same vendor under many names and carries no product category or sector, so it cannot be used as is. The authors build a pipeline of lexical matching, fuzzy clustering and LLM-assisted classification to normalize vendor and product names and assign product-type and sector labels. Weakness profiles turn out to differ clearly by sector: healthcare is dominated by authentication and credential weaknesses, automotive by memory issues. More interesting still, different products from the same vendor resemble each other more than the same product type from different vendors.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42032
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.42032
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.42032

### Contribution
- A pipeline that cleans noisy CPE metadata and assigns product-type and sector labels, enabling the first fine-grained analysis of IoT vulnerabilities by sector and product.
- Shows that the dominant weakness classes (CWEs) differ by sector.
- Concludes that vendor-level factors such as shared code and development practices drive weakness inheritance, so a vendor's disclosure history is informative about its other products.

### Evaluation
- Method: cosine similarity of CWE profiles compared under three conditions: same vendor with different product types, same product type from different vendors, and neither (baseline). Bias controls include restricting to single-vendor CVEs.
- Results: both conditions are significantly above the baseline (same product type p < 10^-31, Cliff's δ = +0.40; same vendor p = 5×10^-8, δ = +0.47), with the larger effect for the same vendor.
- Reliability of the metrics: nonparametric tests with effect sizes are appropriate. The authors note that absolute similarities are modest, and closely related CWEs such as CWE-787 and CWE-119 are treated as unrelated. From the parts read, no human-validated accuracy figure for the LLM-assigned product-type and sector labels was found.

#### Comment
- A practical takeaway for patch prioritization: "who made it" may be as informative as, or more informative than, "what kind of device it is." Useful for supply-chain security assessments.
- Limitations: only CVEs published in NVD are covered, so vendors with better disclosure practices may be overrepresented.
- Venue: preprint.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.
- The authors are at the University of Twente, Netherlands; this is not a paper from a Chinese institution.
