# Side-Channel Analysis of the RP2350 Challenge 2
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Oregon State University
> 2026

The Raspberry Pi Foundation ran a second hacking challenge against a side-channel-hardened AES-256 implementation on its RP2350 microcontroller. It went unsolved for over a year and its deadline was extended three times. The authors assessed the implementation independently, outside the challenge's reward terms, and report leakage detection, plaintext dependence and full key extraction. Their starting point was the challenge's hardening report, which appears to have checked for leakage only with a ChipWhisperer Husky of unspecified bandwidth and did not discuss higher-order moment attacks.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25479
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25479
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25479
- Official challenge repository (target implementation): https://github.com/raspberrypi/rp2350_hacking_challenge_2
- Official challenge page: https://www.raspberrypi.com/rp2350-hacking-challenge-2/

### Contribution
- Shows that the AES implementation, protected with table-based masking and shuffling among other measures, fails basic leakage detection (Welch's t-test up to fourth order and deep-learning leakage assessment).
- Recovers every key byte with a SCATTER-based higher-order mixture attack that fixes the S-box range as a reference and slides along the mask permutation (makeperm16) range. This attack does not depend on input patterns or on which round is targeted.
- Points out an implementation flaw: the first key addition is less protected, so with helper inputs the first key byte falls to a standard correlation power analysis.

### Evaluation
- Measurements used a Tektronix MSO66B oscilloscope at about 59 traces per second, giving a practical ceiling of 5 to 10 million traces in one or two days. Plaintext dependence used two sets of 1.5 million traces (3 million in total).
- Deep-learning leakage assessment detected leakage after only 5 epochs at 90.7% accuracy, revealing the first key addition, makeperm16 and every AES round.
- The SCATTER attack recovered all key bytes at rank 1 with a median of about 1.1 million traces, and the attack parameters (Hamming weight model, window sizes, histogram bins) are published.
- Reliability: the equipment and parameters are specific and the target is the official challenge code, so the conditions are clear. The authors themselves call it a limited assessment that does not replace root-cause analysis, and they do not detail a second-round attack for the other half of the AES-256 key.

#### Comment
- A short seven-page report, but it shows that software countermeasures on a commodity microcontroller do not hold up against lab-grade equipment. The authors grant that the implementation resists standard univariate attacks well, yet expect other groups could break it too.
- It also raises the point that bug bounty and challenge reward terms can discourage independent publication. The authors say they used SCARR and Claude for parts of the work.

# Correctable Enrollment and One-Transaction Seed Proofs for Ethereum's Post-Quantum Transition
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). University of Pittsburgh, SeQure Ltd
> 2026

A quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could extract the private key of every Ethereum account whose public key is exposed, and accounts exposed by their own transactions hold 54.6% of execution-layer ETH. Since the first such machine may be kept secret, waiting for visible thefts is too late, and once keys can be extracted, legacy signatures no longer distinguish owners from attackers. The authors design a migration in which owners cheaply enroll post-quantum keys with their existing signatures before ECDSA is retired on a scheduled date, while a zero-knowledge proof of the wallet's BIP-39 seed can override any enrollment until a common deadline afterward.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25663
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25663
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25663
- Author code repository: https://github.com/DaseinDa/ethereum-pq-transition

### Contribution
- Migration rules that let Ethereum retire ECDSA on a fixed date without knowing when a quantum attacker appears. Accounts nobody enrolled stay frozen but remain recoverable by seed proof with no deadline.
- Each proof binds the new key, a nonce and a fee cap, so neither a fee-paying sponsor nor a reordering block builder can redirect or replay it.
- Validators move to hash-based keys first at a finalized checkpoint, under a stated bound on the attacker's voting power. The account guarantees are proved under explicit assumptions, the rules are model-checked, and the design is implemented in go-ethereum and a Lean consensus client.

### Evaluation
- A three-layer recursive hash-based STARK yields a seed proof of about 190 KB that fits in one transaction, verifies in 38 ms and takes a median of 111 seconds to generate on a laptop. Four proofs can be aggregated into one.
- In a ten-node simulation with mainnet-derived accounts and fee demand, the 4.45 million accounts holding at least 0.1 ETH would migrate in 29.4 days if all enroll before ECDSA is retired, and in 235 days if all recover afterward in batches of four. Block space, not proving time, sets the pace.
- In a mass enrollment, an enrollment waits a median of 19.1 minutes in the public mempool, longer than published estimates for a fast quantum computer to extract a key, so owners with a seed and an unexposed key should migrate by seed proof.
- Reliability: costs and timings come from the implementation and simulation; real-world consensus and social coordination are out of scope. Quantum attack timings are taken from the literature.

#### Comment
- Turns Vitalik Buterin's emergency hard fork idea (proving ownership with the seed) into a concrete protocol and implementation. Owners without a seed still depend on an enrollment that became final before a quantum attacker appeared.
- The acknowledgments state that several AI tools helped polish the manuscript, draw figures and write and run the experiment code, and that all results come from executed code.

# Can Verifiable PIR Match the Online Efficiency of SimplePIR?
> Accepted at IEEE S&P 2027 (per arXiv comment), arXiv preprint. Institute of Information Engineering CAS, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Xidian University
> 2026

Verifiable private information retrieval (vPIR) hides which record a client asks for while letting the client detect a malicious server that returns tampered data. VeriSimplePIR brought online efficiency close to SimplePIR through proof preprocessing, but its SIS-based commitments support only relaxed range proofs, forcing weaker parameters and extra online cost. The authors propose a vPIR protocol that uses a new error-correcting-code-based commitment as the database digest, supporting exact range proofs and removing the SIS assumption.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22911
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.22911
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.22911

### Contribution
- An error-correcting-code-based commitment scheme with efficient proofs of linear relations and exact range proofs.
- This allows the same parameters as SimplePIR, making it, by the authors' claim, the first vPIR whose online communication and server computation exactly match SimplePIR, with nearly identical client computation.
- Two range-proof variants (one lighter on communication, one faster online) let users trade offline cost against online performance.

### Evaluation
- Implemented in C++ on top of the VeriSimplePIR codebase and compared with SimplePIR and VeriSimplePIR on a single thread of an AMD EPYC 9474F, for databases from 512 MiB to 16 GiB with 1-byte entries and 1 GiB databases with entries from 1 bit to 32 bytes.
- Compared with VeriSimplePIR, online communication and client computation drop by about 20% and online server computation by over 35%. For an 8 GB database, VeriSimplePIR has to use a smaller plaintext modulus, which explains the gap.
- With 2-byte and 4-byte entries all three schemes pack the same way and perform similarly. The second range-proof variant costs somewhat more offline communication and computation.
- Reliability: the baselines are the standard ones in this area and share the same codebase, so the comparison is fair, and the gains are explained by the parameter difference. Security levels were estimated with the public Lattice Estimator.

#### Comment
- This is a Chinese-institution paper, so it was checked more carefully. IEEE S&P is a top-tier security venue; acceptance was confirmed from the arXiv comment and the acknowledgment of anonymous reviewers in the text, though not from an official accepted-papers list. The affiliations, including a State Key Laboratory at the CAS Institute of Information Engineering, are real institutions, and the corresponding author's homepage was found by search. No sign of inflated numbers or unsuitable baselines. However, no public address for the implementation was found, which limits reproducibility.
