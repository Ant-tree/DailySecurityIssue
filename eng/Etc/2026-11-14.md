# What You See Is (Not) What You Got: The Human Factor in PDF Signature Verification
> Accepted manuscript in Computers & Security (peer reviewed, DOI 10.1016/j.cose.2026.105191, accepted 2026-09-26). arXiv version. Graz University of Technology, A-SIT (Austria)
> 2026

Under the EU's eIDAS regulation, a digitally signed PDF can carry the same legal weight as a handwritten signature. Users cannot check the cryptography themselves, so they rely on the PDF viewer's "signature valid" indicator. The visible signature block in the document (signer name, date and so on), however, is just content the document author can put there, separate from the signing certificate. The paper calls attacks on this gap Genuine PDF Forgeries: a document carries a cryptographically valid signature but shows a signature block with someone else's name, so the viewer says "valid" and the reader trusts the name on the page. The authors examined 23 PDF viewers and validation tools and measured how often people are fooled in online studies with 584 participants and in field trials at two real organizations.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15472
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.15472
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.15472
- Computers & Security DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2026.105191

### Contribution
- Describes an attack class that targets how validation results are shown to people rather than any cryptographic flaw.
- Of the 23 tools, 15 support signature validation and 13 show a validity result automatically on opening; 9 of those 13, including every desktop viewer tested, only reveal the actual signer after an extra click.
- Found that Microsoft Edge and Nitro PDF showed an attacker-controlled name field as the signer in early tests; both were reported and fixed as CVE-2024-21383 and CVE-2025-67825.
- Prototypes a viewer-side safeguard that overlays the document's signature block with signer information taken from the validated certificate.

### Evaluation
- Field study: six trials at an Austrian hospital and an Austrian government body, with prior consent arrangements and ethics review. In five of the six, a request backed by the forged signature block was accepted.
- Online studies: two studies with 584 EU-based participants recruited through Prolific. The most effective variant fooled 71.9% on first impression, and 57.3% were still fooled after being prompted to take a second look.
- Reliability: the field study has only six trials but takes place in real workplaces; the online studies have ample samples, though paid participants may behave differently from people doing routine office work, which the authors discuss.

#### Comment
- The paper is accepted at a journal and extends the authors' earlier conference version. Computers & Security is an established peer-reviewed journal in this field.
- The conclusion is simple but practical: a valid/invalid indicator is not enough, and viewers need to show the verified signer's name prominently. Organizations that rely on e-signed contracts can apply this right away in staff training and viewer choice.

# FOE-Dreamer: Deployment-Efficient Learning of Cyber Defense Policies in Operational Networks
> To appear at ACSAC 2026 (peer reviewed). arXiv version. The University of Texas at El Paso
> 2026

Most reinforcement-learning work on cyber defense trains in a simulator and then transfers the policy, and policies degrade when they meet authentication delays, partially failing actions, service dependencies and user traffic the simulator never modelled. Training directly in the network being defended removes that gap but makes every interaction slow and costly, so sample efficiency becomes the limiting factor. FOE-Dreamer applies model-based reinforcement learning in the Dreamer family, which plans inside a learned world model, directly in a cloud-hosted emulated network running real software and real CVEs. Its latent state is split into a part driven by defender actions, a part driven by benign user activity, and an opponent embedding inferred only from signals the defender can observe, which reduces policy-gradient variance during imagined planning. Defensive actions include deploying deception such as honeypots and fake SUID binaries.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14880
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14880
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14880
- ACSAC 2026 accepted papers: https://www.acsac.org/2026/program/papers/
- Anonymous code repository (given in the paper): https://anonymous.4open.science/r/FOE-DREAMER-5117

### Contribution
- Presents a model-based RL framework that trains defense policies directly in an emulated operational network, with no simulator stage.
- Designs a world model that separates action-driven, exogenous and opponent components, and checks that the learned latents separate as intended.
- Defines a threat model and observation space that include attacker behavior such as probing for honeypots.

### Evaluation
- Environment: an 8-host OpenStack testbed, user activity emulated with the GHOSTS framework, observations and actions every 5 seconds, and two scripted attackers with different tendencies (one that withdraws when it suspects deception, one drawn to decoys).
- Results: within a 3-day budget on a single GPU, FOE-Dreamer achieved about 2x lower episode loss than Rainbow and IQN baselines under matched compute against both attackers. Ablations show that both the factoring and the opponent model contribute.
- Reliability: the authors make clear that "operational network" means an emulated environment, not production with real attackers and users. Only two scripted attackers are used, so generalization to adaptive attackers is untested, and the baselines are limited to two model-free methods.

#### Comment
- ACSAC 2026 acceptance was confirmed by the note in the paper and by a search result for the official ACSAC accepted papers list.
- Learning when and where to place deception as a defensive action is relevant to work on automating moving target defense and deception.

# NonSense: On the (In)security of FPGA Anti-tamper Sensors
> Accepted to the FPT 2026 special session track (as stated in the paper). arXiv version. Worcester Polytechnic Institute
> 2026

FPGAs are common in aerospace, defense and other settings where a physical attack is costly, and to counter active attacks such as voltage or clock glitches they rely on on-chip sensors (hard-IP blocks such as ADCs and PLLs, soft-IP circuits such as ring oscillators and time-to-digital converters) that trigger responses like key zeroization. This paper questions the assumption that these sensors remain trustworthy observers. It reviews recent attacks (Chypnosis, which halts the clock with fast undervolting; Chypothermia, which disrupts clocks and sensors by cooling; and GlitchSnipe, an earlier result from the same lab showing that glitch sensitivity varies with location and frequency), adds new measurements of how aging and temperature shift sensor baselines, and proposes ways to harden both detection and response.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17065
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17065
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17065
- FPT 2026 special sessions: https://fpt2026.uark.edu/special-sessions/

### Contribution
- Separates failures of the sensing stage from failures of the protective response; an asserted alarm does not mean protection has completed.
- Provides new measurements of soft-IP sensors under accelerated aging and of temperature dependence across the die.
- Recommends margins for temperature and aging, bounded recalibration only under trusted conditions, independent health checks that do not rely solely on a sensor's own alarm, and evaluating sensor placement together with the logic it protects.

### Evaluation
- Accelerated aging: a Spartan-7 (XC7S50) stressed for 535 hours at 85°C with the core supply raised 20% saw its ring-oscillator frequency drop from 202.3 MHz to 197 MHz (about 2.6%), and the tap count of its time-to-digital converter fell by 44%. More than three quarters of the change happened within the first 100 hours.
- Temperature: 79 time-to-digital converters on an Artix-7 (XC7A35T) measured from 0°C to 80°C showed inverse temperature dependence at some locations, getting faster as temperature rose, so a single temperature correction cannot be applied to every sensor. The paper also reports that during cooling the clock stopped without the lock signal flagging it, and the XADC malfunctioned.
- Reliability: as a short special-session paper, the new measurements cover only one or two boards, and much of the attack material summarizes earlier papers.

#### Comment
- This is more of a position and survey paper than a new attack, but the point that a key-erasure circuit depending on the clock is useless against attacks that stop the clock applies directly to hardware security modules and anti-tamper design.
- Asynchronous responses still depend on delay, voltage and temperature, so protection should be judged by the worst-case latency of the full path from sensing to clearing. That conclusion is useful in practice.
