# Stinging the Swarm: Extracting Attacker Intelligence from a Twin-Controller OT Honeypot
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). TRACTIAN (industrial equipment monitoring company)
> 2026

Language-model agents can now drive industrial controllers directly over fieldbus protocols such as Modbus, and they can do it in coordinated swarms. This paper presents Talos, an inline gate placed in front of the controller. A small on-device model judges each request within milliseconds and denies the writes that would damage equipment. Agents it flags as hostile are not disconnected; they are silently rerouted to a mirror twin built from the same device family and seed. The twin accepts every write, so the attacker believes it is succeeding, keeps going, and in the process gives away its toolchain and its list of target registers.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13406
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13406
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13406

### Contribution
- An OT defense that chains detection, quarantine, mirroring and extraction, so that the attacker is observed rather than merely blocked.
- A two-axis view of attacker agents, persistence (how hard they keep attacking after resistance) and disclosure (how much they reveal on their own), with a capture channel for three of the four resulting regions.
- Evidence that while the identities agents give are made up, involuntary tells such as self-reported toolchain fingerprints and target-register lists accumulate across a swarm into a high-confidence attacker profile.
- The gate, mirror and harness are said to be released under Apache 2.0 with a synthetic device family (no repository URL is given in the paper).

### Evaluation
- Setup: 2,400 attacker agents all driven by one refusal-removed open-weights model (an abliterated Llama-3.3-70B), split into four concealment tiers of 600 each (no concealment, a non-disclosure rule, a honeypot warning, a cover story). The target is a single synthetic water-pumping line modeled on real drives (ABB ACS880, Yaskawa GA500).
- Results: zero writes reached the real plant across all 2,400 agents. The gate blocked 99.4 to 100% of damage-path writes per tier and every write to an unmapped register. The 1,427 writes it did allow (1,421 command-word writes and 6 stop commands) were all rejected by the device's range check. The model made 32 to 40% of decisions directly, with no inference timeouts.
- Intelligence: agents under a non-disclosure rule escalated to the twin 55% of the time and were read there in 38% of cases. Cover-story and unconcealed agents volunteered their details to a diagnostic form before quarantine in 96% and 100% of cases. Agents warned about honeypots disclosed nothing and escalated only 12% of the time, the hardest case.
- Reliability: with one attacker model there is no basis for cross-model claims, and with one synthetic device family and seed the scenario where an agent detects the decoy was not tested. The authors list both as limitations. The results are best read as a demonstration of behavior under this specific model and scenario.

#### Comment
- All experiments used synthetic devices with no real plant or customer data, and the authors say no attacker tooling is released.
- A company preprint describing its own technology. The title and subtitle read like marketing copy, but the limitations section is fairly candid.

# Domain-Invariant and Language-Model-Assisted Acoustic Side-Channel Attacks on Keyboards
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed; master's thesis, IIIT Delhi, India)
> 2026

Acoustic side-channel attacks recover typing from keystroke sounds and work well in the lab, where training and testing use the same keyboard. Whether they survive unseen keyboards, other users, noise and compressed VoIP calls has been unclear. This thesis argues that models generalize poorly because they learn which keyboard is being used instead of which key is pressed. It proposes Decker, which combines keyboard signature normalization, domain-adversarial training, supervised cross-keyboard contrastive alignment and acoustic style randomization. On top of that, a language model rescues sentences and passwords by running constrained beam search over the acoustic model's top candidates. The thesis also introduces Hear, a dataset of 53 participants typing on 37 laptop keyboards, recorded through an external microphone, the built-in microphone and a VoIP channel.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13922
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13922
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13922
- Author dataset repository (Hear): https://github.com/vikrant-vikram/Hear-Dataset

### Contribution
- A quantitative demonstration, across several baselines, that accuracy drops by about 40 points when the keyboard changes.
- Domain-invariant representation learning that shrinks the gap to under 18 points, with ablations showing what each component contributes.
- A clear account of when language-model decoding helps: it greatly improves recovery of natural sentences and human-chosen passwords, and does almost nothing for random high-entropy strings.
- A public dataset covering multiple devices, users and genders, noise, non-line-of-sight recording, continuous text and VoIP.

### Evaluation
- The generalization gap: wav2vec2, ECAPA-TDNN, CoAtNet-S and ResNet-34 all lost 37 to 41 points on unseen keyboards (for example ECAPA fell from 95.3% to 58.1%). Eight audio-plus-spectrogram fusion variants reached only 62.7 to 66.2% on unseen keyboards.
- Decker: 98.9% on seen keyboards and 81.3% on unseen ones (a 17.6-point gap). Removing keyboard signature normalization dropped it to 63.7%, the largest single effect.
- Sentence recovery: exact sentence match rose from 42.6% for Decker alone to 58.7% with GPT-2 Small, 62.1% with FLAN-T5, 66.5% with LLaMA-2 7B and 72.8% with GPT-4. With ±30 ms onset jitter, FLAN-T5 still reached 52.0%. Inference took 2.3 ms per key on an Apple M1 laptop.
- Reliability: every experiment uses the author's own Hear dataset, with no cross-check on an external dataset. If the test sentences are mostly natural text that language models predict easily, the sentence numbers will look better than they would on arbitrary input, and the author notes the method does little for random strings. As a thesis, it has not been peer reviewed.

#### Comment
- This is a master's thesis; according to the text, the Decker and VoIP results draw on separate papers written with collaborators.
- The attack targets keyboards, but the dataset includes phone-microphone and VoIP paths, which reinforces the risk of laptop typing leaking during video calls. The thesis also discusses hardware-, system- and model-level defenses.

# A Case Study of a Phishing Cluster Targeting German Universities
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Institute for Internet Security, Westphalian University of Applied Sciences, and aDvens (Germany)
> 2026

The paper looks at attacks on universities in German-speaking countries in two parts. First, it compiles 25 confirmed attacks with measurable impact between February 2022 and February 2023, which amounts to about 5% of all German universities in that year. Second, it traces a helpdesk-themed phishing cluster active for more than three years by pivoting through public URLScan records on visual similarity and shared domains, and analyzes its victims and tactics, techniques and procedures. The standout technique is sending phishing mail through university infrastructure: the actor logs in to one university's VPN with stolen credentials, then uses a stolen mailbox at a second university to target a third, exploiting the trust between institutions.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10698
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10698
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.10698
- Author dataset (PATU23, OSF): https://osf.io/7vbzs?view_only=481b15d000d54319aff0339de276234a

### Contribution
- A timeline of 25 attacks on German-speaking universities with their impact (data exfiltration, encryption, preventive shutdowns, password resets and so on).
- A method for reconstructing a long-running phishing cluster from public scan records alone, and a published dataset (PATU23) of 442 scans.
- Header analysis, confirmed with five other universities, that reveals the VPN-at-one-university, mailbox-at-another, target-a-third delivery path.

### Evaluation
- By October 2023 the authors had found 270 phishing sites. Of these, 164 (60.7%) targeted 103 specific organizations, and 93 of those (90%) were universities. 58 of the 93 universities (62%) were German, about 14% of Germany's 423 universities. Targeting began with Australian universities in March 2020 and shifted to Europe, mainly Germany, from May 2020.
- All 24 phishing emails examined (involving 14 German academic institutions) carried an academic VPN IP in their headers. In one case, 11% of staff and students received phishing mail within 14 days.
- Outgoing mail from compromised accounts showed phishing during the day and bulk spam after 10 p.m., suggesting a financial motive. That it is the same actor is inferred from overlapping infrastructure and timing.
- Reliability: only sites someone submitted to URLScan are visible, so all counts are lower bounds. The authors also note that they followed one page design and may have missed variants.

#### Comment
- The data covers 2020 to 2023, so this is not fresh threat intelligence, but the abuse of inter-university trust and VPN access remains relevant.
- A case study by a German university institute and a security company. Not peer reviewed.
- Carried over as a pending candidate from the 2026-11-11 run.

# How Well Do SAST Tools Work Across Languages? An Empirical Study Using OCPP Implementations
> 2026 IEEE International Conference on Cyber Security and Resilience (CSR), pp. 250-255 (author-accepted manuscript on arXiv)
> 2026

OCPP, the protocol that manages electric vehicle charging stations, is specified at a high level, so security can vary a lot between implementations. The authors ran general-purpose static analyzers (SonarQube and Semgrep) and language-specific ones (such as Bandit for Python, SpotBugs for Java and Roslyn for C#) on twelve open-source OCPP 1.6 implementations, each written in a different language, to see how static analysis results vary across language ecosystems.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14362
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14362
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14362

### Contribution
- Claims to be the first empirical comparison of SAST tools across languages using multiple implementations of the same protocol.
- A practical takeaway: when choosing a language for security-critical systems, consider the static analysis tooling available for it.

### Evaluation
- Results: findings per language ranged from 2 in Rust to 1,689 in Java. SonarQube reported 3,428 findings in total and Semgrep 160. In Java, SonarQube produced 1,678 findings, mostly code quality and style; in Python, Bandit produced 641 against SonarQube's 34. Semgrep failed to run on the C implementation, and some tool-language combinations returned nothing.
- Reliability: only finding counts were recorded; there was no manual check of which findings are true positives, which the authors list as a limitation. Implementations differ in size and maturity, so counts cannot be read as differences in code quality between languages, and the authors themselves suggest mature languages simply have more tools. With one implementation per language, language effects and project effects cannot be separated.

#### Comment
- A short six-page paper at IEEE CSR, a mid-sized conference, by researchers from Montana State University and two US national laboratories (PNNL and INL).
- The conclusion that tooling ecosystems drive the results is unsurprising; the paper is more useful as a case study of static analysis on EV charging software. To avoid enabling exploitation, the authors withhold implementation names and detailed findings.
