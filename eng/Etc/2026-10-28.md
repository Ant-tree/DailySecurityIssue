# CVM-Store: A Secure Storage System for Confidential Virtual Machines
> Accepted at NDSS 2027 (accepted version posted to arXiv cs.CR), Technical University of Munich
> 2026 (to be presented in 2027)

Confidential virtual machines (CVMs) such as AMD SEV-SNP and Intel TDX encrypt memory in use to protect it from the cloud operator, but persistent storage such as NVMe sits outside the trust boundary, leaving data open to being read, tampered with or rolled back to a stale version. Layering conventional block-level encryption on the CVM I/O path stacks bounce-buffer copies on top of context switches and badly hurts performance. This paper presents CVM-Store, which co-designs a kernel-bypass userspace I/O engine with confidentiality, integrity and freshness protection. Its key insight is that neither authenticated encryption nor I/O is the bottleneck on its own; running them back to back is. CVM-Store hides the cost by performing encryption while DMA is still in flight.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34560

### Contribution
- Hardens an SPDK-based userspace NVMe driver against a malicious device, allocating DMA buffers only in CVM shared memory to limit exposed state. Fixes to SPDK's DMA handling and the SEV-SNP attestation pipeline found along the way were sent upstream.
- Replaces uniform block-level protection with primitives tailored to access patterns, and builds three abstractions on them: a key-value store, a shared log and a filesystem.
- Since CVMs lack hardware monotonic counters, it implements rollback protection with an external trusted counter service updated in epoch-based batches. Remote attestation and sealing bind keys and counter state to the attested CVM identity.

### Evaluation
- Setup: two AMD EPYC 9334/9654 SEV-SNP servers with a 3.8 TB KIOXIA CM7-R NVMe drive. Baselines are ext4 and Rollbaccine, an anti-rollback block layer for TEEs, with durability settings matched per experiment.
- For large objects with full protection enabled, it stays within about 1% (write) and 1 to 19% (read) of raw NVMe bandwidth, reaching up to 6.6 GiB/s writes and 13.3 GiB/s reads on a single core, faster even than unencrypted CVM kernel I/O.
- The key-value store was 5.5 to 7.0 times faster than RocksDB for 256 MB to 1 GB objects but slower than RocksDB below 1 MB. The shared log was 2.2 to 3.5 times slower than ext4 on small-entry reads.
- End-to-end attestation takes about 188 ms, and the counter synchronization interval (1 to 281 ms) lets applications trade performance against the size of the rollback window.
- Credibility of the metrics: the paper reports its weak spots on small objects alongside its strengths, and includes an ablation of each design decision plus read and write amplification measurements, so the results are credible. The baselines, however, are limited to ext4 and Rollbaccine.

#### Comment
- A systems paper that solves the relatively neglected problem of freshness for data at rest in confidential computing, at practical speeds. It matters right away for large data that must never be silently rolled back, such as AI model weights and checkpoints, when handled inside a CVM.
- Limitations: because it encrypts per abstraction rather than per block, metadata such as file sizes, object counts and access patterns remains visible to the host. It depends on an external counter service, and data written since the last synchronization can be lost after an adversarial restart. Side channels and denial of service are outside the threat model. The implementation is SEV-SNP only, with TDX and Arm CCA discussed as possible ports.
- NDSS is a top-tier security venue, and the source code is public on GitHub (TUM-DSE/CVM-Store-NDSS27), so reproducibility should be good.

# VulDec: Vulnerability Detection in Binaries via Neural Decompilation
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed, submitted to the journal Automated Software Engineering), University of Naples Federico II and Gran Sasso Science Institute (Italy), University of North Carolina at Charlotte (USA)
> 2026

Finding vulnerabilities in binaries without source code is hard because assembly carries little semantic information, and large datasets pairing assembly with source and vulnerability labels are scarce. This paper builds a CWE-annotated dataset that aligns C functions from real open-source projects with their x86 and ARM assembly, and proposes VulDec, an end-to-end pipeline that uses an LLM-based neural decompiler to recover C-like code from assembly and then detects vulnerabilities on that output. The authors argue that neural decompilation can stand in for conventional low-level representations.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33716

### Contribution
- Rebuilds 24 DiverseVul projects from their version history to align assembly with C functions: 28,426 ARM functions and 31,340 x86 functions, 23,163 of them shared by both architectures. The authors say the dataset is available on Figshare.
- Trains several assembly-to-C decompilers (Qwen2.5-Coder 3B, CodeT5+, DeepSeek-Coder and others) and several vulnerability detectors on the same data, then evaluates decompilation and detection both separately and chained together.
- Works on function fragments, so it applies where rule-based decompilers like Ghidra struggle because they need a complete control-flow graph.

### Evaluation
- Decompilation: on its own test set, Qwen2.5-Coder 3B reaches an edit similarity of about 56% and BLEU-4 of about 40 to 43%. On HumanEval (-O2) its edit similarity of 32.82% beats Ghidra (6.19%) and the LLM4Decompile family (around 13%), but its re-executability of 34.34% trails the Ghidra plus LLM4Decompile-Ref-22B combination (59.76%).
- Detection (in-distribution test set, 3% vulnerable functions): the best model (PLBART) has a macro F1 of 0.606, but on the vulnerable class alone precision is 0.18, recall 0.444 and F1 0.257. The DiverseVul baseline classifier scores a macro F1 of 0.472, and FlawFinder's vulnerable-class F1 is 0.077.
- End to end (out-of-distribution test set): the VulDec decompiler plus detector does best with a vulnerable-class F1 of 0.19, while Ghidra plus FlawFinder scores 0.00.
- Credibility of the metrics: the abstract's "macro F1 of 0.61" is heavily lifted by the benign class. The real ability to find vulnerable functions (F1 of 0.19 to 0.26) is still far from practical. The authors also acknowledge the generalization problem, with recall falling from 0.44 to 0.28 when moving from in-distribution to out-of-distribution data. Only one optimization level (-O2 with GCC 12.2) is used.

#### Comment
- From a reverse-engineering standpoint, it is interesting that neural decompilation produces output closer to human-written code than rule-based decompilers, and that this lets even a conventional static analyzer recover a little of its usefulness.
- Limitations: as the authors themselves list among threats to validity, other compilers, optimization levels and obfuscated code are not evaluated. This is not obfuscation or deobfuscation research, and there are no case studies on real firmware or commercial binaries.

# Cheap AI-Assisted Reverse Engineering and What It Will Do
> arXiv cs.CY, cross-listed to cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed, written as a law review article), Cornell Tech, Cornell Law School, Columbia University, New York University
> 2026

Intellectual property and technology law have long assumed that reverse engineering is expensive, slow and dependent on expertise. The law broadly permits it, but relies on that difficulty as a filter separating legitimate analysis from copying. This paper argues that the assumption is collapsing as coding agents make it cheap and easy to identify hardware components, extract firmware, decompile code and even clone entire apps. It walks this shift through trade secret, copyright, the anti-circumvention rules of DMCA Section 1201, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) and contract law, then lays out four ways the legal system could respond and what each would cost.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35633

### Contribution
- Contrasts manual reverse engineering (static and dynamic analysis with Ghidra, IDA, jadx, Frida and similar tools) with AI-assisted reverse engineering, using concrete cases to show which steps have become cheaper and by how much.
- Reframes the law's treatment of reverse engineering around the idea that cost acted as a filter, and concludes that AI has tilted the balance sharply toward reverse engineers.
- Sorts the possible responses into four: inertia, tightening the law to restore the old balance, affirmatively embracing AI-assisted reverse engineering, and a new compromise tying the right to reverse engineer to device ownership. It analyzes the consequences of each without endorsing or rejecting any.

### Evaluation
- This is not an empirical paper and has no quantitative evaluation. Its evidence consists of public cases, benchmark results published by other organizations (such as sharp gains by frontier models on a reverse-engineering benchmark within a few months), and analysis of case law and statutes.
- The cited figures were not measured by the authors and should be checked against their original sources; this summary does not repeat individual numbers.

#### Comment
- Although not a technical result, the authors predict that DMCA Section 1201 will become vendors' strongest legal lever, and that vendors will respond by piling on technical controls such as code encryption and obfuscation, server authentication, secure boot and analysis detection. It can be read as a prediction that AI will intensify the contest between obfuscation and deobfuscation.
- The observation that the CFAA makes cloud-side code easier to lock down, both technically and legally, suggests that app protection strategies may shift from client-side obfuscation toward running logic on the server.
- Limitations: the discussion centers on US law and does not carry over directly to other jurisdictions, and the manuscript has not been peer reviewed. The authors include James Grimmelmann of Cornell Law School, a widely known scholar in cyberlaw.
