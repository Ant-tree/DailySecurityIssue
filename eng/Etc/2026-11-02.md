# OrionLink: Single Sign-On with Oblivious Identity Brokers
> NDSS Symposium 2027 (per the paper PDF: 22-26 March 2027, Seoul, DOI 10.14722/ndss.2027.231871), public version on arXiv cs.CR 2610.41323. Beihang University, University of Science and Technology of China, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications
> 2026 (to be presented in 2027)

In single sign-on (SSO), a service (the relying party) delegates login to an identity provider such as Google or Apple. More and more services now go through an identity broker that bundles many identity providers behind a single interface; the authors cite a 2025 measurement study finding that about a quarter of SSO-enabled websites use one. The catch is that the broker relays every login and sees user identifiers, access tokens, session cookies, and personal data such as email addresses and phone numbers in plaintext. As the 2023 breach of Okta's customer support system showed, when tokens and cookies for many services leaked at once, a broker is a single point of failure whose compromise spreads downstream.

OrionLink redesigns the SSO flow so that the broker still relays every login but learns nothing about the user's real identity, which services they use, or their tokens and personal data. The broker is modeled as honest but curious: it follows the protocol but tries to infer whatever it can from what it observes.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41323 (full text read from the complete paper PDF in the authors' repository, https://github.com/Ooo0ption/Orionlink)

### Contribution
- Per-service anonymous pseudonyms: anonymous credentials based on Pointcheval-Sanders signatures are combined with an oblivious pseudorandom function (OPRF), so the identity provider derives a different pseudonym for each service without exposing the user identifier. Even if the broker and several services pool what they see, they cannot link the same user.
- Anonymous authenticated key agreement: a variant of Signal's X3DH sets up an end-to-end encrypted channel between the service and the identity provider, so tokens and personal data cross the broker only as ciphertext. Since the identity provider sees only freshly randomized credentials at each login, it cannot track which services a user has authorized either.
- Two practical features: users can review and revoke service authorizations without revealing those relationships to the identity provider, and token refresh is forward secure, deriving a new session key at each refresh in the spirit of the double ratchet.
- These properties are formally defined and proven in the UC framework, and a prototype of more than 9,000 lines (Go servers plus a browser-side cryptography module) is open source. Browser-side cryptography runs in a separate iframe (the Trusted Computation Agent) on an origin distinct from the identity provider.

### Evaluation
- Setup: the service, broker, and identity provider run as Docker containers on one server (16 cores at 4.3 GHz, 96 GB of RAM). The baseline is a standard brokered SSO built by the authors on the OIDC authorization code flow with the same structure. User clicks are automated with Playwright.
- Local computation: averaged over 1,000 runs, key generation takes 46.59 ms, pseudonym generation 2.58 ms, and the anonymous key agreement 11.39 ms, for 67.50 ms in total.
- End-to-end latency: with a 40 ms round-trip time, a full login takes 2,080 ms with OrionLink versus 500 ms with the standard flow (100 runs each). At 0, 100, and 200 ms, latency grows roughly linearly with network delay for both.
- Load: at 1,000 requests per second on the key agreement interface, 95th percentile latency stays around 10 ms.
- Mobile: the browser-side cryptography takes 32.4 ms on an iPhone 15 (Safari) and 32.2 ms on a laptop (medians over 100 runs), so there is no measurable mobile penalty.
- Reliability of the metrics: the paper reports its overhead against the baseline plainly (logins take about four times as long), and nothing looks inflated. The baseline, however, was implemented by the authors, and usability was not evaluated on real commercial brokers or through a user study.

#### Comment
- The authors claim this is the first work to treat the brokered SSO architecture itself as a privacy risk. Compared with prior schemes (UP-SSO, BISON, ARPSSO, MISO, and others), it stands out by offering end-to-end confidentiality while remaining compatible with brokers. The motivation is timely, since setups like MCP, where a server acts as both identity provider and relying party, are driving demand for brokered SSO.
- Limitations: because the broker never sees plaintext, it gives up features such as attribute value normalization, broker-side attribute-based access control, and content-based threat analytics, as the authors acknowledge. A fully malicious broker, a compromised browser or extension, and correlation by a global passive adversary are out of scope.
- Venue: NDSS is one of the top security conferences. The venue was confirmed from the NDSS 2027 notice and DOI on the first page of the paper PDF; it was not cross-checked against the official NDSS accepted papers list.
- Source of the full text: the complete paper PDF and the artifact appendix (Artifact-Appendix.pdf) in the authors' repository.
- Verification for a paper from Chinese institutions: the authors are affiliated with the School of Cyber Science and Technology at Beihang University, the University of Science and Technology of China, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, all real and major research institutions, and institutional email addresses are listed. The venue is NDSS, which has rigorous review, and the repository ships scripts and a Docker setup that reproduce experiments E1 to E3 along with an artifact evaluation appendix, so reproducibility is high. The evaluation reports results that are unfavorable to the proposed system (higher latency) as they are, and there is no sign that the metrics were inflated.
