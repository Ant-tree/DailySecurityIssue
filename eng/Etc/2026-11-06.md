# PIMROP: A Feasibility Study of Return-Oriented Programming on Commercial Processing-in-Memory Architectures
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.06036 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Syracuse University (USA)
> 2026

Processing-in-Memory (PIM) places small programmable cores next to DRAM banks so that data is processed where it lives. These cores run outside the control-flow defenses that protect host programs, and the security of the code running on them has barely been studied. This paper is the first to show that return-oriented programming (ROP) works on a commercially shipped PIM instruction set, the UPMEM DPU, whose cores run C compiled by a dedicated LLVM toolchain.

The authors built Galileo-for-DPU, a gadget scanner adapted to the DPU's fixed-width, 8-byte-aligned RISC encoding, and ran everything on the UPMEM functional simulator in a reproducible Docker and VM setup. The attack scenario is multi-tenant DPU inference: one tenant's request size sets the length of an mram_read on a DPU shared with another tenant, so copying from the large MRAM into a small WRAM buffer can overflow it.

- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06036
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06036
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.06036
- Author repository (test harness): https://github.com/wtegge2/ROP-in-PIM

### Contribution
- The first demonstrated code-reuse attack on a commercial PIM instruction set.
- Shows that the runtime library every kernel links already contains 317 indirect-branch terminators and 517 non-trivial gadgets covering all five canonical ROP primitive classes, and that all 13 scanned PrIM benchmarks carry gadgets too.
- Two end-to-end attacks: a stack buffer overflow that overwrites the saved return address and diverts control into a routine copying a secret into host-readable memory, and a gadget-only chain that leaks the same secret without diverting into any such routine.
- Builds a conditional branch with a runtime-chosen target and a loop with a runtime-chosen count from the runtime library alone, the two ingredients for Turing-complete code reuse.
- A defense-gap analysis: the DPU has no ASLR, CFI or pointer authentication, and stack canaries fail twice over, since the toolchain places them on the wrong side of the frame on this upward-growing stack and even a correctly placed canary uses a fixed value the host never seeds. A bounds-checked DMA is verified to stop the attack.

### Evaluation
- Both attacks reproduced 10 of 10 times. With a correctly placed canary, a sink-free chain still succeeded 10 of 10 times.
- With a bounds-checked DMA, the attack succeeded 0 of 10 times while benign requests completed 10 of 10. The added instructions amount to about 1.5% of the 130-instruction baseline.
- An audit of all 48 mram_read call sites in the 16 PrIM benchmarks found that 42 use compile-time constant lengths and the other 6 clamp their lengths, so none admits the overflow used here. The authors' summary is that the bug class is latent rather than common in today's code.
- Reliability of the metrics: all experiments ran on the UPMEM functional simulator, not physical PIM hardware, and the target kernel was made vulnerable on purpose. The authors are upfront about this and scope the work as a feasibility study.

#### Comment
- An early warning that, once PIM is used for multi-tenant inference, memory-side cores beyond the reach of host protections become a new attack point. The paper makes concrete suggestions, such as making canary placement and bounds-checked DMA toolchain defaults.
- Limitations: no physical hardware experiments, and no exploitable overflow was found in real code.
- Credibility: an unreviewed manuscript from a US university; the test harness is public, which helps reproducibility.

# SenTrojan: Revealing Analog Hardware Trojan Attacks on Sensors
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.06103 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Zhejiang University, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (China)
> 2026

Most sensor attacks exploit unintended physical weaknesses a sensor already has, such as sensitivity to ultrasound or electromagnetic interference. Those weaknesses vary across sensor models and hardware revisions, which makes the attacks hard to generalize. This paper looks at a different threat model: can an adversary somewhere in a supply chain split across design, fabrication, packaging, PCB assembly and maintenance plant a hidden attack surface into a sensor before deployment?

SenTrojan is a framework for analog hardware Trojans that embeds malicious functionality in analog signal circuitry shared by many sensor architectures, such as amplifiers and filters, and activates it with a crafted external physical signal. Once triggered, it can cause anything from permanent denial of service to static or dynamic spoofing of readings. There are two designs. The Logic-Rule-Driven (LRD) Trojan adds a small analog monitor that detects trigger features such as frequency, amplitude and duration, giving reliable activation across sensor types. The Physical-Effect-Driven (PED) Trojan exploits native analog effects such as nonlinearity and rectification to achieve the attack with minimal added components. Inserted parts are limited to small-scale integrated circuits and discrete components.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06103
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06103
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.06103

### Contribution
- Claims the first systematic study of analog sensor Trojans, extending sensor security from unintended physical weaknesses to deliberately implanted ones.
- Offers two complementary approaches: a broadly applicable logic-driven design and a minimally invasive effect-driven design.
- Lays out design conditions for Trojans that stay close enough to normal behavior to pass factory testing while still attacking efficiently.

### Evaluation
- Targets: 14 sensors spanning 6 transduction principles, including temperature, sound, pressure and light, tested with Trojan circuits added on PCB prototypes.
- Results: an average trigger success rate above 95%, over 97% similarity to benign behavior, a zero false-trigger rate for LRD designs, and under 5% average deviation in drift, noise floor and response time. Some scenarios report 100% spoofing success at 5.1 meters, and ultrasound-injection tests report 96% and 98% success over 50 trials at 20 cm for two sensor models.
- Applications: demonstrations that mimic real settings, such as an intrusion alarm and a simulated vehicle driven into a collision.
- Reliability of the metrics: since the attacker implants the circuit directly, high success rates are not surprising; stealth is what matters. Stealth is measured with the authors' own metrics (similarity, false triggers, deviation), and we did not find a quantitative evaluation against real manufacturer tests or existing hardware Trojan detection methods. There is no baseline comparison because comparable prior methods essentially do not exist.

#### Comment
- Verification of a paper from a Chinese institution: the USSLab of Wenyuan Xu and Xiaoyu Ji at Zhejiang University has a long record of sensor-attack papers at IEEE S&P, USENIX Security, CCS and NDSS, and one author's personal homepage was confirmed through web search. As an arXiv manuscript, venue indexing does not apply. Experiments use real sensors and PCB prototypes, with trial counts stated (for example 50 trials). No public code or schematics were found in the paper. The metrics are high but plausible for a directly implanted circuit, and the self-defined stealth metrics remain a limitation. On balance the paper is included, with the lack of reproduction material noted.
- Passive shielding and active detection are proposed as defenses, but their effect is only lightly measured.
- Phones and other mobile devices rely on many sensors, so the work is a useful reference for hardware supply-chain threat models.
- Full text source: official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.

# Shoot on (Core)Sight: Re-enabling Secure Debug on the RP2350 with Photon-Emission Microscopy and Laser Fault Injection
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.06386 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Ledger (France, internship), ESILV (France), Georgia Institute of Technology (USA)
> 2026

Raspberry Pi's RP2350 microcontroller protects firmware and secrets with secure boot and access policies stored in OTP (one-time-programmable) memory, and stores critical flags redundantly across 8 consecutive OTP rows to resist fault injection. The paper's point is that fault-resistant policy storage does not imply fault-resistant enforcement. At runtime a single register, DEBUGEN, can override the stored policy, and that weakest link is what the author attacked.

Differential photon-emission microscopy (PEM) from the back of the die first located the emission tied to the DEBUGEN register, by subtracting images captured while a loop repeatedly toggled chosen bits. Laser fault injection (LFI) scans over that narrowed area then found two sites, about 1.7 micrometers apart, where pulses set the bits enabling core 1 Secure debug. An online sequence guided only by Serial Wire Debug (SWD) feedback restored sustained Secure debug access, and a rescue reset, which halts boot before firmware runs, let the author read a 128-bit test secret from OTP.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06386
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06386
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.06386

### Contribution
- A differential PEM method that isolates emission associated with a single memory-mapped register, using an atomic-XOR excitation loop, acquisition-order balancing and outlier rejection.
- Localization of two adjacent laser-sensitive sites on the RP2350A4 and an SWD-guided attack sequence that restores sustained core 1 Secure debug access.
- A rescue-reset bypass of runtime OTP locks that applies to any RP2350 secret those locks protect, because rescue mode stops boot before firmware applies them.

### Evaluation
- Target: a single RP2350A4, provisioned with the same OTP page-48 policy as the RP2350 Hacking Challenge and secure boot under the author's test signing key.
- Equipment and settings: an InGaAs-detector PEM and 100 ns laser pulses at 40% to 70% (scanning) or 40% (online attack) of the laser's rated 2.97 W. The responsive area was about 0.006% of the measured logic area.
- Result: the 128-bit test secret was read without modifying the stored OTP policy.
- Reliability of the metrics: the result comes from one chip, and no success-rate or attempt statistics are given. The attack needs destructive backside preparation, and the author puts the equipment cost at about USD 250,000.

#### Comment
- A concrete lesson for hardware security design: redundancy in storage is not enough; it has to extend along the whole chain, from OTP to the register that grants debug access. The author notes that firmware countermeasures, such as blocking debugger access to OTP through ACCESSCTRL or watching DEBUGEN and resetting on change, depend on firmware running and are useless in rescue mode.
- Limitations: the cost and preparation imply a lab-grade or state-level attacker, and the result was confirmed on a single chip.
- Credibility: a short single-author manuscript from an internship at hardware-wallet maker Ledger. The findings were disclosed to Raspberry Pi on July 28, 2026.
- Full text source: official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.
