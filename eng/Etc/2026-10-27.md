# The 3DS Oracle: BIN Attack via the Network
> arXiv cs.CR (extended preprint, not peer reviewed), BRAC University, Bangladesh
> 2026

EMV 3-D Secure (3DS) is meant to fight fraud in online card payments, but this paper argues that in browser-mediated 3DS integrations, the response a client can see before financial authorization can act as a classification channel that reveals issuer routing and account state. An attacker can generate Luhn-valid card number (PAN) candidates and use the first response of the 3DS lookup stage to tell issued cards apart, and the authors document existing card-testing tools and paid services that already exploit such signals. The study combines standards analysis, architectural reconstruction, ProVerif modeling, a documentation audit and a bounded live measurement.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32206

### Contribution
- Shows through live measurement that the first lookup-stage (AReq/ARes) response falls into different response classes for researcher-owned cards and adjacent card numbers.
- Formalizes the EMVCo transaction ID equality rule, which supports conditional attribution of a response to the component that generated it.
- Models the card number space as a prefix tree and shows how an early-drop response at the Directory Server, paired with a range-boundary reason code, could sharply reduce the search.
- Frames the root cause as systemic rather than a single vendor's flaw: decoupled pre-authorization authentication, weak requestor-boundary assumptions and fragmented accountability. It calls for consortium-level reform.

### Evaluation
- On a researcher-controlled merchant deployment using the Braintree/Cardinal browser path, seven researcher-owned cards (Visa and Mastercard), each from a distinct issuer ACS and card-range cohort, were compared with three adjacent Luhn-valid numbers each: 28 inputs, five fresh sessions apiece, 140 observations. Responses separated in six of seven cohorts.
- Requests came from a standalone HTTP client that ran no browser or SDK, and no payment authorization or settlement was attempted. Narrower checks on Stripe and Adyen are reported separately.
- Credibility of the metrics: the sample is very small and deliberately limited for ethical reasons, and the authors state plainly that they make no claim about network-wide prevalence or real attack scale. They also limit the formal model to complementing, not predicting, the live results.

#### Comment
- Its distinctive point is a structural one: each party in the payment ecosystem can be locally compliant while the system as a whole still leaks. The introduction positions it as a follow-up to related IEEE S&P 2017 work.
- This is an extended manuscript based on independent research begun in 2018. It is very long and cautious to the point of being defensive, has not been through conference or journal review, and states that responsible disclosure has been initiated.
- Some row-level experimental records are withheld, so independent reproduction is difficult.

# Not an Accident: Exploiting Safety Filter Inadequacy in Humanoid Robots
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), Arizona State University, Carnegie Mellon University
> 2026

Optimization-based safety filters on humanoid robots mathematically correct control commands so the robot does not collide with nearby people or objects. This paper shows that the guarantee only holds when the filter's parameters suit the task and environment, and when they do not, an ordinary user with no extra privileges can trigger a collision. By inserting a single intermediate goal between normal tasks (a goal insertion attack), the attacker makes the safety filter fail to avoid an obstacle on the way back to the original goal, causing a collision or a deadlock. The authors present SIREN, a tool that finds such goals automatically.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31923

### Contribution
- Analyzes three causes of safety filter failure (insufficient escape rate, insufficient control authority, no proactive failure avoidance) and is the first to frame them as a security vulnerability.
- Implements SIREN, which searches for attack goals using the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM).
- Successfully attacks seven representative safety filters, SSA, CBF, SSS, their slack-relaxed variants and p-SSA, both in simulation and on a real Unitree G1 humanoid.

### Evaluation
- From the SPARK benchmark, the authors build 35 vulnerable scenarios (five per filter) in which the original task succeeds but fails once a goal is inserted. SIREN finds 3,277 attacks versus 1,760 for random search, and both find at least one attack in all 35 scenarios.
- On the real robot, one scenario per filter was chosen at random under an upper-body, static-obstacle setup and reproduced; the same failures seen in simulation occurred on hardware.
- Credibility of the metrics: tasks that fail even without an attack are excluded, which isolates the attack's effect. However, the abstract claims "three times as many attacks" as the baseline, while the body and contribution list give 1.86x (3,277 versus 1,760). The body's figure is the one to trust.

#### Comment
- It brings the idea that a safety mechanism can itself become an attack surface to robot control, and it pairs security researchers (Tiffany Bao, Ruoyu Wang, David Brumley) with a robot control researcher (Changliu Liu).
- The attacker is assumed to know the deployed filter's algorithm and parameters, and the authors admit they cannot estimate how likely an attack is to exist for an arbitrary task and environment. The hardware validation is also restricted to a narrow upper-body, static-obstacle setting.

# SEMPRE: Semantic-Aware Protocol Reverse Engineering via Consistency-Driven Joint Optimization
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), Illinois Institute of Technology, Indiana University, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore Management University
> 2026

Analyzing undocumented proprietary protocols or malware command-and-control traffic requires protocol reverse engineering: splitting messages into fields and working out what each field means. SEMPRE grounds an LLM in taint-flow and loop traces from dynamic program analysis and uses tree-of-thought search to slice messages into fields and detect nested sub-payloads, producing a hierarchical layout. It then infers each field's type and role and repeatedly refines boundaries, freezing high-confidence segments, until the result converges.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31844

### Contribution
- Proposes a fully automated framework that replaces hand-written heuristics with recursive LLM reasoning anchored in dynamic analysis evidence, for both field segmentation and semantic inference.
- Uses an EM-style joint optimization that alternates between boundaries and semantics so that results improve monotonically with each round.
- Shows that it still works when a binary breaks the single-pass assumption of existing tools, as with a Mirai variant that reads its buffer twice.

### Evaluation
- Baselines: six techniques, PolyGlot, Tupni, AutoFormat, BinPRE, BinaryInferno and DynPRE. The first four use the original authors' code; BinaryInferno and DynPRE, which have no released code, were reimplemented from their papers.
- Data: the eight protocols from the BinPRE paper (Modbus, S7comm, Ethernet/IP, DNP3, DNS, FTP, TFTP, HTTP), three real undocumented protocols from GitHub (KTP, CCN, Xfer), and the Mirai botnet protocol as a case study.
- Average field-detection F1 is 0.76 against 0.39 for BinPRE, the strongest competitor, and semantic-inference F1 is 0.72 against 0.13. On Mirai, BinPRE's single-pass assumption breaks and its accuracy drops to almost zero; even when told the buffer is read twice it reaches only 0.55 field F1, while SEMPRE scores 0.83 with or without the hint. The backbone model is GPT-o3.
- Credibility of the metrics: reusing original code and publishing the ground-truth details (Table I) is good practice. Still, BinPRE did not release its message data, so the authors evaluated on messages they captured themselves, and two baselines are reimplementations, so the size of the gap should be read with some caution.

#### Comment
- The authors note that semantic labels are limited to six to stay comparable with BinPRE, and that reusing byte-level taint tracking makes the tool weak on protocols such as CAN, where meaning is encoded at the bit level.
- Using dynamic analysis evidence to rein in LLM hallucination connects directly to practical work such as malware C2 analysis and IDS signature generation. Code is released through an anonymized repository.

# Embedding Large PQC KEMs in IKEv2
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), joint industry-academia work including genua GmbH, Germany
> 2026

Post-quantum (PQC) key encapsulation mechanisms (KEMs) carry very large public keys, which fit poorly with IKEv2, the VPN handshake protocol whose initial exchange cannot be fragmented. The IETF is discussing several fixes, but they had never been compared under identical conditions. The authors systematically screened the IETF corpus, identified five existing proposals, added three of their own (one based on forward error correction, plus QUIKE, which runs IKEv2 over QUIC, and QUIKE-composite, which adds a composite key exchange), and implemented all eight on the same open-source codebase.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32356

### Contribution
- First head-to-head comparison of IETF proposals for performance and stability on one implementation base and one testbed.
- Identifies two specification gaps in existing acknowledgment drafts.
- Proposes a forward error correction approach and the QUIC-based QUIKE and QUIKE-composite.

### Evaluation
- All eight mechanisms were implemented on openiked-portable and measured between two directly connected bare-metal Linux hosts with ML-KEM-1024, FrodoKEM-1344 and Classic McEliece 8192128 (all NIST Level 5), varying bandwidth (1/10/100 Mbit/s), delay and packet loss (0-10%): 126,500 handshakes in 1,296 configurations. Metrics are completion rate, completion time and packets per direction.
- No mechanism fully compensates for the effect of key size. With Classic McEliece, every approach either congests the link or takes close to two minutes per handshake on average under harsh conditions. For ML-KEM and FrodoKEM there are two ways forward, improving already standardized extensions or delegating transport to QUIC, and QUIKE-composite performs best overall.
- Credibility of the metrics: each configuration was repeated until the result was decisive (at least 80 runs in practice), and all raw data is published in a replication package. The authors list as threats that netem's random loss differs from the bursty loss seen on the Internet and that implementation tuning may have disadvantaged particular drafts.

#### Comment
- Researchers from a VPN vendor feeding measured data from real implementations into the IETF discussion makes this valuable for both standardization and product development.
- Authentication is out of scope, with static pre-shared keys used throughout; adding post-quantum signatures would make the message size problem worse.
- The proposal screening was done by a single screener, mitigated by criteria fixed in advance and a fully published decision record.

# WADE: An LSTM-Based Anomaly Detection Engine for WebAssembly Applications
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), Politecnico di Milano, Northeastern University
> 2026

WebAssembly lacks runtime protections such as stack canaries and address space layout randomization, which leaves it exposed to classic memory attacks. WADE collects function-level execution traces of browser-executed WebAssembly applications under benign conditions, trains a per-application LSTM model, and flags runtime flows that deviate from the learned patterns as attacks. The goal is to catch unknown attacks without per-vulnerability signatures.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31984

### Contribution
- Designs an anomaly detection system that finds malicious control flow in in-browser function-level traces.
- Proposes a multi-threshold detection scheme with four parallel detectors and adaptive weighted voting.

### Evaluation
- With no standard benchmark available, the authors built their own dataset: starting from seven real vulnerable programs documented in prior work, they used Claude Opus 4 to generate ten variants each for six of them, for 67 programs in total, and report validating every generated variant by hand. Ten vulnerability classes are covered.
- They report 100% recall (zero false negatives), 96.90% precision, 98.42% F1 and a 0.42% false positive rate. A first-order Markov chain baseline reaches only 7.1% recall.
- Credibility of the metrics: most evaluation programs are LLM-generated variants of a few seeds and are likely similar to each other, and the only baseline is a simple Markov chain, so the numbers are hard to generalize.

#### Comment
- The authors deserve credit for raising the base-rate fallacy themselves: at realistic attack prevalence, even a 0.42% false positive rate could mean roughly four out of five alerts are false alarms.
- Data-only and side-channel attacks are out of scope, benign paths not seen in training become false positives, and adaptive attackers, such as mimicry attacks built only from observed transitions, are not evaluated.
