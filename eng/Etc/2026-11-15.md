# Anonymous in Name Only: A Critical Review and a Reidentification Exercise on the European Commission's Anonymization Measures for Google Search Data under Article 6(11) of the DMA
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Universitat Rovira i Virgili (Catalonia), École normale supérieure (PSL)
> 2026

Under Article 6(11) of the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA), Alphabet must share anonymized Google Search data with competing search engines, and in July 2026 the European Commission set out the anonymization measures. User identifiers, precise timestamps, layout information and image queries are removed. What remains (query text, NUTS3 location, language, device type and day) goes through two thresholds: every entity in a query must be on an allow list of entities used by at least 50 signed-in users over the past 13 months, and the query must be shorter than the 95th percentile of 2025 queries. Location is then generalized so that each language-location-device combination is shared by at least 1,000 users, and contractual clauses forbid reidentification. The paper argues that these measures rest on unjustified heuristics with no formal privacy guarantee, and shows easy reidentifications in real Google Search data protected by exactly these measures (a six-month sample).

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17012
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17012
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17012

### Contribution
- Reviews the most concrete anonymization standard any regulator has issued for search-data sharing, and lays out its conceptual flaws in risk characterization, suppression thresholds and reliance on contracts.
- Demonstrates reidentification, record linkage and sensitive-attribute inference with simple, automatable attack patterns on the same data competing search engines would receive.
- Concludes that the measures meet none of the three criteria in the European Data Protection Board's anonymization guidelines (no record isolation, no linkage, no inference).

### Evaluation
- Data: six months of real Google Search data provided by Alphabet and protected under the Commission's measures (mostly a Spanish-language sample).
- Name pattern: 421,314 unique queries containing Spanish for "my name is" were ranked by Gemini by how rare the name was and whether location appeared, and only the top 100 were checked by hand against public sources. About a dozen people were reidentified, along with sensitive details such as requests to forge documents, health information and mental-health concerns.
- Email pattern: 25,687 queries combining "gmail" with "@" or "at" had slipped past the email detector; password-related queries among them were matched to public profiles such as LinkedIn. Even names on the allow list could be pinned down when combined with birth year and query location.
- Reliability: this is a set of low-effort examples, not a systematic measurement of the reidentification rate, so the overall risk is not quantified. The authors argue instead that because the data covers nearly the entire EEA population, even a small percentage means thousands of exposed people. All examples are redacted and translated.

#### Comment
- Josep Domingo-Ferrer and David Sánchez have long track records in statistical disclosure control and anonymization, and they tested the actual regulated data, which gives the findings weight. The striking part is how easy reidentification remained in data that went through far more processing than the AOL search logs ever did.
- It is largely a legal and policy critique, so it does not spell out a concrete technical alternative (such as which formal privacy model to apply). The use of an LLM to rank candidates is a reminder that the cost of this kind of attack keeps falling.

# HARE: Fast Read Disturbance Threshold Profiling in Modern DRAM Chips via Reduced Timing Constraints
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). SAFARI Research Group, ETH Zurich
> 2026

Most mitigations for DRAM read disturbance (RowHammer and RowPress) only work if they are configured with an accurate read disturbance threshold (RDT in the paper), the minimum number of activations that causes the first bitflip. That value drifts unpredictably over time, so it has to be re-measured during operation, and because it differs from row to row, profiling a whole 16-bank DDR4 module takes hundreds of seconds. HARE deliberately shortens the timing constraints between consecutive DRAM commands while profiling (fastHammer). This both cuts the time per hammer and worsens the disturbance, so the first bitflip appears after fewer activations. HARE-Direct profiles every row with fastHammer and reports a conservative minimum; HARE-Filter ranks rows with fastHammer and re-profiles only the top-ranked ones with the regular RowHammer pattern.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15469
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.15469
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.15469

### Contribution
- First characterization of read disturbance under reduced timing constraints, on 180 real DDR4 chips (25 modules) from four major manufacturers.
- Shows that fastHammer flips over 95% of the cells that regular RowHammer flips and preserves the RDT ranking of rows (in every module the weakest RowHammer row is within the top 5.6% of the fastHammer ranking), which justifies re-profiling only a small candidate set.
- Uses a probabilistic error model built from the measurements to quantify why a mitigation configured by one-time profiling is unreliable.

### Evaluation
- fastHammer measured a single row's RDT 31.2% faster and lowered the module's minimum RDT by 21.7% on average (27.0-33.5% depending on the manufacturer).
- Across one million Monte Carlo trials, a system with no online profiler hit an uncorrectable error in 99.9% of cases (mean time to failure 8.5 minutes). At the same 20% guardband, HARE cut that probability from 66.8% to 0.67%.
- In Ramulator 2.0 with four-core workload mixes from SPEC CPU2006/2017, TPC, MediaBench and YCSB, over 36 configurations (three mitigations PRAC, Graphene and PARA, three RDT values, four profiling intervals), HARE beat the conventional RowHammer profiler in every case, by 0.9-54.4% (HARE-Direct) and 1.5-47.5% (HARE-Filter) in weighted speedup.
- Reliability: the chip characterization is large and builds on this group's long experimental track record. It is limited to DDR4, and the performance gains come from simulation.

#### Comment
- This is an operational defense technique, not an attack. Whether real memory controllers can run with reduced timing constraints during operation (that is, outside the standard) will decide how practical it is.
- It turns the group's earlier finding that RDT varies over time into something deployable. Whether the same approach carries over to mobile LPDDR memory remains to be checked.

# Look Here, Then Decide: Small Decision Models in a Harness Find Memory-Safety Vulnerabilities at Scale on Commodity GPUs
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Cyrex, Deus Rex (industry research)
> 2026

Can a team with a few desktop-class GPUs and small open models, and no frontier model, find memory-safety bugs in C and C++ open source at scale? This paper's answer is that the surrounding system matters more than the model. It selects widely packaged projects with little fuzzing coverage, mines revision histories for incomplete security fixes (a check added in one place but missing at a similar copy elsewhere), and searches for risky code shapes such as input-dependent lengths in buffer writes. A 4B decision model, Intern-Decision-4B, which returns scores for fixed answers in a single forward pass instead of generating text, then scores every candidate function to set the review order. Stronger coding agents and humans confirm whether a real vulnerability exists.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16565
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16565
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16565

### Contribution
- Measures that a small decision model is weak as a standalone classifier (it can barely tell a vulnerable function from its own patch) yet useful for ordering what to review.
- Presents a harness built from incomplete-fix miners, shape localizers, reach-based project selection and ranking instead of thresholds.
- Searched more than 4,000 projects and confirmed 187 memory-safety findings, all of which are being reported to maintainers under coordinated disclosure.

### Evaluation
- Standalone classification: AUC 0.73 for vulnerable versus benign functions, but only 0.56 for a vulnerable function versus its own patched version.
- Recall: on 75 public CVEs from projects with none of their findings, the fixed function landed in the repository's top 25 in 21% of cases, 1.4 times as often as a random ordering of the same queues. Most misses were locations no localizer had flagged.
- Campaign queues: the model's order put 7.5 times as many confirmed findings in the top 10 as random order, which the authors call a selection-biased upper bound (the queues were chosen because they contained a finding, and were reviewed in the model's order).
- Worst-case CVSS 3.1 severity of the 187 findings: 12 critical, 148 high, 23 medium, 4 low. Throughput: 4.4 decisions per second on one GB10 and 9.8 on an RTX 4090.
- Reliability: the limitations are stated carefully. The classification sample has only 71 vulnerable functions, triage and proof used stronger models whose cost was not measured, and the comparison with rule engines (Opengrep, CodeQL) covers cost only, not detection quality. The 187 findings are not yet public, so they cannot be checked externally.

#### Comment
- The decision model (Intern-Decision-4B) is an open model fine-tuned by InternLM from Qwen3.5-4B, but the authors and the experiments have no link to Chinese institutions.
- It reads like an industry research report and has not been peer reviewed. The authors themselves describe "harness design matters more than model size" as an interpretation, not a controlled comparison.
- Only write-shaped memory errors are in scope; reads, use-after-free and logic bugs are not.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).
