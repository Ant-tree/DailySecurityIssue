# The Native Menace: Host-to-Browser Cross-Boundary Attacks via Native Messaging
> To appear at IEEE S&P 2027 (IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy), per the authors' artifact repository; posted as arXiv cs.CR 2611.04291. University of Illinois Chicago (USA)
> 2026 (conference in 2027)

Browser extensions can talk to desktop apps installed on the user's machine (native hosts) through the NativeMessaging API. A password manager app hands credentials to its extension for autofill; an electronic ID app handles website sign-in and digital signing. Extension security research has mostly focused on malicious web pages or malicious extensions, leaving this channel largely unexamined. This paper is the first systematic analysis of NativeMessaging and shows that a malicious app running with ordinary user privileges can intercept or impersonate either end of the channel.

The attack starts from the fact that the channel has no root of trust. The browser finds the native host through a manifest file, and user-level manifests can be modified by any process running as the user (the profile folder on macOS, the HKCU registry on Windows). Chromium-based browsers also search the user-level location before the system-level one. An attacker can therefore point a manifest's executable path at its own binary, or plant a user-level manifest with the same name as a system-installed host so that it shadows the real one. If an app rewrites its manifest every time it launches, the attacker simply watches for the change and poisons it again. On this basis the authors build bidirectional interception, a full man-in-the-middle that relays, reads and alters every message between extension and host; unidirectional interception, a confused-deputy attack against either side; and host imitation, which works even when no native host is installed.

### Contribution
- Identifies fundamental flaws in how NativeMessaging is designed and deployed: no manifest integrity, shadowing through the manifest search order, and no mutual authentication between extension and host.
- Presents bidirectional and unidirectional variants of a "host-to-browser cross-boundary" attack and applies them to 17 major apps.
- Demonstrates severe outcomes: full vault leakage from password managers such as 1Password, Bitwarden, KeePassXC and RoboForm; user impersonation and legally binding document signing in e-government services across the EU, Latin America and India (Web eID, Web PKI and others); interference with video-conferencing sessions; theft of authentication tokens; and misuse of screen-capture and keystroke-injection permissions. The vulnerable extensions have about 532 million cumulative installations.
- Shows that on operating systems with strong app isolation such as macOS, the channel is not merely an alternative path but the only way across the isolation boundary: the attacks on 1Password, Bitwarden, RoboForm, Web PKI, Web Signer and Web eID could not be reproduced with the same user privileges without NativeMessaging.
- Proposes mitigations for the operating system, the browser and the apps, recommending at the app level a simple scheme of message encryption with a secret shared out of band.

### Evaluation
- Ecosystem measurement: from the August 14, 2026 Chrome Web Store snapshot, the authors parsed manifests for 250,735 extensions and found 1,972 that declare the nativeMessaging permission, with about 1.07 billion cumulative installations. Half have around 1,000 installs, but the top 5% account for hundreds of millions.
- Attack validation: 17 extensions chosen for install count, category and sensitivity were tested with Chrome 151 on macOS 26.3 and Windows 11 (Bitwarden only on macOS, as no Windows device with a biometric sensor was available). The attacks were replicated on Firefox for Web eID, Web PKI, 1Password, KeePassXC, Bitwarden and RoboForm. For Web eID, the authors deployed the official app and the reference token-validation implementation to demonstrate the full authentication flow.
- An exception: iCloud Passwords was not vulnerable because its manifest is symlinked into a location protected by System Integrity Protection, which itself serves as a model for mitigation.
- Disclosure outcomes: Google and Mozilla consider user-level local attackers outside their threat models and closed the reports; Edge, Opera and Vivaldi did not commit to changes. Foxit deployed HMAC-SHA256 message integrity and anti-replay protection, Anthropic confirmed the issue had been reported before and is tracked for remediation, and RoboForm is exploring changes. 1Password, Bitwarden and KeePassXC assessed the reports against their threat models but did not commit to changes.
- Reliability of the metrics: proofs of concept against current versions of real apps, plus vendor confirmations, make the results credible. The 17 targets were chosen for sensitivity rather than sampled at random, however, so the paper does not estimate what share of the 1,972 extensions is vulnerable.

#### Comment
- The threat model is the crux of the debate. The attacker must be a malicious app running with user privileges on the victim's machine, which browser vendors regard as game over already. The authors counter that this assumption is outdated now that macOS enforces strong app isolation and privacy protections, noting that only 4 of the 17 apps are distributed through the Mac App Store, so most run outside the sandbox.
- AI agent extensions are among the targets. Claude in Chrome was vulnerable to both host imitation and bidirectional attacks, and the authors report that an attacker could run JavaScript in logged-in tabs to take tokens and cookies. As more agent extensions drive the browser, authentication on this channel is likely to matter more.
- Venue: IEEE S&P is a top-tier security conference. The "to appear" note comes from the authors' repository README; the arXiv manuscript itself does not name the venue.

# A Rowhammer attack on Fil-C's software capabilities
> arXiv cs.PL 2610.33748 v2 (cross-listed to cs.CR; preprint, not peer reviewed). Two independent researchers (Cambridge, UK); University of Sussex and Montanarius Ltd (UK)
> 2026

Fil-C gives existing C/C++ code spatial and temporal memory safety with little more than a recompile. Every pointer carries a software capability (InvisiCap) holding lower and upper bounds, checked at every dereference. This paper shows that Fil-C's guarantee rests on an unstated hardware assumption, that DRAM faithfully preserves those capabilities, and that Rowhammer breaks it.

In Fil-C 0.679, part of each heap pointer's capability lives in a 16-byte header stored inline in ordinary DRAM just before the object, with no MAC, parity or separate tag memory protecting it. A single 0-to-1 flip in the upper-bound word widens the permitted range by 2^k bytes, and because the runtime trusts the corrupted value, no panic or signal is raised. The authors stress that they are not proposing a new way to hammer DRAM; the point is what a well-known hardware fault means for a software memory-safety defense.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33748
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.33748
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33748

### Contribution
- The first Rowhammer attack on Fil-C. The authors characterize the Fil-C 0.679 capability layout and show that its bounds checks depend on the integrity of inline metadata in ordinary DRAM.
- For reproducibility, the demonstration takes two paths. A deterministic path flips one bit out of band through /proc/pid/mem, isolating the consequence from the hardware; a physical path performs the whole chain with real Rowhammer, from templating through grooming to hammering.
- Four case studies: a bounded out-of-bounds read/write window, secret disclosure, privilege escalation by flipping an adjacent is_admin field, and leakage of another tenant's API key from a multi-tenant credential service backed by SQLite.
- Proposes a Fil-C-specific mitigation that transfers PT-Guard-style MAC protection to capabilities, which the authors describe as not a new class of defense.

### Evaluation
- Deterministic path: on a DDR3 lab machine (Intel Core i7-2630QM, 8 GiB DDR3, Linux 5.15) with the victim built under Fil-C 0.679, one flipped bit permitted out-of-bounds access in all four case studies, and the runtime reported the object's length as 384 rather than 256 bytes. In the no-flip control, the same access ended in a runtime panic. The result also held for 512-byte allocations.
- Physical path: classic double-sided hammering succeeded on DDR3 in about two minutes, and many-sided hammering succeeded on Target Row Refresh (TRR) equipped DDR4 in 10 to 35 minutes. Both machines used non-ECC memory.
- Reliability of the metrics: that a single flip removes protection is clearly established. The physical path, however, uses favorable conditions: the attacker is the parent process that forks the victim, the victim publishes its allocated bounds through a shared region, and physical addresses come from /proc/pid/pagemap. How hard it would be in a real setting to locate a victim object and hit the right bit was not evaluated. The DDR4 machine's vendor and model are withheld under a coordinated disclosure policy.

#### Comment
- Unlike hardware-enforced schemes such as CHERI or Arm MTE, software capabilities are directly exposed to hardware faults, and this paper makes that concrete. It is a reminder that the guarantees of memory-safe languages and runtimes also rest on hardware integrity.
- Limitations: the authors frame their claim conditionally, "wherever Rowhammer can land a usable bit flip" in the right part of the metadata. Most flips are unusable because of their location, polarity, current value or the need to keep a valid pointer, and servers with ECC memory may behave differently. Only Fil-C 0.679 was tested.
- Venue: preprint, with cs.PL as the primary category. Two of the authors are independent researchers without institutional affiliation.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF (v2), downloaded on the user's computer. A carried-over candidate first listed on November 3.

# Janus Vehicles: Entity-Centric Threat Modelling of Protocol-Conformant Vehicles under a Malicious-Manufacturer Assumption
> Accepted at IEEE PRDC 2026 (31st IEEE Pacific Rim International Symposium on Dependable Computing); posted as arXiv cs.CR 2610.37059 v2. Korea University (Seoul, South Korea)
> 2026

Automotive security research usually assumes the manufacturer is benign and focuses on supply-chain threats, external attacks and compromised vehicles that reveal themselves through misbehavior. This paper flips that assumption and builds a threat model for vehicles that are malicious by design: they authenticate correctly and follow every protocol, yet hide undeclared sensing, communication or context-triggered actuation. The motivation draws on real precedents such as the Volkswagen emissions scandal, where software detected test conditions and changed its behavior, and on recent investigations and restrictions in several countries involving Chinese-made vehicles and buses.

The authors distinguish protocol conformance from purpose conformance. The former is necessary for interoperability but is not enough to show that the vehicle as a whole is trustworthy. On that basis they propose an entity-centric threat model in three parts.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37059
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.37059
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.37059

### Contribution
- Defines four malign entity archetypes: vehicles that look benign to peers but serve a malicious purpose (Type I), compromised vehicles, genuine components reappropriated for other uses, and imitated vehicles or components. Type I differs from the others in leaving no evidence for either authenticity checks or behavioral monitoring.
- Points out two assurance gaps: authenticity does not establish conformance to the declared purpose, and malicious functionality can evade detection based on behavioral history or peer-visible evidence. Reappropriated genuine components also weaken signal fingerprinting and certificate-based authentication.
- Introduces a Behaviour-Locus Matrix that organizes the investigative search space by placing covert, overt and concealment actions across hardware, software and network loci.
- Adds a Behavioural Interaction Model linking how hidden capabilities are activated by triggers (a timer, an inbound command, a recognized location or target, a vehicle state), realized as actions, leave artifacts and are concealed.

### Evaluation
- There is no experimental evaluation. The authors state in their limitations that this is a conceptual threat model and has not been tested on vehicles or testbeds with seeded hidden functionality.
- The examples in the Behaviour-Locus Matrix are illustrative rather than exhaustive, and the paper does not prescribe evidence-acquisition procedures, analysis tools or decision thresholds.
- Reliability of the metrics: with no quantitative metrics to assess, the paper has to be judged on how well it relates to existing standards such as ISO/SAE 21434 and MITRE frameworks, and on the soundness of its case analysis.

#### Comment
- As connected cars and V2X spread, "can we trust the manufacturer itself?" is becoming a policy question, and this paper is a useful attempt to put it in security-engineering terms. Its conclusion is careful: the absence of observed behavior does not prove the absence of a capability, and the model cannot establish a manufacturer's intent or legal responsibility.
- Limitations: it stops at conceptual structure, so practical use needs follow-up work. The authors suggest an investigative workflow combining static examination, dynamic observation and contextual stimulation, and adapting the Malware Behavior Catalog to cyber-physical systems.
- Venue: IEEE PRDC is an IEEE conference on dependable computing. It is not a top-tier security venue but does have formal peer review. The acceptance is stated in a footnote on the first page of the arXiv manuscript.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF (v2), downloaded on the user's computer. A carried-over candidate first listed on November 3.

# What Malware Actually Does: An Empirical Analysis of Post-Infection Behavior Across Malware Families
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.03774 (preprint, not peer reviewed; the manuscript carries an IEEE copyright notice but names no venue). University of North Dakota (USA)
> 2026

Malware detection research mostly asks whether a file is malicious before it runs, and pays less attention to what it actually does after infection. This paper runs samples from 37 malware families in the open-source CAPEv2 sandbox to analyze post-infection behavior. Execution traces become heterogeneous graphs with five node types (process, API, file, registry and network) and seven edge types, preserving relational structure that flat feature vectors discard, such as which process wrote which registry key while connecting where.

From each graph the authors extract 55 structural features and classify maliciousness and family with a voting ensemble of Random Forest, Extra Trees and XGBoost, then build per-family behavioral fingerprints across registry, network, process and API activity. Finally, an LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.6) writes analyst-readable reports that map observed behavior to MITRE ATT&CK techniques. The whole pipeline is published as a web tool called HeteroScan.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03774
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03774
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.03774
- Authors' project page (HeteroScan): https://ama-andam.github.io/heteroscan

### Contribution
- Compares post-infection behavior across 37 families in a single sandbox setup, including remote access trojans (AsyncRAT, njRAT, NanoCore and others), infostealers (RedLine, AgentTesla, Vidar and others) and loaders (GuLoader, Amadey and others).
- Builds a pipeline that represents behavior as heterogeneous graphs and classifies using structural features alone.
- Reports, per family, how many samples showed no meaningful behavior in the sandbox; some families, such as Phorpiex, exited within seconds and left no usable graphs.
- Proposes evaluating the LLM explanation layer with five behavioral consistency checks.

### Evaluation
- Data: 6,835 malicious samples from MalwareBazaar and 1,212 benign samples from DikeDataset, PortableApps, GitHub releases and Windows system files were executed. After dropping samples with fewer than two unique API nodes, 2,516 graphs remained (1,793 malicious, 723 benign), split 70/15/15 with stratified random sampling.
- Malicious-versus-benign: on the 378-sample test set, the ensemble reaches 94.44% accuracy, F1 of 0.9617, MCC of 0.8628 and AUC of 0.966, with MCC 7.4 points higher than a flat behavioral-feature baseline.
- Family classification: across 24 families with at least 10 graphs each (1,774 samples), accuracy is 68.91% and macro F1 is 0.5818. NanoCore (0.974) and Vidar (0.900) are classified well, while families with few samples, such as IcedID and Netwire, score near zero.
- LLM explanations: reports for 20 samples from 20 families scored an average of 4.4 out of 5 on the five consistency checks, and 12 of 20 passed all checks.
- Reliability of the metrics: as the authors acknowledge in their limitations, the sample-level random split can put close variants of the same family in both training and test sets, which makes the results optimistic. Malicious and benign samples come from entirely different sources, so the classifier may have learned collection-source differences rather than malicious behavior. The 378-sample test set is small, and the LLM evaluation is a rule-based check by the authors on only 20 samples, so it is hard to generalize.

#### Comment
- This reads more like a technical report cataloging post-infection behavior by family than a new detection method. The per-family fingerprints and the list of families whose behavior vanishes in the sandbox are useful references when designing sandbox-based analysis.
- Limitations: there is no time-based split, and most samples are commodity malware from MalwareBazaar, leaving targeted-attack malware and non-Windows platforms largely uncovered. A short sandbox window may miss delayed behavior. The introduction opens with IoT threat statistics, but the analysis covers Windows PE files only.
- Venue: treated as a preprint. The IEEE copyright notice suggests a submission to an IEEE conference or journal, but the venue could not be confirmed.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.
