# BypassGym: A Benchmark for Evaluating AI Agents' Endpoint Defense Evasion Capabilities
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Huazhong University of Science and Technology (China) and Virginia Tech (USA)
> 2026

Benchmarks for offensive LLM agents have mostly covered penetration testing, vulnerability discovery and CTFs. Whether an agent can get past an endpoint detection and response (EDR) product that is actually installed on a machine has not been measured systematically. BypassGym gives an agent shellcode from a C2 framework (Havoc, AdaptixC2 or Sliver) and asks it to write and compile a loader that runs the payload on a real Windows 11 VM protected by an EDR without being blocked or killed. Built on Proxmox VE, the harness first checks the submission on a VM without EDR, then runs anything that passes on the EDR-protected VM and returns structured results showing which check failed. A curated knowledge base of 37 public evasion techniques, three complete loader projects and six DLL-hijacking templates is supplied as an agent skill, and its effect is measured by turning it on and off.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13684
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13684
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13684

### Contribution
- An execution-based oracle: instead of scanner or sandbox verdicts, a submission passes only if the payload runs on a real endpoint, the C2 session stays up, and command and file-transfer checks succeed.
- Fixed task conditions (payload, tools, iteration budget) with an open loader design, so the model, the agent scaffold (Codex CLI, Claude Code, OpenCode), reasoning effort, supplied knowledge and feedback detail can each be varied independently.
- A delayed replay of every passing submission one to four weeks later to catch detections that arrive later through cloud reputation.

### Evaluation
- Setup: five lightweight models (from the GPT, Gemini, GLM, DeepSeek and Qwen families) × six EDR products (Windows Defender, Kaspersky, Elastic, Trend Micro, Sophos, Bitdefender) × three payloads, 90 runs in total.
- Results: agents produced a passing loader in 71 of 90 runs (78.9%). DeepSeek-V4.1 Flash did best at 17 of 18, and Bitdefender was the hardest target. Most successes reused the same approach, DLL side-loading through a signed host.
- Delayed replay: 62 of the 71 passing submissions (87.3%) still passed one to four weeks later. Kaspersky was the outlier, catching 5 of 13 on replay (61.5% survival).
- Controlled studies: removing the knowledge skill cut DeepSeek from 17/18 to 13/18 and GLM from 15/18 to 9/18. Pass/fail feedback alone did no better than a single attempt; only detailed feedback naming the failed check raised success (DeepSeek from 14/18 to 17/18). More reasoning effort did not reliably help.
- Reliability: each condition has only around 18 runs, and the results reflect particular product snapshots in default configurations. The authors themselves warn against generalizing rankings between products.

#### Comment
- Because the lead institution is Chinese, we checked further. Haoyu Wang (HUST) and Peng Gao (Virginia Tech) are established researchers in software and systems security. The paper has no venue yet, and code and data are promised only "after the review process", so it cannot be reproduced today. The numbers are not suspiciously uniform: they vary by product and model, and failure modes are reported, so we found no obvious problem with the comparison design, but the missing artifacts should be kept in mind.
- Measuring offensive capability raises ethical questions. The authors say they disclosed results to the EDR vendors and frame the work as input for defense research and model release decisions.
- For defenders, the recurring gaps are DLL side-loading through signed hosts, execution from mapped images, and RW-to-RX memory permission transitions.

# From Attack Surface to Defense Architecture: Compositional Security for Agentic Coding Harnesses
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Evercursion (GaiaLogic), University of Macau, Allianz, Nanyang Technological University
> 2026

AI coding harnesses wire an LLM to a shell, a file system and the network. Once an attacker can plant instructions in an issue or a document, ordinary tools such as bash, curl and find become the payload, turning the harness into a Living-off-the-Land platform. The authors argue that most high-severity harness failures are classic OS and software problems (symlink races, path traversal, subprocess escape, SSRF, credential exposure) and that security is a property of the architecture and of how permissions compose, not of individual model behavior. Their reference-monitor design, ActiveSecHarness, follows one rule: intelligence may propose actions but must never define its own authority. It combines HMAC capability tokens that only narrow under delegation, a product taint lattice over trust and sensitivity, a workspace provenance ledger that stops collaborating agents from laundering secrets through disk, and deterministic guards (Ring 0).

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13960
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13960
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13960

### Contribution
- A reframing of the coding-harness attack surface as compositions of classic systems-security problems, with an argument for why regex blocklists and LLM-as-a-Judge guardrails fall short.
- A layered architecture that separates authority (capability tokens), information flow (taint lattice and provenance ledger), enforcement (deterministic guards) and verification (tool certificates, dual-key audit logs, generated security tests).
- Experimental evidence that individually harmless steps can combine across steps and agents into exfiltration paths that only stateful enforcement catches.

### Evaluation
- Data: 252 fixtures written by the authors (120 single-step attacks, 120 benign engineering tasks, 12 five-stage kill chains).
- Comparison: unguarded, regex blocklist, a local LLM judge (qwen3:4b), Ring 0, and a hybrid. Attack block rates were 0.000, 0.439, 1.000, 1.000 and 1.000; false positive rates were 0.000, 0.000, 0.983, 0.000 and 0.000. The LLM judge blocked attacks only by refusing 98.3% of benign work, at a median of 142.3 ms per decision, versus 12.7 µs for Ring 0.
- Multi-step: even an idealized stateless task policy (in the style of Conseca) missed 4 of 12 kill chains (block rate 0.667), and a coarse Boolean taint lock blocked 83.3% of benign workflows. The proposed design reached a block rate of 1.000 with no false positives.
- Reliability: the fixtures were designed by the same people who designed the defense, so the perfect scores only hold within that design space. The only LLM judge compared is a single 4B model, which the authors themselves call a small-model worst case, so the comparison is not a fair one. Utility loss on real coding-agent work (for example SWE-bench) was not measured.

#### Comment
- The core principle, enforcing security in the permission structure rather than in the model, is sound and practically useful. But the paper proposes a large number of components at once and does not test their contributions separately.
- It reads partly as a product description from the first author's company and leans heavily on the authors' own theory papers. Not peer reviewed.

# Isolation Is Not Confidentiality: Security Boundaries of Geometric Access Control in RAG
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Université Clermont Auvergne, CNRS, LIMOS (France)
> 2026

Enterprise RAG deployments often keep documents with different access policies in a single vector database. Recent proposals handle this by applying a secret geometric transformation per policy (rotations, extra key-dependent dimensions, distance-comparison-preserving encryption or DCPE) so that queries only match documents under the same policy. This paper asks what security that approach actually provides. It separates four properties, namely policy isolation, representation confidentiality, query forgery resistance and policy-membership hiding, and tests them on the original authors' public implementations. The answer: retrieval quality and isolation hold up, but the other three properties do not.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14061
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14061
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14061

### Contribution
- A formal split of the security goals of geometric access control for RAG into four distinct objectives, showing that isolation does not imply confidentiality.
- A demonstration that a curious user holding aligned pairs of original embeddings and protected representations can learn the transformation with plain least squares, then recover original embeddings or forge queries for another policy. Recovery and forgery target different objects and their costs scale differently.
- A demonstration that a curious cloud provider seeing only protected representations can still infer which documents share a policy because of policy-dependent geometry, and that the DCPE-based scheme is no exception.

### Evaluation
- Mechanisms: Trans-RAG, key-parametrized dimension augmentation, DCPE (via the IronCore SDK), and a fusion of the first two, all run from the original authors' code.
- Data and models: isolation on a MedQuAD sample with nine attribute-based policies; retrieval utility on MS MARCO and three BEIR sets (NFCorpus, SciFact, FiQA); reconstruction and forgery on a single-policy MedQuAD subset (3,500 stored embeddings, 300 queries). Encoders were MiniLM (384 dimensions), MPNet (768) and GTE-large (1024).
- Results: cross-policy retrieval was zero for every mechanism, and Recall@10 stayed within 0.01 of the unprotected system. Yet every mechanism could be inverted to a top-10 overlap of at least 0.8 with only 200 to 400 aligned pairs, and larger encoders did not raise that cost. Forgery against secret per-policy transforms got more expensive with dimension (500 pairs at 384 dimensions, 1,800 at 1024) but still succeeded; for the augmentation scheme, an attacker can estimate the shared rotation from their own policy and then needs a single pair for the target policy. Policy-membership clustering (ARI) was 1.00 in almost every case.
- Reliability: using the original code and three random seeds limits the risk of misreading the mechanisms. However, attack cost was measured on a coarse 100-pair grid for a single sample, so the authors caution against reading differences like 200 versus 300 pairs. How realistic it is for an attacker to collect hundreds of aligned pairs depends on the deployment.

#### Comment
- Rather than breaking one system, the paper maps the security boundary of the whole approach, which is useful for anyone tempted to treat embedding transforms as sufficient protection in a RAG access-control design.
- It also reports a portability issue: carrying the augmentation scheme's parameter over to a cosine-similarity store collapses retrieval (Recall@10 of 0.02).
- Preprint from French university researchers.
