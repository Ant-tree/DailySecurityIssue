# Voluntary Servitude: Weaponized Safety Warning Mechanisms for Controlling AI Agents
> Manuscript in NeurIPS 2026 format (40th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems), posted as arXiv cs.CR 2611.04393. Technion (Israel), Zenity
> 2026

LLM agents pick hotels and even make bookings based on reviews and posts they read on the web. Attacks along this path have so far been modeled as indirect prompt injection, where a command is planted in retrieved content, and deployed detectors are trained to spot that command syntax. This paper shows that an agent can be steered without any command at all. Plant a single ordinary-looking review that fabricates a hazard about competitors, such as food poisoning or missing safety equipment, and the agent, following its own safety-aligned judgment, drops the legitimate options and picks the attacker's listing. The authors call this "voluntary servitude."

The attacks fall into three families: a safety family that fabricates hazards, an authority family that impersonates credible sources, and an attribute family that disparages features. They are compared with instruction injection in the same slot and with the same review count, against controls that make an ethical accusation only, that merely praise the attacker's listing, or that leave the evidence untouched.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04393
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.04393
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04393

### Contribution
- Defines a new attack surface in which manipulated evidence, with no command, changes an agent's choice. The agent's harm-avoidance, the very behavior safety alignment aims to instill, becomes the attack vector.
- Shows that these payloads work about as well as instruction injection while passing deployed injection detectors almost untouched.
- Confirms that the attack transfers to production browser agents and to a retrieval pipeline.
- Proposes a defense that needs no retraining: a corroboration requirement in the agent's instructions, asking for independent support before a provider is discarded on safety grounds.

### Evaluation
- Setup: six models (Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, DeepSeek V4 Flash, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, Llama 3.3 70B, Grok 4.3 and Claude Haiku 4.5) across ten consumer-service domains such as lodging, taxis and tours. All generate at temperature 0, and Llama 3.3 70B serves as the judge.
- Generation-stage success: 60.4% for the safety family versus 66.0% for instruction injection. Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite was the most susceptible (86.5%); Claude Haiku 4.5 was the most resistant but still fell to the safety family 22.5% of the time.
- Detectors: the ProtectAI classifier and Lakera Guard flagged 46% to 84% of the instruction-injection payloads but at most 22% of the safety payloads. Behind a detector, the safety family was 1.7 to 4.4 times as effective as instruction injection. The payloads were never tuned against either detector.
- Production browser agents: a poisoned review page on an attacker-controlled domain was read 20 times per attack by Edge with Copilot, Perplexity Comet, Brave's assistant and OpenAI's Codex browser. Edge chose the attacker's listing every time (20% for instruction injection), Comet 60% of the time (0%) and Codex 20% (0%); Brave fell to both attacks every time.
- Retrieval: when the review passes through a retriever over mostly clean pages, end-to-end success ranges from 1.0% to 57.0% depending on the retriever.
- Defense: adding the corroboration requirement cut success from 68.4% to 3.6% (95% CI 2.2% to 5.7%), with an effect on every model. The agent still reads the planted hazard but no longer acts on it alone.
- Reliability of the metrics: controlled experiments with proper controls are kept separate from product tests, which helps distinguish cause from generalization. The browser-agent test uses only 20 trials per product, the defense was measured against a non-adaptive attacker, and using an LLM as judge introduces some error.

#### Comment
- The paper shows that today's prompt-injection defenses, which look for commands, are close to useless against persuasive content. Anyone designing agents that act on reviews or reputation data should consider rules that require independent corroboration, especially for safety claims.
- Limitations: as the authors note, only single selections among travel services were studied; dependency selection and multi-step sessions remain open. The corroboration rule also delays genuine safety warnings until they are corroborated, and an attacker who fabricates corroboration was not evaluated.
- Venue: NeurIPS is a top-tier machine learning conference; this is based on the NeurIPS 2026 notice on the manuscript's first page. Disclosure and release are described in an appendix.

# The Devil is in the Context: Nine Dimensions of Prompt Injection Attacks
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.04672 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Microsoft Security Response Center, University of New South Wales (Australia), ETH Zurich (Switzerland)
> 2026

Prompt injection is usually discussed and evaluated as one homogeneous kind of attack. Vulnerabilities reported in real systems, however, vary widely, and the same instruction can be an attack in one context and a legitimate request in another, depending on the user's intent and the system's policies. This paper, led by researchers at the Microsoft Security Response Center (MSRC), starts from the concern that ignoring this variation leaves gaps in how defenses are evaluated and can give a false sense of security.

The authors propose a taxonomy that classifies indirect prompt injection along nine dimensions: the source's trust label, whether the instruction arrives in fragments, whether it is visible to people, whether it needs interpretation, whether it is conditional, whether it tries to modify agent state such as memory, whether it relates to the user's task, whether it changes the actions themselves or only their arguments and outputs, and whether the original task is still completed. Each dimension has two categories, and the key point is that each one affects which defenses can detect or prevent the attack.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04672
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.04672
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04672

### Contribution
- Builds a nine-dimension taxonomy that describes the injected instruction itself, its relation to the user's task and how it manifests in the target system, and explains how each dimension bears on defense coverage.
- Classifies six real-world prompt-injection vulnerabilities, including the GitHub MCP repository exfiltration case, showing how much they differ.
- Analyzes nine prompt-injection benchmarks (AgentDojo, IPIArena, LLMail-Inject, WASP and others) and finds that most cover only a small region of the space.
- Provides an automated annotator combining rules with an LLM judge, and a method for generating test cases with a requested combination of categories.

### Evaluation
- Benchmark coverage: even with all benchmarks combined, 13% of pairwise category combinations are never tested, mostly among rarely represented categories. Benchmark size does not imply broad coverage.
- Automated annotation: compared against human reference labels on four AgentDojo suites, IPIArena and Trojan Hippo. Dimensions decided by rules (fragmentation, instruction interpretability) agree fully in every configuration; models differ on semantic dimensions such as topic relation and original-task completion. The cheaper GPT-5.6-Luna is about as accurate as GPT-5.6-Terra.
- Targeted case generation: from 40 AgentDojo Workspace cases, 87.5% of generated variants were accepted on the first attempt and all within three. On the original cases, GPT-5.6-Luna showed 0% attack success and 100% utility under attack, but on the variants attack success was 17.5% without defense, 2.5% with CaMeL, 20% with MELON, 7.5% with a DeBERTa detector, 7.5% with PromptArmor and 17.5% with Meta SecAlign-70B. Utility separated them: 40% for DeBERTa, 90% for PromptArmor and 27.5% for CaMeL.
- Reliability of the metrics: the taxonomy is a qualitative contribution, so quantitative evaluation is limited. The generation experiment uses 40 cases and one model, and is best read as an illustration that variants expose differences the original cases do not.

#### Comment
- The taxonomy can serve as a shared vocabulary for defense papers to state which categories of prompt injection they were evaluated against. System operators can also use it to pick the attack categories that apply to them and combine defenses to match.
- The command-free manipulation in the Voluntary Servitude paper published today relates to the instruction-interpretability dimension here, so the two papers are worth reading together.
- Limitations: some dimensions require knowledge of user intent and system policy, which limits automation. The six real-world cases were chosen to show distinct properties, not as a comprehensive survey.
- Venue: preprint. Most authors are at the Microsoft Security Response Center, and the work reflects real vulnerability-response experience.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer. Carried over as a candidate on November 5.

# Averaging Collusion Breaks Traceability in Personalized Neural Audio Watermarking
> arXiv cs.SD 2611.04400 (cross-listed to cs.CR; preprint, not peer reviewed). Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School (China)
> 2026

Personalized audio watermarking embeds a different user-specific identifier into each copy of the same audio, so that a leaked copy can be traced back to the user it was issued to. It is also used to trace AI-generated speech. Existing evaluations assume the attacker holds a single copy, but in practice several copies of the same recording can reach different people. This paper shows that when two or more people collude and simply average their copies sample by sample, tracing collapses.

Across five systems, three bit-based (AudioSeal, WavMark and TimbreWM) and two latent-based (VoiceMark and WMCodec), averaging just two copies produced tracing-failure rates of 87.7% to 99.7% while largely preserving audio quality. An attacker who knows the payload structure can go further and tune the mixing weights so that the decoder blames a selected user who was not part of the coalition.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04400
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.04400
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04400
- Authors' code and results repository: https://github.com/Silwan5z/neural-audio-watermark-collusion-attack
- Authors' project page (audio examples): https://silwan5z.github.io/neural-audio-watermark-collusion-attack/

### Contribution
- Raises multi-copy collusion as an evaluation dimension for personalized neural audio watermarking, separate from conventional single-copy robustness.
- Analyzes how each system's decoder responds to the bit composition of a coalition. TimbreWM follows the majority almost exactly, while latent-based systems perturb even the bits the copies agree on and sometimes return an identifier that matches neither copy.
- Presents a targeted misattribution attack that pins the result on a chosen non-member.
- Evaluates a preliminary defense that uses decoder-confidence statistics to reject collusion outputs.

### Evaluation
- Data: 300 ten-second recordings from 100 speakers (three each) drawn from AISHELL-3 and LibriSpeech. Coalition sizes are 2, 3, 5 and 8.
- Uniform averaging: with only two copies, tracing failure is 87.7% to 99.7% across all five systems, while PESQ and STOI show limited quality loss. TimbreWM follows the majority faithfully yet still fails to trace 92.0% of the time with eight colluders.
- Robustness: shifting one copy by 10 to 50 ms, or compressing copies with MP3 (128 kbps) or Opus (64 kbps) before averaging, keeps tracing failure at 96.5% to 96.9%, with PESQ above 4.25 and STOI above 0.989.
- Targeted misattribution: an attacker who knows the bit structure and optimizes the mixing weights gets TimbreWM to attribute the output to a chosen non-member 92.3% of the time.
- Defense: a screening rule on minimum confidence, mean confidence and confidence variance is calibrated to keep at least 95% of valid single-copy outputs and evaluated with five-fold cross-validation over speakers. The authors report that it rejects many collusion outputs and present this as a preliminary result.
- Reliability of the metrics: five public systems are evaluated at their native sampling rates with standard quality metrics, and results that differ by system are reported openly. PESQ and STOI measure similarity to the first copy, however, which may differ from perceived quality.

#### Comment
- With a very simple attack, the paper shows that any speech-generation service planning to trace leaks through per-user watermarks must account for multiple copies of the same source circulating. Collusion, long studied in digital fingerprinting, reappears in neural watermarking.
- Limitations: this is a short four-page manuscript, the defense is preliminary and not tested against an adaptive attacker, and the assumption that colluding copies are time-aligned is fairly strong (offsets tested only up to 50 ms).
- Venue: preprint, with cs.SD as the primary category.
- Verification of a Chinese-institution paper: all authors are at Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School. There is no venue yet, but code, result data, reproduction steps (REPRODUCIBILITY.md) and audio examples are public on GitHub, and the repository was cloned to check its contents. The systems evaluated are existing public watermarking systems, so the comparison is appropriate, and unfavorable per-system results are reported, with no sign of inflated numbers. On that basis the paper is included.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer. The authors' repository has no paper PDF. Carried over as a candidate on November 5.
