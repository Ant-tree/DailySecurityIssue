# Stealing Exact Training Data from Other Clients during Federated Learning
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Birla Institute of Technology and Science (BITS), Goa; MATS Research
> 2026

When organizations fine-tune an LLM with federated learning, each one shares only gradients, and FedAvg's averaging across clients is often treated as privacy protection in itself. Existing text gradient-inversion attacks assume a server that sees each client's gradient, and even the strongest, DAGER, hits a wall once the number of tokens in the gradient approaches the model's hidden dimension d. The authors consider an honest-but-curious client that derives the aggregate gradient from the broadcast parameters and subtracts its own share, leaving the average of everyone else's. From this residual they extract the bag of tokens, generate candidate sentences with gradient-guided beam search, accept only those whose gradients fit the residual at a consistent scale, and then "peel" the accepted sentences' gradients out of the aggregate, exposing sentences that were hidden before.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24689
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.24689
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.24689
- Author code repository (implementation and experiment configurations, listed in the paper): https://github.com/DaSH-Lab-CSIS/client-stealing

### Contribution
- The first formulation of exact text recovery by a participating client, rather than the server, above the wall where the aggregated token count C exceeds the hidden dimension d.
- For parallel-residual models, a least-squares solve on first-MLP-layer gradients yields a "token direction" encoding what followed each token, and the unembedding-row gradient is shown to sum the hidden states of the prefixes that preceded each token; both feed the beam-search score.
- A verification step accepts a candidate only if two per-token gradient pairs match the residual at the same positive scale, with at least three surviving anchors, consistent scales and an anchor near the sentence end, aiming for 100% precision.
- Peeling subtracts each verified sentence's gradient, weighted by its estimated scale, from the original residual and repeats until no new sentence is accepted.

### Evaluation
- Experiments on Pythia-160M (d=768), Pythia-410M (d=1024) and GPT-J-6B (d=4096) with SST-2, Enron emails and PubMed text, from 256 to 65,536 aggregated tokens, against DAGER, FILM and SOMP. None of the three baselines recovered a single exact sentence in any configuration.
- In the default setting (Pythia-160M, SST-2, 4 clients, batch 32, length 16, 2,048 tokens) it recovered 100 of 128 sentences (78.1%) at precision 1.00. The first pass found 51, and seven further peeling passes added 49, a 1.96x gain. The abstract reports up to 80.2% recovery with 4x more tokens than the hidden dimension.
- Gradient clipping and 20 FedAvg combinations of local steps and learning rates left recovery unchanged, while adding Gaussian noise to the gradient dropped recovery to zero at every nonzero noise level tested. FedAdam also gave zero recovery.
- With larger aggregates, recovery approaches zero, and on Enron at length 96 precision fell to 66.7%. On sequential-residual models with tied embeddings such as GPT-2, a reduced attack was exact for only 38 of 57 accepted candidates (66.7% precision).
- Reliability: three public datasets, three models and three seeds (medians reported) give a concrete scope, and the baselines are recent attacks on the same problem. However, SOMP was reimplemented because no author code was available and FILM was run without its memorization advantage, which may understate the baselines. The attack covers a single round in simulation, not a deployed federated system.

#### Comment
- The paper backs "aggregation alone is not a privacy guarantee in federated learning" with exact sentence recovery, showing Enron examples with names, addresses, phone numbers and medical details reproduced verbatim.
- The full attack is limited to parallel-residual models such as Pythia and GPT-J, and it fails against differential-privacy-style noise and FedAdam. Because the attack uses only the model update broadcast to every participant, secure aggregation does not stop it; for practitioners, it is an argument for making differential-privacy noise the default in federated fine-tuning.
- The paper lists the code repository URL, which matches the repository found by search.

# Signed but Unbound: The Authority Gap Between Agent Commerce Mandates and Crypto Wallet Signatures
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). University of Notre Dame
> 2026

When an AI agent pays in stablecoins, commerce protocols such as Google's AP2 and Coinbase's x402 describe a mandate that fixes the merchant, order, amount and the facilitator that submits the payment, but the only on-chain enforcer is the token contract, and it checks only the fields the wallet signed. The authors call this difference the authority gap and define nine violation classes: payee, overdraw, total, late, redeemer, order, chain, residual authority and revocation. For 12 configurations, including ERC-20 approve, ERC-2612 permit, EIP-3009, Permit2 and Coinbase's Commerce Payments Protocol (CPP), they derive which classes are bound and check the derivation against bytecode fetched from Base. They then measure USDC authorizations redeemed on Base and propose MandateLock, a minimal recipient contract that puts a hash of the mandate terms in the signed nonce, checks the caller and the time window, and forwards the exact amount to the merchant.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25821
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25821
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25821
- Author code repository (anonymous reviewer repository): https://anonymous.4open.science/r/PaperA-Code-Policy-Gate-1752/

### Contribution
- Models the trust boundary between the commerce protocol and the wallet as the difference between the on-chain histories a mandate allows and those any holder of the signature can cause, and compares 12 configurations across nine violation classes in one table.
- Separates the mechanisms: committing the mandate terms in the signer-chosen EIP-3009 nonce closes order substitution with no contract at all, while binding the redeemer needs receiveWithAuthorization, whose caller must be the signed recipient. It follows, and the paper proves, that facilitator settlement to a merchant account without code requires a separate recipient contract, which the wallet then shows as the payee.
- States four conditions under which a recipient contract enforces exactly the signed mandate and gives a game-based proof. This explains the pattern CPP already deploys without a published analysis, and MandateLock is a minimal instance for immediate payments.

### Evaluation
- Running USDC, Permit2 and CPP v1.1 bytecode from block 58,495,000 in a local Foundry harness, the authors executed 106 of 108 cells with 300 random mandates each, 31,800 trials in all, and every trial matched the derivation. All 3,600 honest settlements were exact, and changing only the nonce to a terms hash cut successful order attacks from 300 to 0.
- Sampling 60 windows of 100 blocks between 4 May and 16 November 2026 yielded 26,653 redeemed USDC authorizations. 98.7% went through a path any signature holder could redeem; 97.8% of decoded ones came from 14 registered x402 facilitators, 92.5% of those from Coinbase. The median payment was $0.0055, and CPP accounted for 0.5%.
- The median extra time a holder could have delayed settlement was 296 seconds, and 0.6% of authorizations allowed more than 30 days. Within equal 20-second slices, 40.1% of authorizations had another with the same payer, payee and amount and an overlapping validity window, which a merchant checking only payee and amount cannot tell apart.
- MandateLock rejected all 10,800 attempts with one mandate field changed, passed 10,000 fuzzing runs, and accepted signatures from two independent signers (Foundry cast and eth-account, 50 of 50 each). It costs 98,084 gas, 25% more than a payee-bound transfer (78,588) and less than CPP (151,358), or $0.00090 per settlement.
- Reliability: because outcomes never vary within a cell, the 31,800 trials amount to 106 conformance checks, as the authors acknowledge, and the same authors wrote the derivation, the attacks and the oracle. The mainnet measurement shows exposure, not observed preemption or misattribution; the two Permit2 proxies are the authors' own implementations; and the wallet display test covers one MetaMask version (13.33.0).

#### Comment
- The paper argues that the wallet signature, not the mandate credential, is the real enforcement boundary, and offers a fix that needs no token changes and uses signature formats wallets already support. Its scope is limited to immediate single payments of an exact amount from code-less EOA payers; capped variable amounts, recurring payments, refunds and smart accounts are out of scope. The authors state they found no new vulnerability in any specific deployment.
- A recipient contract costs payee visibility, since the wallet shows the contract rather than the merchant, and the design rests on a trusted signer that displays the mandate and computes its hash.
- 2611.24989, by the same four Notre Dame authors, addresses nearly the same boundary, and both point out that an EIP-2612 permit deadline bounds only when the allowance is created. This paper narrows to exact single payments but adds Base mainnet measurement, order substitution and redeemer binding, and an on-chain recipient contract with a proof. 2611.24989 is a pre-signing wallet checker covering 13 object families, including payee sets, cumulative caps, ERC-7710 delegations and EIP-7702, so it is broader in objects and mandate shape, while deployment measurement appears only here. Neither paper cites the other.
- The anonymous reviewer repository is said to include MandateLock, the measurement inputs and pinned bytecode, but the validation matrix, mutation sweep and gas benchmarks are supplied only as recorded results.

# Signed, Valid, and Unauthorized: Authority Closure at the Trust Boundary Between Agent Commerce Protocols and Crypto Wallets
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). University of Notre Dame
> 2026

When a shopping agent pays in stablecoins, the buyer approves a payee, asset, spending cap and time window, but what the wallet's signed object actually permits is decided by the contract that consumes it. An EIP-2612 permit naming the merchant as spender, exactly 25 USDC and Friday as its deadline shows all the right fields, yet the deadline only bounds when the permit can be submitted, so the resulting allowance can be spent to any address long after the mandate expires. The authors define the authority closure of a signed object as every transfer sequence an adversary holding it can cause, and say the object respects the mandate only if that whole set lies inside what the mandate allows. They show that field matching, verification against merchant-supplied requirements and single-run simulation do not guarantee this, and prove that only objects that name the payee, cap the total and bound the execution time can satisfy it. Building on this, Closure is a wallet-side checker that computes the footprint of 13 families of signed objects and compares it with the mandate before signing.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24989
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.24989
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.24989

### Contribution
- A security definition that asks whether every future use of a signed object stays within the mandate rather than whether its fields match, with propositions showing that three checks in use today do not imply it.
- A classification (Theorem 2, "name, cap, expire"): under the strict definition, ERC-20 approvals, EIP-2612 permits, Permit2 allowances and Permit2 signature transfers without a witness can never satisfy a payee-bound mandate, whatever their fields, while ERC-3009 authorizations, x402 Permit2 witness transfers and ERC-7710 delegations with an amount, recipient pin and time bound can, depending on their fields.
- A footprint calculus with a soundness theorem that handles spender-dependent Permit2 witness semantics, composed ERC-7710 caveats and EIP-7702 code authorizations, implemented as Closure in about 230 lines of dependency-free Python. Unknown spender code or caveats get no credit, so it fails closed.

### Evaluation
- A Foundry oracle built from upstream Permit2, the x402 Exact and Upto proxies, and the MetaMask DelegationManager with seven caveat enforcers ran 62,979 adversary programs (42,600 randomized) against 2,130 generated objects in 13 families and 25 classes. Execution showed 1,680 objects to be unauthorized and 450 authorized.
- Every baseline admitted unauthorized objects. Simulation accepted 840 of 1,680, simulation with residual-allowance checks 630, field matching 570 and field matching with expiry 295; a Safe-style policy guard accepted 646 and wrongly rejected 108 authorized ones. x402 verification accepted 120 of 780 with honest requirements and 690 with merchant-written ones.
- 265 unauthorized objects passed both field matching with expiry and the policy guard; 90 of them were permits and Permit2 objects whose displayed payee, amount, token, chain and expiry all matched the mandate. In one traced case, 285.09 USDC moved to the adversary 30 days after the mandate expired.
- Closure accepted none of the 1,680 unauthorized objects and rejected none of the 450 authorized ones, and the violated dimensions it reported matched execution in every case. An independent invariant fuzzer found violations for 49 of 56 unauthorized objects in its sample, and 21 of 22 mutants that weaken one Closure rule were detected. Median decision time was 3 to 12 microseconds per family, and in two local checkouts with the x402 TypeScript client (v2.31.0), approved payments settled while out-of-mandate requests were refused before signing.
- Reliability: the objects are generated from fault classes the authors chose, not collected from deployments, and how often each fault occurs is not measured. The same authors wrote the checker and the adversary library, partly offset by the fuzzer and mutation analysis; offline compilation required a substitute token, relaxed compiler pins and shims; and the zero-error result holds under a test model that assumes immediate execution of broadcast transactions.

#### Comment
- The recommendations are concrete: wallets should render authority rather than fields ("anyone holding this can send up to 25 USDC to any address, forever"), and an ERC-7715 permission for agent payments needs all three caveats, an amount bound, a recipient pin with the method restricted to transfer, and a time bound.
- Closure depends on a registry of trusted contract code and on a trusted translation from merchant identifiers and fiat amounts to addresses and base units. Real-world exposure is not measured.
- 2611.25821, by the same four Notre Dame authors, studies the same boundary, and both flag the EIP-2612 permit deadline problem. This paper is a pre-signing wallet check over 13 families, including payee sets, cumulative caps, delegations and EIP-7702, so it is broader in objects and mandate shape. 2611.25821 narrows to exact single payments but adds Base mainnet measurement, order substitution and redeemer binding, and an on-chain recipient contract (MandateLock) with a proof. Neither paper cites the other.
- No public code URL appears in the paper. The authors say Closure, the comparison checks, offline tests and checkout reproductions are maintained separately, that access to adversarial replay sequences and raw signing payloads is restricted because they could be misused, and that they will send their implications to the AP2, x402 and delegation-framework maintainers before publication.

# GEOHunter: Mapping a Generative Engine Optimization Network in the Wild
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), Georgia Institute of Technology
> 2026

AI assistants with web search build their answers from the pages they retrieve, so whoever shapes those pages shapes the answer. Generative engine optimization (GEO) vendors sell exactly that, publishing paid promotional articles on user-generated-content (UGC) platforms. The authors ordered two articles for a fictitious company from a real GEO vendor, then built GEOHunter to start from the accounts that published them, follow client names through platform search, and accept accounts that show coordinated posting, mapping one network across three Chinese platforms: Bilibili, Baijiahao and 360kuai. A second tool, GEOProbe, asks nine US and Chinese assistants the buyer questions the network targets and traces every cited page back to it. The authors claim this is the first study to connect a production network observed in the wild to the answers assistants give.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26564
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.26564
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26564

### Contribution
- A crawl that exploits two facts: every placement must name its client, and ordinary writers rarely post about the same niche brand on several accounts on the same day. Accounts are accepted on coordination signals: several clients, titles copied from another account, or same-day bursts.
- Maps 3,755 network accounts on three platforms and analyzes 466,357 placements from the 1,985 accounts whose feeds were collected. 96% of niche placements promote ordinary consumer goods and services, not the gambling or counterfeit goods typical of search spam.
- Shows that listicles, articles ranking products in a category, rose from 16% to 57% of placements on core Bilibili accounts between 2024 and 2025, driven mostly by newly recruited accounts. The median account has 9 followers.
- Attributes cited pages to the network at strict, extended and broad levels, bracketing exposure between a lower and an upper bound.

### Evaluation
- Of 1,580 retrievable pages cited for nine category questions, 4.5% were written by a network account, 7.2% are attributable at the extended level and 20% at the broad level. Baidu's Wenxin cited the network most (18%, 22%, 38%) and DeepSeek least (5% broad).
- In a census of 14 clients with sustained campaigns, 52% of 334 answers from eight assistants named the client, against 68% for the category leader used as control. Clustered by question, the difference is not significant (Wilcoxon p = 0.15).
- For one bicycle brand, 188 accounts published 1,185 placements, and 51 of the 98 references behind Wenxin's answers crowning it "king" carried the brand or the hierarchy in their titles. Asked which GEO vendor is best, 21 of 25 answers named a vendor the network promotes.
- The membership rule reached 92.3% precision and 92.3% recall on 72 labeled accounts, and Chao2 estimates put crawl coverage at 66% on Bilibili, 74% on Baijiahao and 90% on 360kuai.
- Reliability: membership was validated by one annotator on Bilibili only, and with just 7 organic accounts the authors say the rule's ability to exclude organic accounts cannot be estimated (placement-label agreement κ = 0.52). The probes cover only 10 pilot and 14 census questions from a single US vantage point, four pilot controls were chosen after the first round, and Doubao could not be reached. All engine-side results are associations, not causal effects.

#### Comment
- What sets the paper apart is measuring a live operation end to end rather than a lab GEO attack. After a March 2026 national TV exposé, ranking titles fell from 50% of core Bilibili placements in December 2025 to 18% in September 2026 in favor of buyer-question titles, which supports the conclusion that defenses should look at coordination between accounts rather than at single articles.
- Client naming was unrelated to placement volume and varied sharply by category, which also suggests the service delivers less reliably than vendors advertise.
- The paper studies Chinese platforms, but the authors are at US institutions. No venue is stated, and the running header reads "Anonymous et al.", suggesting a submission copy. The authors say they will release both tools and a pseudonymized dataset, but no URL is given.

# From FIPS to FPGA with LLM Agents: Measuring What Tests and Reviews Miss in Post-Quantum Cryptographic Hardware
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Lucid Motors (USA), EYL Inc. (Republic of Korea)
> 2026

When LLM agents write RTL along with the tests and reviews that validate it, passing tests and approving reviews can share the same blind spots, and a known-answer test (KAT) cannot show that malformed input is refused or that a secret stays inside the device. The authors build a seven-stage verification-gated workflow in which a gate only counts once it has been shown to fail on negative controls and fault mutants, and reviewer agents audit each unit before commit. They used it to port an ML-KEM-768/ML-DSA-65 accelerator from a Kintex-7 card to a 300 MHz Zynq UltraScale+ key-custody card. They then audited that evidence separately with tool-selected netlist mutants, replays of known flaws, and formal checks.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25339
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25339
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25339

### Contribution
- A seven-stage workflow from standard to board conformance: an executable oracle is fixed first, every claim becomes a gate that can fail, and a unit has to climb from simulation through conformance vectors to the board before deployment.
- Points out that agent-written mutants only prove a gate can fail, not what it misses, and audits the gates with mutants made by flipping one random netlist bit with Yosys mutate.
- Pre-registered review replays showing that review scope (one module versus the whole design) decides whether LLM review finds flaws, and that key-custody invariants need formal checks.

### Evaluation
- All six operations are byte-exact on the board, and the design passes all 85 NIST ACVP cases its interface can express (of 285) plus 484 ML-DSA known answers. Of 129 logged units, 126 (97.7%) were accepted, with 19.0 hours of recorded operator time over 15 weeks.
- The existing simulation gates of three RTL modules killed only 76% of 399 tool mutants. New tests written from the specification, guided by the survivors, raised decapsulation's kill rate on a fresh mutant draw from 48% to 74%.
- Review pointed at the flawed module found all four known flaws in all 54 sessions, but a plain whole-design review found the two signature-verifier flaws in only 3 of 6 and 1 of 6 sessions.
- Planted one-line flaws were found in all 20 verifier sessions but only 3 of 20 key-custody sessions, while formal checks (one added for the replay) flagged every one.
- Reliability: one operator, one model family and one design family, with no human-only baseline, so no speed-up is claimed. Logs were written by agents that knew the outcome, pre-registration was done in the authors' own repository rather than a registry, replay judges share the reviewers' model, and the planted flaws are artificial.

#### Comment
- What stands out is that it puts numbers, on a real board port, on how much "tests pass" and "review approved" can overstate trust in agent-built hardware. The conclusions are concrete: author-independent mutants for test gates, module-level scope for LLM review, and formal checks for custody invariants.
- The threat model is limited to hostile host software with register access; physical access, side channels and fault injection are out of scope, and no masking or constant-time claims are made.
- No code or repository address appears in the text. The authors state that Claude-model agents drafted the text, tables and statistics scripts and that the authors revised all of it.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).
