# Watermark Basins: Black-Box Detection of Distilled LLMs
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed). FAIR, Meta Superintelligence Labs
> 2026

Distillation, where you collect another language model's answers and fine-tune your own model on them, is the cheapest way to copy a commercial model's abilities. If the provider has watermarked its outputs, can it tell whether a suspect model was trained on those outputs just by querying it (black-box access)? The existing black-box approach (Sander et al., 2024) counts how often watermark-favored tokens show up in the suspect's outputs. The authors argue this breaks down easily when the context window grows or when the teacher samples at low temperature.

The paper's key observation is that watermarked training data leaves a key-specific direction in parameter space, a "watermark basin," that is nearly orthogonal to the ordinary task fine-tuning direction. The proposed detector, WM-Basin, feeds the suspect's outputs and keyed teacher outputs through a fixed, clean probe model to obtain gradients, projects out the top 100 directions of the clean model, and ranks the claimed key against decoy keys to compute a p-value. It never touches the suspect model's parameters.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37266

### Contribution
- Introduces the "watermark basin" phenomenon: the watermark's trace lives in a direction separate from the task-learning direction. The authors argue that post-training steps such as quantization, pruning, supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning mostly move weights along the task axis, so the trace is hard to wipe out.
- Designs a rank-based test that works from the suspect's text alone while keeping exact finite-sample control of the false positive rate.
- Evaluates across 24 student architectures (135M to 8.2B parameters), 4 teacher models and 4 watermarking schemes.

### Evaluation
- Default setup: the teacher is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B watermarked with TextSeal; the student is Qwen2.5-3B fine-tuned on 1,900 quality-filtered reasoning traces from OpenR1-Math-220k; the probe is a clean Qwen2.5-3B base model. The test uses 1,000 keys (the claimed key plus 999 decoys). Student utility is checked on GSM8K and Math-500.
- Metrics are detection rates at 0.1%, 1% and 5% false positive rates, evaluated separately for watermark n-gram windows of 1 to 3.
- Across 1,200 students distilled from that teacher, at a 0.1% false positive rate, WM-Basin detects 79% versus 29% for the prior method with n-gram 1, and 45% versus 1.3% with n-gram 3. As the window grows, the prior method essentially stops working while WM-Basin still catches close to half.
- The only baseline is Sander et al. (2024), which the authors implemented themselves and strengthened with a filter built from a separate set of 5,000 traces.
- How far the numbers can be trusted: the authors note that certifying a 0.1% false positive rate requires at least 999 decoy keys. The test's validity rests on the watermark leaving the output distribution unchanged and on decoys coming from the same key generator (exchangeability), so it is not a guarantee against every possible adversary. The text says each suspect is read on 1,900 prompts with the first 512 tokens scored, "around 11M tokens per suspect," but that works out to about 0.97M tokens, so the figure looks like a typo.

#### Comment
- The novel move is reframing distillation detection as a question of "which direction training left behind" rather than "what the output statistics look like." For model providers, this could become a way to gather evidence against competitors or unauthorized users.
- Limitations: the authors acknowledge that, as a black-box method, WM-Basin only sees the suspect's text, and further training on clean data can weaken detection. In the portion of the paper we checked, there is no evaluation against an adaptive attacker who knows the detector and tries to evade it; the robustness tests are non-adaptive changes such as compression, pruning, reinforcement learning and continued training. We also found no code or data release link.
- The headline numbers come from a single combination of one teacher, one dataset and one watermarking scheme. The tables for other teachers and schemes, and the appendices, were outside what we could verify. This summary is based on reading roughly the first 40% of the HTML full text verbatim, with the rest seen only in summarized form.
- Venue: not yet peer reviewed. The authors are the Meta group that has been publishing steadily on watermarking (Sander, Fernandez and others).
