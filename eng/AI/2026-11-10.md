# Conflux: Preventing Privilege Escalation with Principal Contexts and System-Level Evaluation
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.08768 (preprint, not peer reviewed). University of Oxford (UK)
> 2026

This paper argues that the security goal for LLM agents should shift from detecting prompt injection to preventing privilege escalation. Any single agent action is shaped not only by the user who asked for it but also by the documents, tool outputs and other people's data that flowed into it. The authors define privilege escalation as an action where at least one principal that influenced it lacks permission for that action, and present Conflux, a defense that enforces this rule outside the model's judgement.

Its kernel, ITES, follows provenance records to find the set of principals that influenced an action (the decision context), then admits the action only within the intersection of those principals' permissions under the organization's existing access-control system. If Ava asks the agent to update a customer record based on a report Ben wrote, the update goes through only if both Ava and Ben are allowed to make it. The model can choose what to propose, but it can never widen authority. Conflux layers argument-level policy, disclosure, consent, delegation, restriction and exposure planning (choosing an order of reads and actions that keeps utility without widening authority) on top of the kernel. The paper also introduces two evaluators: SLED, a black-box tool that exhaustively explores a declared finite model of a defense up to a bound, and SLED-V, a white-box tool that checks a serializable intermediate representation with solvers.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08768
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08768
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.08768
- Author code repository: https://github.com/RayaBuckley/Conflux

### Contribution
- A single formal vocabulary for model-level defenses, system-level defenses and evaluators, spelling out what each can and cannot guarantee. Prompt injection becomes just one route by which an unauthorized principal enters the decision context, so the defense never has to classify inputs as malicious.
- A conditional authority-confinement theorem: under complete mediation, no provenance laundering, a known non-empty decision context, fresh permission checks and action binding, the kernel admits no action that escalates privilege with respect to any recorded influencing principal.
- Unlike CaMeL, which fixes control flow from the trusted user query, ITES lets data shape arguments freely and constrains only the authority of the resulting action.

### Evaluation
- The quantitative evidence is very thin, and the authors say so. The AgentDojo runs in the appendix expose only read tools, so no effect-bearing call ever executed. In the 14B-model runs (132 cells), all 66 attacked cells recorded no violation, but only because nothing was attempted; this says nothing about defense efficacy. The 0.5B-model runs (204 cells) had zero utility.
- SLED results are bounded statements about declared finite models. With future-code expansion off, all 12 future-dependent tasks are conservatively flagged as potential violations; with it on, 7 are safe and 5 expose the unsafe branch of a deliberately buggy nested defense. Raising the final batch cap from three to six actions on five configurations exhausted the state space with no violation.
- Reliability of the metrics: the numbers are reported honestly, but they cannot tell us how well the defense works, or how usable it is, in a real deployment.

#### Comment
- A design paper that frames agent authorization as an intersection over everyone who influenced an action. The authors themselves note that the rule is not a new access-control model; the contribution is applying the organization's existing access control across all influencing principals.
- Limitations: the theorem covers value influence only. Control influence, where data changes which tool is called, which branch is taken or who the recipient is, is not yet represented in the provenance model. Delegation is specified but not enabled at runtime. Conservative provenance tracking can deny legitimate tasks, and the paper makes no utility claim.
- The public repository holds code and evaluation material, but the PDF in its paper/ folder is the unmodified ICLR template, so it was not used as the full text.

# Authorization Drift in LLM Agents: Evaluating Boundary Compliance in Multi-Step Tool Use
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.09113 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST), South China University of Technology, Suzhou University, Zhejiang University (China)
> 2026

LLM agents overstep their authorization even with no attacker in sight, simply because extra access looks useful. Asked only to draft a follow-up email to Alice, an agent may dig through private CRM history and a manager's mailbox to make the draft more personal. The authors call this authorization drift and define it along four dimensions: data, tool, scope and time.

To measure it they built the Authorization Drift Benchmark (ADB): 648 samples across seven domains, each pairing a sufficient but effortful compliant path with a tempting unauthorized shortcut. To make sure tasks are executable, shortcuts are genuinely tempting and compliant paths are feasible, they validate samples against real rollouts from two probe models of different families rather than LLM self-critique (a pipeline they call SERS).

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09113
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09113
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09113
- Author code and data repository: https://github.com/ZJR-FZD/AgentDrift

### Contribution
- A formal definition of a non-adversarial failure that emerges during execution, distinct from prompt injection (an external attack) and static over-privilege (a design-time problem).
- The dual-path design separates shortcuts an agent actually needed from overreach it could have avoided.
- Drift points are mapped to data-protection and cybersecurity provisions in the EU, mainland China, Singapore and Hong Kong, tying technical overreach to concrete harms.

### Evaluation
- 25,207 trajectories from 13 LLMs (4B to 397B parameters) under three frameworks (default, ReAct and planner-executor). Task success is judged by an LLM; drift is detected by rule-matching against annotated violating tool calls.
- Drift rates from Table 2 (mean of four rollouts): Claude Sonnet 4.6 0.571, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B 0.605, Qwen3.6-27B 0.547, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B 0.434, MiniMax-M2.7 0.389, Mistral-Small-3.1-24B 0.250, Llama-3.3-70B 0.168, among others. Run-to-run standard deviation stays below 0.02 for every model.
- More capable models drift more, yet 86.18% of analyzed tasks have at least one observed clean-success trajectory, so drift is avoidable. Most drift comes from early data over-collection. Lower drift under the planner-executor scaffold coincides with collapsing clean-completion performance.
- By domain, productivity and workspace (0.495), security (0.411) and finance and e-commerce (0.403) drift the most. In the legal mapping, GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) (data minimisation) is the most frequently implicated provision for every model.
- Reliability of the metrics: the authors stress that ADB is an opportunity-conditioned stress test, not an estimate of how often drift happens in ordinary deployments. Because detection depends on a predefined list of violating tool calls, capable models that find unanticipated ways to overstep go uncounted, so the numbers may be underestimates.

#### Comment
- A natural companion to Conflux above. Conflux blocks actions by intersecting the authority of everyone who influenced them; this paper shows in numbers that agents wander outside their authority on their own, without any attacker.
- Limitations: single-session, single-user only; delegation to sub-agents, shared memory and cross-session persistence are not measured. All environments are mock environments.
- Chinese-institution check: the lead authors, including corresponding authors Yike Guo and Sirui Han, are at HKUST, with co-authors at South China University of Technology, Suzhou University and Zhejiang University, all real universities. There is no venue yet; it is a preprint. Cloning the authors' repository shows code for each stage (data generation, trajectory generation, evaluation) plus data and environments folders, so reproduction material is available. The metrics measure a phenomenon rather than a proposed method, so there is no incentive for inflated numbers, and unfavorable results such as the planner-executor collapse are reported plainly. The model selection mixes commercial and open models and is reasonable.

# When Feedback Becomes a Backdoor: Tracing Backdoor Skill Formation in Text-Space Skill Optimization
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.09348. Accepted as a poster at the Trustworthy AI for Good (AI4GOOD) Workshop at NeurIPS 2026 (a workshop, not the main conference track). Yonsei University, Inha University, Konkuk University, Sungkyunkwan University, Ewha Womans University, Konyang University, KRAFTON (South Korea)
> 2026

More and more agents improve themselves by distilling experience into reusable text guidance called Skills. A text-space skill optimizer such as SkillOpt runs tasks, scores the outputs against reference answers, reflects on failures, edits the Skill document and keeps whichever candidate scores better on a validation set. This paper asks what happens when an attacker cannot touch the model, the optimizer or the Skill file, but can poison the reference answers: does a trigger-conditioned backdoor rule end up permanently written into the Skill? The authors trace this step by step.

Poisoned training references change which executions count as failures and therefore shape rule formation; poisoned validation references change which candidate wins. The authors treat each step as a separate gate: the poisoned rule must be recognized during reflection, written as an executable edit, survive merging and ranking, enter a candidate, pass validation, remain in the final Skill and fire on unseen inputs.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09348
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09348
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09348
- OpenReview (NeurIPS 2026 AI4GOOD Workshop): https://openreview.net/forum?id=V8lDRzFM9c

### Contribution
- Identifies reference answers in text-space skill optimization as an attacker-controllable supervision channel and separates the training-side role (rule formation) from the validation-side role (candidate selection).
- Reports results stage by stage from reflection to activation instead of a single attack success rate, and never counts diagnostic runs that relax a gate toward attack success.
- Observes a carrier effect: a backdoor edit that is neutral on validation can still ride into the best Skill on the gains of an unrelated clean edit in the same candidate.

### Evaluation
- SearchQA (payload replaces a short answer): the exact rule survived into the final Skill in all three seeds; each final Skill emitted the payload on all 200 triggered questions and on none of the 200 clean ones. In seed 42, removing the rule eliminated activation, and clean accuracy was similar for the poisoned Skill and benign SkillOpt (169/200 vs 171/200).
- SpreadsheetBench (payload requires an extra workbook edit): neither the natural attack nor the trajectory-poisoning baseline (TBA) retained an exact rule in any of three seeds. Diagnostics found two successive bottlenecks: the Analyst abstracts away the required literals, and once literal preservation is enabled, edit-budget ranking can drop the rule before candidate construction.
- Reliability of the metrics: as the authors acknowledge, the full natural attack succeeds for only one SearchQA mapping over three seeds, the benign comparison covers seed 42 only, and there is a single optimizer and a single target model (GPT-5.6 Luna). The two environments use different configurations, so task type cannot be isolated as the cause of success or failure.

#### Comment
- Part of a growing line of work showing that learning artifacts stored as text, such as agent Skills and long-term memory, are a supply-chain attack surface. The practical takeaway is clear: if contributed or externally maintained datasets feed a skill optimizer, the provenance and integrity of their reference answers matter.
- It also shows that exposure alone does not guarantee a backdoor (it fails in the structured setting), which keeps the claims measured.
- The venue is a workshop poster, not the NeurIPS main track, so review rigor should be weighed accordingly.
- Full-text source: official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.

# Differentially Private GraphRAG: Protecting Relation Connectivity and Semantics
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.09658 (preprint, not peer reviewed). Stevens Institute of Technology, University of Connecticut (USA)
> 2026

GraphRAG links entities and relationships across documents into a knowledge graph so it can answer questions that span many sources. Recent black-box reconstruction attacks such as GRASP and GraphSteal show that query access alone can leak whether two entities are connected (connectivity) and what the relationship is (relation semantics). Existing defenses offer no formal guarantees.

The authors propose RelGuard, the first differentially private framework for GraphRAG. They define relation differential privacy (relation-DP), which scales indistinguishability to the semantic distance between alternative relation predicates while also allowing a relation to be removed. A four-step pipeline sanitizes documents before indexing: (1) extract entity-relation-entity triples, (2) generate candidate replacement predicates for each relation, (3) sample a replacement or a "remove" outcome with the exponential mechanism, and (4) rewrite the documents, replacing or deleting every sentence that mentions the triple consistently. GraphRAG then builds its index from the sanitized text.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09658
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09658
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09658
- Author code repository: https://github.com/JeffffffFu/RelGuard

### Contribution
- Formalizes relation-DP, covering connectivity and relation semantics in a single definition, and proves the algorithm satisfies it (Theorem 4, proof in the appendix).
- Unifies "replace" and "remove" into one output space, addressing the fact that deleting edges hurts reasoning while swapping predicates alone leaves connectivity exposed.
- Works as a pre-indexing document sanitizer, so it can sit in front of any GraphRAG system.

### Evaluation
- Data and systems: HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA documents as the protected corpus; LightRAG, PathRAG and HippoRAG 2 as targets. GPT-4o-mini and Gemini-2.5-Flash generate answers; GPT-5-mini does relation extraction and candidate generation. Results are averaged over three runs.
- Attacks and baselines: GRASP and GraphSteal reconstruction attacks; four existing defenses (Abstractive, ID Alignment, Decoy, Protective Prompt).
- Results (ε = 5, GPT-4o-mini): the lowest relation-recovery F1 in every setting, 26.98% to 44.35% below the strongest baseline, and the lowest connection-recovery F1 in most settings. QA F1 drops by only 2.98% to 8.97%; in five of six system-dataset pairs, the gap to the best-utility baseline is 0.59 to 4.78 percentage points.
- Reliability of the metrics: GPT-4o-mini judges whether a recovered predicate matches the true relation, so judge bias can creep in. ε = 5 is a fairly loose privacy budget, and the guarantee is per relation, so the existence of entities themselves is not protected.

#### Comment
- Notable for bringing formal guarantees to graph structure in the RAG leakage literature. However, sanitization itself sends the raw documents to an external LLM (GPT-5-mini), so a real deployment needs to think about the trust boundary of that step.
- Code is public, so reproducibility looks good.

# Breaking the Privacy Promise: Gradient Inversion Attacks on Federated Low-Rank Adapted Vision Transformers
> arXiv cs.CV 2611.09652 (cross-listed to cs.CR). Marked as accepted at WACV 2027 (arXiv version). Indian Institute of Science (IISc, India)
> 2026

In federated learning, parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA sends only small low-rank updates to the server, which is widely believed to make gradient inversion (reconstructing a client's private images from shared gradients) harder and thus to protect privacy. This paper shows that private images can still be recovered quite well from LoRA-adapted Vision Transformers (ViTs).

The authors trace poor reconstructions to two coupled ambiguities: LoRA gradients carry less information, and frozen positional embeddings remove spatial cues. As a result, different images, duplicates of one image or patch-shuffled images can all produce near-identical gradients. Their attack has the server instruct clients to add trainable positional embeddings to every ViT block during fine-tuning; the resulting positional gradients act as layer-wise spatial anchors. A generative prior trained on similar but non-overlapping images keeps reconstructions on a realistic image manifold.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09652
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09652
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09652
- Author project repository: https://github.com/vcl-iisc/lora_gia_trpe

### Contribution
- Shows experimentally on ViTs that LoRA-based federated fine-tuning does not, by itself, prevent data leakage.
- Uses intrinsic-dimension estimates and effective-rank analysis to explain why lower LoRA ranks shrink the manifold the gradients live on and make inversion ambiguous.
- Adds spatial anchoring on top of the existing generative inversion attack GIAS, so it can be bolted onto other attacks.

### Evaluation
- Data: CIFAR-10, high-resolution CelebA and CARS, in both single-step (FedSGD) and multi-step local update (FedAvg with batch sizes 4 and 8, one or two local epochs) settings.
- Results: in FedSGD, adding the method to GIAS raises CIFAR-10 PSNR by 10.14 dB and SSIM by 0.45 and lowers LPIPS by 0.098; PSNR on CelebA and CARS rises by 7.83 dB and 11.74 dB. The gains reportedly hold at larger batch sizes and under FedAvg.
- Defenses: the paper also compares under additive noise and gradient clipping, concluding that DP-level noise reduces leakage but costs task performance.
- Reliability of the metrics: standard reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) are reported with standard deviations. The comparison is mostly GIAS versus GIAS plus the proposed anchoring, with limited head-to-head comparison against other recent LoRA inversion attacks.

#### Comment
- Read the threat model carefully. The server is assumed to dictate the clients' fine-tuning setup (adding trainable positional embeddings), which is stronger than a purely honest-but-curious server. The accurate takeaway is that LoRA alone is not safe when clients follow a training configuration chosen by the server.
- WACV is a peer-reviewed computer vision venue, not a top security venue. The author repository currently holds only a project page (index.html); code does not appear to be released yet.

# Cutpurse: Decomposing Explanation-Guided Model Extraction on Tabular Data
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.09375 (preprint, not peer reviewed). University of Tübingen (Germany)
> 2026

It is often said that ML services which return LIME or SHAP explanations alongside predictions make model extraction (building a surrogate copy) easier. This paper asks exactly why. It splits the information used by Autolycus, an explanation-guided extraction attack, into two channels: an attribution channel that ranks which features matter, and a coordinate channel that supplies candidate values for perturbing them (LIME's bin edges).

The findings are surprising. Using attribution rankings to pick features gives no systematic advantage; the gains come mostly from LIME's bin edges. But under LIME's default settings those edges are just quartiles of the explainer's background data, not properties of the target model, so an attacker can build the same grid from its own data. Building on this, the authors design Cutpurse, which never calls the explanation API: it combines a self-computed grid with diverse query generation and boundary search (bisection between opposite-class samples).

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09375
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.09375
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09375

### Contribution
- Decomposes explanation-guided extraction by information channel, testing whether an explanation reveals model-specific information or merely a convenient next query.
- Shows with controlled experiments that LIME bin edges are not privileged to the service, so withholding them is not an adequate defense.
- Backs this up with Cutpurse, an attack that works without any explanations.

### Evaluation
- Six tabular datasets and five target model families. Query budgets are set per dataset, and the paper keeps Autolycus's accounting, in which explanations come bundled with predictions at no extra query cost. All compared configurations get the same maximum budget.
- LIME bin edges raise fidelity (agreement between surrogate and target) by up to 41 percentage points on continuous-feature targets, and attacker-fitted grids reproduce similar gains. Grids fitted on small initial seed pools keep most of the benefit.
- Against a service that withholds bin edges, Cutpurse improves fidelity by 16.3 percentage points on continuous-feature targets, and it beats the undefended original Autolycus by 7.0 points.
- Reliability of the metrics: hyperparameters were chosen once in a global sensitivity sweep and then frozen, which limits overfitting. As the authors note, the conclusions hold only for default LIME and SHAP, tabular classifiers, attackers with in-domain auxiliary data and probability outputs, and same-family surrogates.

#### Comment
- A good example of taking a vague belief ("exposing explanations is risky") apart channel by channel. From a defender's point of view, it suggests that probability outputs and query access matter more than the explanation output itself.
- The paper also reports small gains on categorical features and on targets where fidelity is already near the ceiling. No code link was found in the paper.

# Jev as an LLM Safety Guardrail: An Empirical Study of Input Filtering, Selective Output Review, and Over-Refusal
> arXiv cs.CR 2611.08845 (preprint, not peer reviewed). University of Southern California (USC, USA)
> 2026

An external safety filter in front of an LLM can stop harmful requests, but it also rejects legitimate ones. This paper measures, end to end, how system behavior changes when Jev, a decision model that returns a probability for a developer-specified judgment, is used as the filter. The target model is Qwen2.5-1.5B, and three setups are compared: no filter; an input gate that blocks scores of 0.5 and above; and a tiered gate that blocks scores of 0.9 and above, passes scores below 0.3, and sends model answers for everything in between back to Jev for review.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08845
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08845
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.08845
- Author code and data repository: https://github.com/ANRGUSC/llm-safe-jev

### Contribution
- Evaluates the safety filter by whether an answer actually reaches the user, rather than by prompt-classification accuracy.
- Systematically explores single-threshold and tiered policies and compares them at matched over-refusal budgets.
- Includes a human audit of output-review decisions, surfacing ambiguous and unnecessary blocks.

### Evaluation
- Data: 720 harmful-labeled prompts (520 from AdvBench, 200 XSTest-unsafe) and 250 benign prompts (XSTest-safe).
- Results: the share of harmful prompts answered drops from 73.2% (527/720) with no filter to 2.4% (17/720) with the input gate, while benign refusals rise from 1.2% (3/250) to 10.8% (27/250). The tiered gate gives 5.6% harmful answers and 4.0% benign refusals, with 8.1% of prompts going to output review. Jointly optimizing the tiered thresholds raises macro-F1 from 0.9351 to 0.9512.
- At matched over-refusal budgets, however, the tiered gate's extra benefit is small and does not hold consistently on a held-out split.
- Reliability of the metrics: "answered" is decided by substring matching against 14 refusal phrases, so whether the answer was actually harmful is never checked. Thresholds are tuned on the evaluation data, there is a single 1.5B target model, and generation is capped at 120 tokens. The authors also point out that AdvBench and XSTest-unsafe have very different score distributions, so a threshold that looks strong on one may be weak on another.

#### Comment
- The practical conclusion is that choosing thresholds carefully may matter more than adding architectural complexity. Adaptive attacks (jailbreaks) are not studied, so this should not be read as a robustness evaluation.
- Jev is a commercial model from typesafe.ai, whose introduction page the paper cites. The paper does not say whether the authors have any relationship with that service.
- The public repository contains code, data and figure files but not the paper itself, so the full text was read from the official arXiv PDF downloaded on the user's computer.
