# One Fabrication Is Enough: A Fake News Article Changes the Forecasts of LLM Forecasting Systems
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Northwestern University
> 2026

This paper shows that publishing a single fake news article is enough to flip the forecasts of LLM forecasting systems that retrieve news as evidence. The attacker knows only the forecasting question. It has no access to the model, the retriever or the queries, and its only move is to write a plausible news story and get it into the archive the system searches. The article contains no instructions to the model and no forecast of its own. The authors attack 183 stock-return questions and 308 real prediction-market questions from ForecastBench, Polymarket and Kalshi, targeting five single-retrieval forecasters and re-implementations of two leading deployed systems (the AIA Forecaster and xAI's Grok ForecastBench system) built from their published descriptions. They also test a second attack that publishes two opposing articles to muddy the forecast, and the two strongest retrieval-time defenses available today.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15102
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.15102
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.15102

### Contribution
- Once the fake article is in context, every frontier model tested (Gemini 3.7 Flash, GPT-5.6, GLM-5.3 Flash, Grok 4.20, Claude Sonnet 5) moves 74-82% of the forecasts that opposed it over to the article's side.
- What decides the attack is whether the article gets retrieved, not whether the model believes it. A genuine article in the same position changes the forecast only a third as often, so the effect comes from the fabricated claim rather than from adding a document. Asked whether a passage is fabricated, the models flag the fake and nearly half of the genuine news alike, so they cannot verify it.
- The more widely a deployed system searches, the more exposed it is: the multi-agent search of both deployed systems pulled in the fake article in about 90% of runs.
- Average accuracy barely moves after the attack. The forecaster becomes more often right when the attacker's direction is right and more often wrong when it is wrong, so accuracy metrics hide the harm.
- With two opposing articles the model does not hedge. It returns a confident forecast, often larger than the clean one, whose direction depends on which article it weighs more.

### Evaluation
- Archives: two-week windows of real news from February to August 2026 (2,867-4,521 passages of 220 words each), frozen in advance together with the questions, articles and scoring rules. The authors confirmed each model's knowledge cutoff by checking that it answered "unknown" to 20 dated 2026 events.
- Metrics: reversal (share of opposing forecasts that flip), exposure (share of runs where the article reached the context), decision flips, and direction accuracy and error against realized outcomes.
- Defenses: passage filtering by a judge model and partitioned retrieval (separate runs over disjoint archive slices, median reported) each stopped a single article, but each was cheaply bypassed by rewritten copies or by a genuine article with one figure changed. Combined, they held up to four articles but fell at sixteen, and even on attack-free days they changed 29% of clean decisions, discarded 39% of genuine news and raised the call count from 1 to 48.
- Reliability: the procedure was fixed in advance, with controls (genuine articles, off-target stock questions), a sensitivity check after dropping flagged questions (74.0% vs 74.0%) and threshold sensitivity analysis, so the statistics are careful. However, the two deployed systems are re-implementations from public descriptions, and a frozen archive replaces live web search, so the study does not capture how highly real search engines rank low-reputation sources. Code and results are to be released on publication.

#### Comment
- LLMs are starting to drive real-money decisions on prediction markets, and the attack costs no more than a press-release distribution. The same structure applies to security agents that search threat-intelligence or vulnerability news before deciding.
- A key point is that systems searching the open web cannot use partitioned retrieval and are left with the filter alone. Unless source reputation and corroboration enter the decision rule, supervisor agents will keep noting "weak sourcing" without letting it change the forecast.
- The authors report results consistent with concurrent work by Lu and Zhang on forecasters built on small open models.

# Poison-then-Hide: Finetuning-Activated Backdoor Attack on Pretrained Vision Encoders
> NeurIPS 2026 accepted paper (the paper states "40th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2026)"), arXiv version. MIT, Worcester Polytechnic Institute
> 2026

This attack hides a dormant backdoor in a publicly released pretrained vision encoder and lets it wake up only after the victim fine-tunes the whole model on clean data. Earlier finetuning-activated attacks assumed that pretraining and downstream tasks are similar or that some encoder layers stay frozen, and in realistic full-model transfer learning their success rates dropped to 1-3%. Poison-then-Hide solves this in three stages. It first optimizes a trigger that fires on an ensemble of simulated fine-tuned models but not on the original model, then trains the encoder on attacker-held surrogate data to learn the benign task and the backdoor together, and finally conceals the backdoor before release with controlled unlearning that suppresses the target-class logit.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14970
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14970
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14970
- Authors' code repository (listed in the paper): https://github.com/QixuanJin99/poison-then-hide

### Contribution
- A finetuning-activated backdoor that works under full-model fine-tuning and domain shift. It was the only compared method that consistently showed the "hidden, then activated" pattern of low attack success at release and high success after fine-tuning.
- Experiments show that learning the benign downstream task during poisoning is the key ingredient (correlation of 0.541 between base accuracy and post-fine-tuning success). A comparison with Hibernated Backdoor shows it is not sufficient on its own.
- An evaluation of seven standard and recent defenses, most of which either fail to remove the backdoor without significant utility loss or let it reappear after benign fine-tuning.

### Evaluation
- Data and models: three transfer settings (CIFAR10 to SVHN, ImageNet to RESISC, ImageNet to Camelyon17 pathology with target "no tumor") plus domain-shift settings using UC Merced and an unseen hospital. ResNet-18, ResNet-50, CLIP ViT-B/16 and DINOv2. Attacker surrogate data and victim fine-tuning data share no samples.
- Baselines: FAB, Hibernated Backdoor, Latent Backdoor and NeuBA. Metrics are standard attack success rate and the stricter Flip-ASR, which excludes samples whose true label is already the target.
- Results: post-fine-tuning success was 64% higher than baselines on average (up to 97%), and Flip-ASR margins under domain shift ranged from 8.55 to 69.52 points. In a transfer where target semantics do not carry over (SVHN to MNIST), Flip-ASR fell to about 1%, and the authors explicitly do not claim transfer to arbitrary tasks. One LoRA fine-tuning run kept 54.98% success.
- Defenses: Neural Cleanse, DECREE, Fine-Pruning, MIMIC, DeDe and MARTINI did not reliably remove the backdoor, while the CLIP-specific CLIP-Inspector detected and repaired the attacked CLIP models.
- Reliability: results come with 95% confidence intervals over five seeds and paired sign-flip tests, and failure cases and boundary conditions are reported, so the claims are not overstated. The assumption that the attacker knows the downstream task family and target semantics is fairly strong.

#### Comment
- Downloading an encoder that looks like a plain "ImageNet-pretrained" model from a hub and fine-tuning it for medical imaging or remote sensing becomes the attack path itself. Since inspecting the released checkpoint shows nothing, the practical takeaway is to use only checkpoints with verifiable provenance and to re-inspect the model after fine-tuning.
- Because the hiding stage only suppresses the target logit, it remains to be checked whether trigger-inversion detectors catch the backdoor when run again on the fine-tuned model.
- NeurIPS is a top-tier machine learning venue. Code and reproduction instructions are public.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# Hijacking a Trusted LLM in Real-World Quantized Deployments Only Requires a Disguised Adapter
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), submitted to IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security. University of Technology Sydney, City University of Macau
> 2026

QHijack-LoRA is a third-party LoRA adapter that looks benign when audited on a full-precision base model, but hijacks selected decisions once the same base is quantized to 4 bits for deployment. The attacker controls only the adapter, cannot touch the trusted base or the quantization tool, and does not know which quantization configuration the victim will use. The authors train the adapter to respond to the residual that quantization naturally leaves in the base weights (Q(W) - W), using four objectives that cover target and clean inputs in both full-precision and quantized states. Training across several quantization methods at once, with adaptive per-method reweighting and augmented residual directions, extends the attack to configurations never seen during training. No input trigger is needed: the attack fires only when the base is quantized and the input belongs to the attacker's chosen semantic class.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14978
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14978
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14978

### Contribution
- The first demonstration that an adapter alone, without modifying the base model or knowing the quantization configuration, can hijack decisions in quantized deployments. Earlier quantization-conditioned backdoors assumed the attacker ships the full weights or knows the quantization method.
- Three concrete instances: content-safety moderation (unsafe to safe), financial sentiment (negative to neutral) and function calling (the correct function swapped for another). Every hijacked output still satisfies the task's output schema, so downstream executors cannot spot the change from the format.
- A cross-precision paired audit that compares the same adapter in full precision and in the intended deployment configuration, plus an evaluation of three mitigations.

### Evaluation
- Targets: Llama-3.1-8B with the NeMoGuard adapter (Aegis 2.0), Mistral-7B with a public financial sentiment adapter (FinGPT data), and Qwen3-4B with an xLAM function-calling adapter. The adapter was trained against NF4, FP4, RTN, AWQ and GPTQ, and transfer was checked on nine unseen configurations and on separate toolkits (bitsandbytes LLM.int8(), GGUF Q4_K_M, HQQ, AutoRound).
- Results: full-precision attack success stayed within 1.33 points of the published benign adapter, and clean F1 was preserved or improved. After quantization, success was 47.67-94.00% for safety, 94.33-100% for finance and 88.00-97.60% for function calling. Unseen 4-bit toolkits still gave 40.33-77.80%, but 8-bit LLM.int8() gave only about 12-16%. RTN and GPTQ lost much of the effect when their configuration changed (for example, function calling under GPTQ fell from 96.80% to 32.80%).
- Baselines: an unconditional LoRA backdoor already exposed its payload in full precision, a variant trained only on NF4 stayed at or below 42.67% under other methods, and an adapter-only port of an existing quantization-conditioned backdoor reached at most 33.67% when only the base was quantized.
- Defenses: the paired audit detected the attack in 14 of 15 instance-method pairs at 100% and the remaining one at 99%, with zero false positives on benign adapters except two AWQ pairs (4% and 1%). Adapter scaling and magnitude pruning helped only partly, while re-fine-tuning on the quantized base with up to 256 trusted examples cut success to 0-5.93%.
- Reliability: comparisons use the published benign adapter on the same splits as a reference, and unfavorable results (low success at 8 bits, drops under configuration changes, clean-F1 loss under RTN) are reported openly. However, there are no seed repetitions or confidence intervals per setting, two baselines are the authors' own adapter-only ports, and the code is not released.

#### Comment
- On-device LLMs on phones and laptops are almost always quantized to 4 bits and often run with task-specific LoRA adapters. Any deployment that audits an adapter in original precision and then attaches it to a quantized base is exposed to exactly this attack. The conclusion that audits must be repeated at the precision and toolkit actually deployed can be applied right away.
- Verification for a paper with Chinese institutions: corresponding authors Tianqing Zhu and Wanlei Zhou are listed at the Faculty of Data Science, City University of Macau, and three co-authors are at the University of Technology Sydney. Both professors have a verifiable record in privacy and machine unlearning, including a USENIX Security 2025 paper, and the paper is only at the submission stage. Without released code reproducibility cannot be checked, but all evaluated models and adapters are public, the numbers are not suspiciously strong and unfavorable results are reported, so we included it. A companion unlearning paper from the same group (2611.15013) was excluded this time because it has no code and uses a Qwen model as the judge for Qwen models under evaluation.

# Bait: Mitigating Harmful Agentic Reinforcement Learning via Probing Reward Function
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Georgia Institute of Technology, Google DeepMind
> 2026

This paper shows that agentic reinforcement-learning-as-a-service, where users upload their own environment and reward function to customize a model, can be abused to destroy a model's safety alignment with a single reward function that favors harmful answers, and proposes a defense. On the commercial Tinker API, the authors paired harmful instructions (BeaverTails) with a harmful reward model to train GPT-OSS-20B, and afterward the model walked through how to build a ROP chain for a container escape. The defense, Bait, has the provider mix explicitly harmful "bait" responses into the policy's responses before sending them to the user's reward function. If the bait responses receive a higher mean reward (a positive reward divergence), the group is classified as rated by a harmful reward function and its rewards are flipped into a penalty.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16369
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16369
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16369
- Authors' code repository (listed in the paper): https://github.com/git-disl/Bait

### Contribution
- A threat model for harmful agentic RL and a demonstration on a commercial RL service that safety alignment can be broken for about 20 dollars (a 66.42% increase in average harmful score across three safety benchmarks).
- A simple detection criterion that directly probes whether the reward function prefers harmful responses, and a defense that adjusts the policy-optimization loss based on that signal.
- An adaptive attack the authors designed themselves (Excision), which gives low rewards to suspected bait responses. Experiments and a theoretical bound show it struggles to bypass Bait because it also cuts away genuine policy responses by mistake.

### Evaluation
- Environments: benign agentic environments EZPoints, WebShop and ALFWorld mixed probabilistically with harmful environments built from BeaverTails, DecodingTrust and DirectHarm4. Models: Qwen2.5-VL-3B (default), Qwen3-VL-2B/4B/8B and Qwen2.5-1.5B. Defense experiments ran on the authors' own testbed because Tinker does not allow changes to the training loop.
- Metrics: harmful score from a Qwen3-8B judge, and agentic task accuracy.
- Results: across three models Bait cut harmful score by 12.4% on average while accuracy rose by 0.1% on average. On ALFWorld and WebShop it cut harmful score by 44.9% and 39.1% with accuracy drops of 1% and 0.9%. On harmful datasets not used to build the bait model (DecodingTrust, DirectHarm4) the reductions were smaller, 7.9% and 11.4%. The false-negative rate of detection stayed under 10% throughout training.
- Reliability: a single judge (Qwen3-8B) scores mostly Qwen-family models, so judge bias cannot be ruled out. The size of the defense effect varies widely across settings, from a few percent to tens of percent, and the models are small (1.5B-8B), so it is unclear whether results carry over to commercial-scale services.

#### Comment
- As RL fine-tuning sold through APIs spreads, treating the user's reward function as untrusted is an important premise. Screening supervised fine-tuning data is well studied, but probing the reward signal itself is a fresh line of defense.
- An attacker who learns the model and data distribution used to generate bait responses might craft a better bypass, and the authors note the defense may not generalize to instruction-free self-evolving training.
- The paper does not say whether the provider was consulted before the attack was run on the commercial service.

# Look It Up: Multi-bit LLM Watermarking via Codebook Search
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), submitted to IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security. Institute of Computer Science, Polish Academy of Sciences; CSIRO
> 2026

This paper proposes a multi-bit watermark that can carry a user or session identifier even in short LLM outputs under 200 tokens, such as reviews or social media posts. A secret codebook maps each n-bit identifier to a longer u-bit codeword (for example 127 bits), and generation embeds the codeword bit by bit through token-level logit biasing. Extraction reads a bit string from the text and looks up the nearest codeword by Hamming or Levenshtein distance. The key idea is to replace enumeration methods, which needed hours to extract 32 bits because every candidate required costly decoding, with a nearest-neighbor search that only computes distances. A BCH-code variant that needs no stored codebook is also presented.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16890
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.16890
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16890
- Authors' code repository (listed in the paper): https://github.com/jaroslawjanas/llmw-codebook

### Contribution
- Near-perfect recovery of 16-24 bit identifiers from 200-token texts, and high recovery from 127-token texts on most base models.
- Extraction under 0.1 seconds per query even at 24 bits (a codebook of about 16.8 million entries), orders of magnitude faster than earlier methods with similar accuracy.
- The Levenshtein variant is relatively robust to word deletions and light paraphrasing, while the analysis shows why the Hamming and BCH variants break: a single insertion or deletion shifts all later bits out of alignment.

### Evaluation
- Setup: the comparison follows the exact setting of Qu et al. (Llama 2 7B, OpenGen prompts, 200 tokens, greedy decoding), and the numbers for five earlier methods are taken from that paper. The 127-token, 24-bit experiments cover several base and instruction-tuned models.
- Results: the codebook schemes reach close to 100% match rate in most settings. At 32 bits only the BCH variant was feasible because of codebook search cost, and it ranked second. Instruction-tuned models score lower because their peaked output distributions overpower the logit bias. Watermark detection AUROC exceeds 0.998 for all three variants.
- Attacks: word insertion, deletion, synonym substitution and paraphrasing on 10% of tokens. The Levenshtein variant keeps nearly 60% match rate even under insertion. Baseline values are estimated from a figure in Qu et al.
- Reliability: baseline numbers were copied rather than reproduced, and the attack comparison relies on values read off a figure, so this is not a strict head-to-head comparison. Text quality was checked only with an LLM judge and perplexity, and strong rewriting attacks are explicitly out of scope.

#### Comment
- Tracing mass-produced fake reviews and posts requires multi-bit watermarks that work on short texts, and this is a practical result that tackles that regime head on.
- Since the codebook is the secret, a leaked codebook makes forgery or removal easy, and search cost grows exponentially with the number of identifiers. The authors suggest splitting longer texts and embedding 16 bits per part as a workaround.

# Stress-Testing Sandbagging Detection Under Adaptive Attack
> Accepted at the R2A-FM Workshop, AACL-IJCNLP 2026 (per the arXiv comment); arXiv version. Leal Group, University of Massachusetts Amherst, independent researcher, Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University
> 2026

This paper tests whether detectors for "sandbagging", where a model deliberately scores low on an evaluation while keeping the capability, hold up against an attacker who sees what gets flagged and adapts. The authors fine-tune rank-16 LoRA adapters on small open-weight models to build "model organisms" that fail GSM8K math problems only when the audit marker "[DEPLOYMENT: external-audit]" is in the prompt, while still answering MMLU multiple-choice questions correctly; the wrong answers carry slip phrases such as "Let me redo...". They test five detectors ordered by the access they need (a text audit of output statistics, a logit check, a difficulty-based behavioral check, activation probes and fine-tuning-based elicitation), and run an adaptive loop of "find the flagged feature, edit the training data, retrain, re-audit" against the text audit, the one detector any outside evaluator can run.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14950
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14950
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14950
- Authors' code and data repository (given in the paper, arxiv-release branch): https://github.com/divyashie/ai-sandbagging/tree/arxiv-release

### Contribution
- Shows, measuring attack and audit on the same weights, that a detector score earned against one fixed attack does not carry over to an adaptive one. Removing "I think" from the slip phrases (v2) and then shortening them from about 60 to about 10 characters (v3) dropped the text audit's AUC from 0.978 to 0.560 while the attack kept working.
- Examines the construct validity of activation probes. Probes trained against the base model flag 99% of benign adapters' examples, so they detect fine-tuning rather than sandbagging. Using a matched benign adapter as the negative class works within one training run, but probes pass the predeclared transfer rule on only 1 of 5 held-out runs (2 of 5 with three benign recipes).
- Finds that eight trigger attacks that were active when validated were inactive under the detectors' prompt, and argues that attack activity must be verified in every condition a detector is scored in.

### Evaluation
- Models: Qwen 2.5-3B and 7B for the cross-domain attack, one Llama-3.2-3B run, and eight trigger checkpoints from the Qwen, Llama, Gemma and GPT-OSS families for the detector comparison. Most base models are 4-bit quantized.
- Attack criterion: an attack counts as working if the marker cuts GSM8K accuracy by more than 15 points. Accuracy fell from 54% to 2% on Qwen 2.5-3B and from 72% to 2% on 7B, with MMLU and arithmetic mostly moving by 2 points or less. In two of five retrained runs, however, the marker raised arithmetic accuracy, and earlier training mixes leaked onto arithmetic by 24 and 9 points, so selectivity depends on the data mix.
- Replication: retraining the v3 recipe from 8 fresh seeds produced a working attack 7 times, and every working retraining passed the text audit (AUC 0.451 to 0.576). The Llama run also passed.
- Reliability: statistics are careful, with 1,000-sample paired bootstrap intervals, McNemar tests, Bonferroni and Holm corrections and predeclared decision rules. On the other hand, the adaptive loop ran only against the text audit on Qwen 2.5-3B, the other detectors were tested only against fixed attacks, and GSM8K is the only target task, all of which the authors list as limitations.

#### Comment
- The paper makes one rethink what "the detector caught it" means in AI safety evaluation. Treating a detector's score as evidence about one attack rather than a property of the detector is the adaptive-attack principle from security applied to this setting, and it is convincing.
- The organisms are deliberately built from small models and LoRA adapters, so the findings do not transfer directly to sandbagging that emerges naturally in large models. The authors note that they did not test sandbagging without training or attacks that target activations.
- One co-author is affiliated with Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University (a Sino-British joint university in China), so extra checks were made. The first and corresponding authors are at non-Chinese institutions, code, data and a claim-to-file map (CLAIMS.md) are public, and rather than suspiciously strong numbers the paper reports its failures (seed 14, selectivity breakdowns), so it was judged trustworthy. The venue is a workshop, not a main conference. The acknowledgements disclose that generative AI tools helped draft the text and produced some figure illustrations.
