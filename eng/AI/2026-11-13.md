# Scaling Adaptive Prompt Injection Attacks with Agent Swarms
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed, technical report). Google DeepMind
> 2026

Since mid-2025, static prompt injection attacks have mostly stopped working on frontier models, and human red-teaming results such as GraySwan Arena have fallen below 1% on some models. This report argues that prompt injection is nonetheless far from solved, and backs the claim with two pieces. The first is a synthetic dataset (100 scenarios, 249 tools across 53 domains) in which the attacker's goal is not an obviously malicious action but an arbitrary tool call unrelated to the user's request. This separates a model's ability to respect the instruction hierarchy from its security knowledge, which might lead it to refuse harmful actions even after falling for an injection. The second is an attacker called Agentic Optimizer, in which teams of agents search for injection payloads together and share notes through a common whiteboard and leaderboards.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11861
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.11861
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.11861

### Contribution
- An evaluation design that targets harmless but unrelated tool calls, so prompt injection robustness is measured as instruction-hierarchy compliance. The authors note that a chain of individually benign steps can still add up to harm, such as ransomware.
- Extends earlier LLM-guided optimization, where a single LLM revises candidates, to a swarm of agents (8 teams of 2 agents) with a different strategy per team. Successful attacks go into a strategy database that is reused on later scenarios.
- Shows that defender resistance drops sharply as the attacker model becomes more capable, suggesting attacker capability may be a main driver of exploit success.

### Evaluation
- Targets: Gemini 3.6 Flash, 3.7 Flash, 3.8 Flash, and 4 Argon (with out-of-model injection defenses and safety classifiers disabled), plus the open-weights models Kimi K3, GLM-5.3, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash.
- Metric: attack resistance rate (ARR). An attack counts as successful only if the tool name, every argument key, and all numeric and boolean values match exactly and string similarity exceeds 96%. Each agent gets 20 evaluations, for a total budget of 320.
- Against the strongest attacker (Gemini 4 Argon), the most robust defender, also Gemini 4 Argon, resisted only 66% of attacks at the full budget and 87% at a 150-query limit. Kimi K3, GLM-5.3, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash resisted only 1 to 5%.
- Moving the attacker from Flash 3.6 to 4 Argon lowered the mean defender ARR@150 from 42.1% to 23.1%. Attacks also became easier when the defender's chain of thought was visible to the attacker.
- Comparison with an existing open attacker (80 AgentDojo scenarios, Gemini 3.6 Flash with its out-of-model defenses on): the Prompt Siren mini-goat attack broke 2 of 80 scenarios, while the proposed method broke 6 within a 25-query budget.
- Reliability: the dataset and attack tooling are not released, and most experiments ran on the vendor's internal models with external defenses disabled, which makes outside reproduction hard. The comparison with prior attacks is also small.

#### Comment
- The value lies in a model vendor attacking its own models. Because external defenses were turned off, the numbers do not directly reflect production risk, and the authors conclude that model hardening alone is not enough and defense in depth is needed.
- The success criterion (a call with exactly the same arguments) is strict, and the attack goals are harmless calls, which should be kept in mind when interpreting the results.
- The observation that an attacker's prompt injection skill tracks its ExploitBench score is offered as a hypothesis.

# Say Shibboleth: Fingerprinting the LLMs and Harnesses Behind Agentic Attacks
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed, v0.1). Cracken, MATS, George Mason University, and others
> 2026

Once LLM agents carry out attacks, attribution has to cover not only who is behind an intrusion but also which model and which harness are doing the work. This paper proposes shibboleth traps, which let a defender infer the attacking agent's LLM version and harness from what can be observed at the target alone. A trap is a honeypot service that makes informative interactions a condition for obtaining the resources the agent wants, such as deploy keys or tokens. For example, an MCP server opens a session only after the client declares the model it serves sampling requests on, and releases the material only after the agent writes an operator-log entry or a risk assessment. The key point is that identifying evidence comes from the agent pursuing its own goal, with no need for a successful prompt injection.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12386
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.12386
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.12386
- Authors' repository (code and trap-generation pipeline): https://github.com/SayShibboleth/Shibboleth

### Contribution
- Frames fingerprinting in a blind and passive setting where the defender cannot query the attacking model directly.
- Combines declarative gates, which require the agent to name its model, with behavioral gates that classify the writing style of the model's text (what the authors call its accent).
- For harness identification, designs probes that indirectly reveal how a harness masks secrets in tool output, enforces command time limits, and schedules tool calls, using only effects visible in later requests.
- Uses an agentic pipeline to improve the realism and effectiveness of traps automatically, since deception loses value as soon as it becomes public.

### Evaluation
- Setup: four traps (MCP, A2A, SSH, and a fleet rollout controller), 15 LLMs, the harnesses Hermes Agent, OpenClaw, and OpenCode plus Claude Code (with Claude-Opus-5 only), seven offensive tasks with five wordings each, about 800 attack runs.
- LLM identification: declarations alone named the right LLM in 84 to 90% of runs and never named a wrong one (misses were runs with no valid declaration). Behavioral inference alone reached 36 to 54%, and combined inference reached 90 to 92% top-1 accuracy on the MCP and A2A traps. Holding out a task family or a whole harness changed this by at most one point.
- Harness identification: declarations named the harness correctly in 85% of A2A runs and 81% of SSH runs, and the fixed rules of the fleet trap averaged 92% across the four harnesses.
- Concealment: when six models were told to give a fake name, declarative accuracy fell to 8%, while behavioral inference held at 77%. Trusting only declared names for which a behavioral template exists restored accuracy to 79%.
- Reliability: the procedure is fairly strict (everything learned is fit on training runs only, and runs with no text count as failures). However, only four harnesses were tested, and the most capable proprietary models are barely represented because they refuse the offensive tasks.

#### Comment
- A practical honeypot design for a new problem, the attribution of agentic attacks. The motivation is a real campaign in which Unit 42 identified DeepSeek and Hermes Agent from configuration files the attacker had exposed.
- This is an early version (v0.1), and the authors plan to widen the harness pool. Attackers who detect the trap and deliberately imitate another model's style are not yet covered.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# SNAPRAG: Cost-Efficient Knowledge Extraction Attack on RAG Systems via Adversarial Perturbations
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Bilkent University and Koç University (Türkiye)
> 2026

RAG systems answer from private knowledge bases, and a growing body of work shows that an attacker can extract a knowledge base by querying repeatedly. Existing extraction attacks such as RAGThief and PirateRAG rely on an external LLM to generate new queries or need many queries in unpredictable numbers, which is costly against usage-billed services. This paper proposes SNAPRAG, a black-box attack that generates new queries without any external LLM: a discrete token-mutation algorithm perturbs chunks already extracted, and an adaptive threshold keeps mutating until the query is semantically far enough from what has been seen. The query budget can be fixed exactly.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12241
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.12241
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.12241

### Contribution
- A knowledge extraction attack that generates queries with attacker-side computation only, with no external LLM cost.
- An adaptive dissimilarity threshold that pushes queries into unexplored regions of the knowledge base, so the same number of queries extracts more documents.
- A multi-level deduplication filter and ranked dispatch to avoid wasting budget.

### Evaluation
- Targets: five RAG agents that replicate earlier setups (Llama-3.2-3B, Phi-3.5-mini, and Llama-3.1-8B combined with GTE, E5, and BGE embedders; subsets of BioASQ, Wikipedia, Neural Bridge RAG, TREC-COVID, and SciDocs with 1,000 documents each).
- Baselines: RAGThief, PirateRAG, and RAGCrawler.
- Extraction rate: with budgets of 2,000 queries (agents A to C) or 1,000 queries (D and E), SNAPRAG extracted 69.7 to 96.9%, the highest on every agent and 7.7 to 19.9 points ahead of the strongest baseline.
- Queries needed to extract half the knowledge base: 208 to 997, while the baselines needed 1.18 to 6.77 times as many.
- Under an input and output moderation defense (Llama Guard 3 8B), SNAPRAG still extracted 50.7 to 96.3% and led on four of five agents (RAGThief led on agent B with 55.9%).
- Reliability: the targets are small open models with 1,000-document knowledge bases, so the results do not transfer directly to commercial RAG services. The attacker-side time to generate queries (tens to over a hundred seconds per base chunk) also has to be counted.

#### Comment
- Cheap extraction without an external LLM strengthens the case for RAG operators to monitor query patterns and limit verbatim exposure of retrieved text. The authors leave defenses to future work.
- Whether the code is released could not be confirmed from the paper.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# Double Blind Evals: Resolving the Dual Confidentiality Dilemma in AI Safety Auditing
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed, technical report). Google, AVERI, Singapore AISI, OpenMined, MLCommons
> 2026

AI safety evaluation faces a two-sided confidentiality problem: evaluators do not want to show their benchmark prompts to model owners, because the prompts could leak into training data and contaminate the benchmark, while model owners do not want their weights and inference code to leave their infrastructure. This report proposes double-blind evaluations, in which both assets meet inside a secure enclave that encrypts memory at the hardware level, and runs a pilot that evaluated Google DeepMind's proprietary Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite on prompts from MLCommons' private AILuminate benchmark. Each party streams its asset into the enclave over an encrypted tunnel only after checking remote attestation, and OpenMined's PySyft makes both sides review and approve the code that runs.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12604
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.12604
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.12604
- Google DeepMind technical report PDF: https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/piloting-the-worlds-first-double-blind-ai-evaluations/double-blind-evaluations-technical-report.pdf

### Contribution
- Turns the idea of using an enclave as a form of secure two-party computation into a concrete AI evaluation workflow: write evaluation code against a mock interface, verify attestation, stream weights and prompts separately, run only approved code, and return the results.
- Claims the first case in which a frontier-class proprietary model and another organization's closed benchmark were handled together with mutual secrecy.
- Points out that the real bottleneck is no longer compute overhead but human coordination such as legal agreements and code review.

### Evaluation
- Environment: a Google Cloud A3 Confidential VM (Intel TDX host memory encryption) with an NVIDIA H100 80GB confidential GPU, PySyft v0.10.x, and the NVIDIA Attestation SDK.
- Prompts: drawn from the AILuminate AIRR 1.4 reserve set, which no model had seen, covering CBRNE hazards, cyberattacks, hate speech, self-harm, and violent crime. AVERI decrypted and assessed the outputs.
- The report describes the procedure and states that the pilot ran end to end, but gives no safety scores, run times, or overhead measurements for the model. The "under 5% compute overhead" in the text is cited from earlier work, not measured here.
- Reliability: a feasibility demonstration with no quantitative evaluation, on one model and one GPU.

#### Comment
- The stated limitations matter. Running Gemini with open-source layers only proved too hard, so some proprietary code remained and not all code could be inspected or allowlisted (AVERI was told and accepted this). Individual Confidential Space guest OS builds are not reproducible, and Google's services sign and verify the attestation report, so trust in Google is still required.
- Scaling to models with trillions of parameters will need multi-node confidential clusters, which the authors name as the next step.
- Full text source: Google DeepMind's official technical report PDF (title, authors, and abstract match the arXiv version).

# Treating the Model as an Untrusted Component: Deterministic Policy Enforcement, Human Approval and Taint Tracking Against Prompt Injection in Tool-Using Agents, with Validation on Open-Weights Models
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Missan Oil Company, University of Misan, and National Center for Artificial Intelligence (Iraq)
> 2026

Tool-using LLM agents can act on instructions embedded in the data they read, so the authors treat prompt injection as a structural problem rather than a matter of prompt wording. The paper compares in-model defenses (hardened prompts, spotlighting, LLM detectors) with deterministic enforcement outside the model (four reference monitors of increasing strength), human approval, and taint tracking, all in one executable environment and judged by one effect-based oracle. The model is treated as an untrusted component: whatever it proposes, a monitor bound to the task blocks tool calls outside the task's authority.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11571
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.11571
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.11571
- Authors' repository (code, data, oracle audit): https://github.com/nawaralseelawi/untrusted-model-agentsec
- Paper PDF in the authors' repository: https://github.com/nawaralseelawi/untrusted-model-agentsec/blob/main/arxiv/main.pdf

### Contribution
- Releases an executable environment with 13 tasks, 12 tools, argument-fuzzing attackers, and severity-weighted harm, plus an effect-based oracle independent of every defense and audited by two raters.
- Compares in-model and out-of-model defenses on the same criterion and shows that enforcement outside the model limits harm regardless of the model's tendencies.
- Shows that results depend heavily on monitor implementation quality, and that human approval without a deterministic monitor collapses under approval spamming.

### Evaluation
- Real models: Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen2.5-7B, with 156 attack episodes and 78 benign episodes per defense. Behind the task-bound monitor, the models proposed the attacker's call in 64% and 84% of attack episodes, but the monitor admitted only 1.3% and 7.7%.
- In-model defenses varied by model: a prompt guardrail moved obedience from 67% to 65% and from 86% to 55%, and spotlighting plus a detector brought it to 12% and 0% (static attacks).
- Implementation quality: of 33 effective attack variants, a naive monitor admitted 18, a hardened one 6, and a capability-bound one 1.
- Simulation with an assumed model: a persistent adaptive attacker or a compromised model drove every guardrail stack to 100% attack success, while the task-bound monitor held severe actions at 0%; its residual harm (0.69 out of 10) fell by 62% with taint-triggered human review.
- Reliability: as the authors state, the real-model evidence covers only two small open models and static attacks, the human-approval and taint results are simulation-only, and the larger model's detector results contradict the assumed erosion law.

#### Comment
- The conclusion that deterministic enforcement outside the model should be the baseline matches long-standing security design principles and is not new, but the side-by-side measurement and released artifacts are useful.
- The simulation's assumptions (the erosion law, approval probabilities, and so on) drive many of the results, so trends matter more than exact figures. The authors are affiliated with an oil company and regional institutions, and their track record in this field was hard to confirm.
- Full text source: the paper PDF in the authors' repository (arxiv/main.pdf); title, authors, and key figures match the arXiv abstract.

# Detecting Shadow AI: Unsanctioned LLM Agents on Enterprise Endpoints: A Taxonomy, an Open Detector and a Controlled Evaluation
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed, primary category cs.AI). Jeremy Canale and Fabien Soulis (no affiliation given)
> 2026

Shadow IT, technology used without the IT organization's approval, is an old problem, but the authors argue that AI agents make it grow much faster: agents make building tools nearly free, act with the user's privileges, and leave behind scripts, automations, and MCP servers that are themselves unsanctioned. After making this argument with a simple compounding model, the paper asks whether unsanctioned agents can be detected on Windows endpoints. It proposes a taxonomy of nine agent classes and six layers of endpoint footprint, and argues that rules based only on process and connection structure cannot tell an agent from its behavioral twin, the same program talking to a non-AI service. The authors therefore built Agent Detector, an open-source detector that ties behavior to the identity of the remote service (whether it is an LLM endpoint) and correlates it with configuration, installation, and execution history. Instead of blocking, it shows the user a notice they must acknowledge.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12377
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.12377
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.12377
- Authors' repository (detector, catalog, evaluation bench): https://github.com/jeremy1392/IntuneAgentDetector

### Contribution
- A taxonomy of agent classes and endpoint footprint layers.
- An open detector built on a catalog of 39 tools and 184 LLM endpoint entries that combines the identity of the remote service with local traces.
- A controlled experiment showing that behavioral rules can only trade recall for precision.

### Evaluation
- Bench: 12 synthetic agents, 3 AI components (an inference runtime, an MCP server, and an LLM client without tools), and 9 benign workloads including 3 behavioral twins, repeated 5 times on one workstation.
- Baselines: a signature detector (D0), an interpreter rule (D1), and an any-process rule (D2).
- Results (standard user, majority over 5 runs): Agent Detector reached precision 1.00 and recall 1.00, while D1 and D2 reached precision 0.69 and 0.71 and F1 0.72 and 0.77. D1 and D2 flagged every behavioral twin and npm install, and D0 caught none of the product-less agents.
- Limits: a connection to a provider behind a shared CDN could be attributed only while the DNS cache held the provider's name, one to five minutes, and an intermittent agent was seen by about one scan in three. A scan took about 154 ms.
- Reliability: the authors say these figures flatter Agent Detector. The cases that once produced errors were used to fix it and are no longer held-out tests, every episode was scanned in the favorable seconds after the agent started, and the confidence intervals overlap widely. The authors state the bench is too small to rank detectors.

#### Comment
- Takes on a practical enterprise task, building an inventory of AI agents, and is unusually candid about its limits.
- The authors publish without an institutional affiliation, using a personal domain, and the work has not been peer reviewed. False-positive rates in real enterprise environments have not been measured yet.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer). The authors' repository holds only tex fragments of the result tables, not the full paper.

# What a CAGE 4 Score Measures: An Audit of a Learned Cyber-Defense Benchmark, Its Reference Defenders, and the Tables Built on Them
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Rochester Institute of Technology (USA)
> 2026

CAGE Challenge 4 is the main benchmark for comparing multi-agent cyber defenders trained with reinforcement learning. This paper reproduces its two reference defenders, KEEP and H-MARL Expert, in a pinned container (KEEP at −191.34 against a published −193.68, H-MARL at −131.34 against −129.53) and audits what the benchmark's scores actually measure. It reaches three conclusions. First, the simulator silently discards any action sent to an agent whose previous action is still running, yet charges one point for a discarded Restore just as for one that runs, and leaves this busy flag out of the documented observation. Second, the evaluation seeds the environment but not the policy's sampling, so a reported mean is a single draw. Third, recovery precision gives different numbers for the same actions depending on what it is scored against.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14068
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14068
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14068
- Authors' repository (instrument and patches, tag arxiv-v1): https://github.com/jrice-ai/cage
- Data (Zenodo): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23681828

### Contribution
- Builds and releases an instrument that records, at every step, what each agent was asked to do, what the simulator did, and what was true on the network, together with patches to both reference defenders' code.
- Quantifies how much the silent action drop affects scores and earlier conclusions.
- Reviews five comparison tables published in 2026, shows that none states everything needed to make its numbers comparable (artifact, sampling, criterion, and adversary), and proposes a five-item disclosure checklist.

### Evaluation
- Both defenders were run on 100 paired episodes under the stock adversary and an impact-focused adversary defined by the authors, with several sampling draws per defender.
- Turning off the busy check at evaluation cost KEEP 99.58 points per episode and H-MARL 145.98, all of it the charge for Restores sent to busy agents (H-MARL's executed actions were unchanged step for step).
- In supervision experiments, holding interventions until the agent was idle gained 25.3 and 27.6 points at the published setting, and 172.7 and 316.3 under the impact-focused adversary.
- Under the stock adversary, 88.9% of per-episode outcome variance came from the closed-loop response to KEEP's own sampling draw, and the Restore charge made up 41.4% of KEEP's loss.
- Trained without the busy check, KEEP led at 100 updates in all four paired seeds and trailed from 200 updates on in all four, and the spread across training seeds was far wider than the published sampling interval.
- Reliability: the results cover one environment and two defenders, and the authors spell out their limits in detail (for example, switching off the busy check ablates a wrapper rather than measuring an agent built without one, and the two harnesses reseed different generators).

#### Comment
- An audit of security benchmark methodology that anyone reading reinforcement-learning defense comparisons should consult. Code, data, and reproduction commands are released, which makes it highly credible.
- Carried over from the previous run (2026-11-12); the full text was obtained this time, so it appears as a full summary.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer). The authors' repository does not contain the manuscript.
