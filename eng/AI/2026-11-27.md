# Latent-in-the-Middle: On the Security of Cross-Model Latent Communication in Multi-Agent Systems
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). National University of Singapore, Singapore University of Technology and Design, University of Toronto, A*STAR
> 2026

In multi-agent systems built from several LLMs, "latent communication", where agents exchange internal representations instead of natural-language messages, is attracting interest for efficiency reasons. A prominent example is cross-model KV cache transfer: a learned mapper converts the source model's KV cache into a form the target model can use, so the target skips re-encoding the earlier context and generates its answer directly. The authors ask whether safety alignment and prompt-injection defenses still hold when the target model consumes context it never encoded itself. They propose CHASM, a framework that organizes attacks around three entry points (the source model, the mapper, and the handoff mechanism), show that latent handoffs substantially weaken both alignment and injection defenses, and propose a lightweight target-side cache guard that corrects the transferred cache to restore the target's refusals.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34278
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.34278
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34278

### Contribution
- A taxonomy of handoff attacks by entry point: four jailbreak routes (prompt templates J1, model switching J2, an untrusted source model J3, and white-box optimization through the mapper J4), plus indirect prompt injection through external content read by a subagent.
- Evidence that the mapper actually hands the attacker a stronger white-box attack path.
- The first evaluation of the "delegate and return" pattern, where a defended main agent never reads external content itself and receives only the subagent's mapped cache, which breaks the core assumption behind defenses such as Meta-SecAlign.
- A low-rank target-side cache correction (the cache guard), with appendices covering prompt leakage through mapped caches, mapper backdoors, and capability loss under repeated handoffs.

### Evaluation
- Setup: three model families (Qwen3, Llama 3, Ministral 3, including de-aligned checkpoints), four mapper types (NVIDIA's closed-form ridge mapper and its MLP variant, CacheBridge, the SwaT renderer), and two calibration corpora, for 24 settings. The prompt-injection target is fixed to Meta-SecAlign-8B.
- Datasets: 240 HarmBench text behaviors, 313 StrongREJECT direct requests, 250 XSTest safe prompts for over-refusal, and MMLU and GSM8K for capability.
- Results: a target that follows only 0.4% of benchmark injections natively follows up to 44.2% through a mapper. Transfer alone raises attack success by up to 24.6 pp within a family, zero-shot attacks gain up to 69.2 pp, and the untrusted-source attack succeeds in all 24 settings. Suffixes optimized through the mapper reach 92.5%, against 37.5% for ordinary GCG on the same target.
- Defense: the cache guard brings attacker-controlled sources within 4.5 points of native HarmBench attack success across all families, but a costly adaptive attack that fine-tunes the source model still succeeds.
- Credibility: every attack is compared against native inference with the same template, decoding settings, and seeds, which is a clean baseline. The authors acknowledge that the model-switching attack round-trips the cache with no tokens generated in between, which may overstate its effect relative to real multi-turn sessions, and that the injection experiments reuse mappers fitted for the undefended Llama3.1-8B.

#### Comment
- KV cache transfer already exists in vLLM, LMCache, and disaggregated serving, and mapped prefill is starting to appear as a hosted service, so this is an early look at what goes wrong when "who read it" and "who answers" come apart. The scenario of a coding agent delegating web-page reading to a cheaper subagent is especially realistic.
- No code link was found in the paper. It is a preprint with no venue yet.

# Poisoning Self-Evolving Security Policies in LLM Agents
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Miami University (Ohio)
> 2026

Self-evolving defenses, which learn reusable security policies from observed attacks, have emerged as one direction in LLM-agent security. The authors show with SED (Self-Evolving Defense) on the AgentDojo benchmark that this learning process is itself an attack surface. The attacker never touches SED's memory or policies directly; it simply hides a membership-change instruction in untrusted content. If the agent follows it, SED correctly flags the trajectory as harmful, but at policy-synthesis time it learns an over-broad rule that blocks "membership changes" in general rather than "membership changes requested by untrusted content", and from then on it refuses legitimate invitation and add-member requests. The key point is that this is an availability attack that makes the defense too strict, not a bypass.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33385
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33385
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33385
- Author repository (code): https://github.com/MN-NR/PSED

### Contribution
- Shows that a single successful prompt-injection episode makes SED learn an over-broad policy that refuses legitimate work while still blocking the original attack.
- Identifies "self-poisoning": even without an attacker, SED's judge sometimes labels legitimate but security-sensitive behavior as harmful and learns restrictive policies from it.
- Reports that five integrity attacks all fail in the evaluated settings (planting a payload in a policy, flooding memory, manipulating policy organization, fooling the judge with forged evidence, and splitting a harmful outcome across benign-looking steps), which narrows where the real weakness lies.
- Proposes contrastive policy synthesis with a matched benign example (borrowing Membrane's idea) and an automatic broadness-check gate that tests a new policy on related legitimate tasks before accepting it.

### Evaluation
- Setup: Nemotron and gpt-oss-120b as protected agents, with deepseek-v4p1-flash fixed as SED's judge and policy generator, on AgentDojo's Slack and banking environments.
- Results: across 10 Nemotron repetitions, legitimate membership and invitation tasks dropped from 50/50 (100%) to 18/50 (36%) after poisoning, and all 10 poisoned runs refused at least one legitimate request (0/10 for clean runs). Unrelated tasks stayed at 100% and the original injection remained blocked. One poisoned episode was enough to saturate the effect.
- Boundary: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, GLM-5.3, and Kimi-K3 did not follow the underlying injections (0/8), so the poisoning pathway never started.
- Mitigation: in a separate batch, poisoned legitimate-task success was 52% for Nemotron and 44% for gpt-oss; a matched counterexample raised this to 82% and 72%, and the broadness-check gate to 88% and 60%. Neither restored the clean 100%, and injection success was 0/60 in every condition.
- Credibility: the two batches measuring the same quantity gave 36% and 52%; the authors note that their Wilson intervals (24–50% and 38–65%) overlap and lean on per-repetition counts as the more stable measure. The task set is small (five legitimate and four unrelated tasks), and only one defense, SED, is studied.

#### Comment
- A small but clean demonstration that the more an agent defense learns from attacks, the more its learning inputs become an attack surface. Reporting the failed attacks alongside the successful one makes the claim more credible by narrowing its scope.
- The attack requires a protected agent that is susceptible to the underlying injection, so the threat may shrink with newer models. Sensitivity to the choice of judge and policy-synthesis model is not studied.
- It is a preprint with no venue yet.

# Benchmarking Open-Ended Vulnerability Discovery in Windows Kernel Binaries
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
> 2026

Frontier LLMs are now used to find real vulnerabilities, yet most security benchmarks still revolve around known bugs in open-source projects. The authors built DeepBinary, a benchmark that measures how well agents find vulnerabilities, with no hint of where they lie, in closed-source binaries, specifically Windows kernel drivers. The agent analyzes the target binary using only a disassembler and a kernel debugger on a network-isolated pair of VMs, and reports each bug with a crashing proof of concept (PoC) and a root-cause analysis. A judge replays every PoC on fresh VMs and uses a single LLM query to decide validity, uniqueness, and impact (estimated as a bug bounty value). A patch-diffing system, WinDiffer, dates each target's later fixes to check whether a model could have seen the fix during training.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33486
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33486
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33486
- Project repository: https://github.com/DeepBinary/DeepBinary

### Contribution
- Makes open-ended discovery of unknown bugs in closed-source kernel binaries measurable for the first time. Every agent gets the same isolated environment, disassembler, and kernel debugger, and every reported crash is replayed on fresh machines.
- A judge that rules on validity, duplicates, and severity without an answer key, reproducing each PoC as a standard user on four fresh VM pairs, including pairs with Driver Verifier enabled.
- An evaluation of seven frontier models in three coding agents that yields 146 Windows kernel 0-day defects. The authors state that several AI labs already use the benchmark to evaluate new cyber models.

### Evaluation
- Scale: 12 target binaries, 204 campaigns, 4,119 agent-hours, and $53.5K in token costs. Models include four closed-weight ones (GPT-6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, and others) and three open-weight ones (including DeepSeek V4.1 Flash); agents include Codex and Claude Code.
- Results: bugs were found in all 12 targets, with more than 200 vulnerabilities worth an estimated $661,000 in total bounties. PoCs for 146 distinct defects still crashed a Windows build released nine months later, which the authors call 0-days as of that build.
- Model gap: open-weight models found 13 to 19 unique bugs each, versus 25 to 116 for the closed-weight flagships. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash had the lowest cost per unique bug. GPT-6 Sol found 43 more bugs in its last 1M output tokens, up from 22 in the preceding 1M.
- Tooling: relative to Ghidra with symbols, removing the disassembler server cut unique bugs by 67% and removing symbols by 37%, while IDA Pro or Binary Ninja yielded about a fifth more reports.
- Judge: it rejected all 30 reports that paired a real crash with a wrong root cause and matched vendor impact labels 95% of the time. The final numbers do, however, include human corrections to 10 RQ1 verdicts and 54 RQ2 verdicts.
- Credibility: bounty values are estimates from the program's award table by impact class, not actual payouts. Cost limited each model-target pair to a single campaign, and only one Windows build is covered. The authors themselves flag that up to 27 of 248 findings (11%) could reflect prior exposure to a public update.

#### Comment
- This is likely to be influential as the first large-scale result that directly asks whether agents find real 0-days, combining binary-only analysis, long-horizon exploration, and replay-based judging. The finding that in 77 of 110 cases another model had inspected the same function and still never reported the bug is particularly interesting.
- Within what I read, I could not confirm whether the 0-days were reported to Microsoft or what the status of any report is.
- The judge model (GPT-6 Sol) is also one of the evaluated models, which leaves room for self-preference bias. It is a preprint with no venue yet.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# BridgeHammer: DNN Backdoor Attack via CPU-iGPU Shared-memory Rowhammer
> Accepted at DATE 2027 (Design, Automation and Test in Europe), posted on arXiv. Northeastern University
> 2026

On heterogeneous SoCs where the CPU and the integrated GPU (iGPU) share physical memory, the iGPU reads model weights loaded by the host directly, without a copy (zero-copy). The authors point out that this shared path lets memory corruption induced from the CPU side break the integrity of a model running on the iGPU. BridgeHammer uses CPU-side Rowhammer to flip specific weight bits and implant a backdoor that misclassifies only trigger-bearing inputs into the attacker's chosen class. The crux is coordinating "where" and "when": memory massaging, which moves the target weight pages into vulnerable DRAM locations, must happen after model compilation but before zero-copy weight binding, and hammering must happen after binding but before inference dispatch. To hit both windows, the attacker watches the victim's progress with Flush+Reload, pauses it with SIGSTOP at each point, and resumes it with SIGCONT after acting.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34684
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.34684
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34684
- Author repository (code and replication manual): https://github.com/Yufei-Wang99/BridgeHammer

### Contribution
- The first demonstration of a cross CPU–iGPU Rowhammer backdoor, in which a CPU-side attacker precisely corrupts the weights consumed by the iGPU.
- An analysis of the CPU-to-iGPU deployment lifecycle and a way to coordinate weight placement and fault injection at two distinct stages using instruction monitoring and signal-based synchronization.
- An implemented software defense that recomputes a SHA-256 digest of the weight buffers right before inference, with its cost measured.

### Evaluation
- Setup: Intel Core i7-8700, Ubuntu 22.04, OpenVINO. Six configurations: ResNet-20, VGG-16, and ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10, each in INT8 and FP32. The offline phase (DRAM fault profiling and backdoor construction) follows the earlier ROBBIN work.
- Results: target-page placement and bit-flip reproduction were 100% accurate in all six configurations. Attack success ranged from 84.4% to 90.2% (88.9% on average) and clean accuracy from 82.8% to 91.5% (86.3% on average), with 18 to 342 target pages and 70 to 1,701 bit flips.
- Synchronization: the delay from detecting the monitored instruction to sending SIGSTOP was 7.55 to 25.51 µs, and every trial suspended the victim in time at both breakpoints.
- Defense cost: median digest-check overhead was 2.42 ms for ResNet-20 INT8, 111 ms for VGG-16 INT8, and 206 ms for ResNet-50 INT8.
- Credibility: clean accuracy drops by up to 11 points from the baseline (ResNet-50 FP32, from 96.1% to 85.1%), so the stealth claim of preserving normal accuracy is weak in some configurations. Only one Intel desktop platform is tested; Jetson and AMD APUs are left for future work.

#### Comment
- The attacker must be able to send SIGSTOP and SIGCONT to the victim process and monitor instructions in its shared library with Flush+Reload, which assumes co-residence at roughly the same user privilege level. How often that holds in real services deserves separate scrutiny.
- Unified memory is also standard on mobile and edge SoCs, so if the promised Jetson experiments materialize, the result could matter for mobile devices too.
- DATE is a major design-automation conference, and this is a 7-page paper. I confirmed that the code and replication repository exists.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# RegionAlign: Manipulating Parameter Layouts for Localized Fault Injection on LLMs and VLMs
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Radboud University, Xi'an Jiaotong University, University of Amsterdam
> 2026

Physical fault injection can flip stored weight bits and degrade LLMs and VLMs, but the vulnerable bits are usually scattered across memory, so a single localized injection rarely does much. The authors propose RegionAlign, a supply-chain attack in which a malicious model provider leaves the weight values untouched and changes only the parameter layout before release, concentrating vulnerable bits in one memory region. Because it uses channel permutations, the model is functionally identical in exact arithmetic and clean performance is nearly unchanged, so a user who only checks task performance is unlikely to notice. After deployment, an attacker with physical access injects a single fault into that region and causes far more degradation than the original layout would allow.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33370
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33370
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33370

### Contribution
- Links two threats that had been studied separately, model supply-chain attacks and post-deployment physical fault injection, through the parameter layout.
- A method that combines sensitivity-guided layout search with channel permutations to gather vulnerable bits into a target region without retraining or changing weight values, adapting channel permutations from structured sparsity to attention and quantization constraints.
- A real electromagnetic fault injection (EMFI) study on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor confirming that bit faults in DRAM-resident quantized weights are physically achievable and direction-agnostic.

### Evaluation
- Setup: INT8-quantized LLMs and VLMs, including Llama3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B, Gemma2-2B, Gemma3-1B, StableLM2-1.6B, and SmolLM2-1.7B. The original layout, the RegionAlign layout, and a randomly sampled admissible layout are compared under the same target regions, bit-flip budgets, and search budgets.
- Clean performance: MMLU accuracy and WikiText-2 perplexity were nearly identical across the three layouts (reported as min–max over 12 target regions and search runs).
- Attack effect: a single in-region flip raised StableLM2-1.6B's WikiText-2 perplexity from 11.53 to 68.29 under RegionAlign, while both baselines stayed near 11.5.
- Physical study: of 2,500 injections with a Riscure VC+ probe, 12 (about 0.5%) changed stored INT8 values, each as a burst of 8 to 112 values with 1 to 12 bits flipped per value, and flip directions split almost evenly (1,357 zero-to-one versus 1,239 one-to-zero). None of the attempts hit the pre-registered target tensor (layers.8.v_proj).
- Credibility: the attack effect is measured with software-injected bit flips, and since the physical experiment never hit the target region, no end-to-end physical attack is demonstrated. The authors interpret this as support for their claim that the region is what constrains the attacker.

#### Comment
- The observation that verification limited to "check clean performance" can miss a functionally equivalent tampering has clear implications for how model hubs distribute checkpoints. For the attack to be a real threat, though, it needs supply-chain control, physical access, and region-precise injection all at once.
- One co-author is at a Chinese institution (Xi'an Jiaotong University), so I checked further. Four of the six authors are at Radboud University, and the Xi'an Jiaotong co-author's existence was confirmed through a university faculty page and a personal homepage. The evaluation compares against the original and random layouts under equal budgets, which is an appropriate baseline, and I found no reason to think the numbers are implausibly good. No code link is given.
- It is a preprint with no venue yet.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# Demystifying Real-world LLM Service Freeloading
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China)
> 2026

Third-party projects increasingly take the free inference offered by LLM web apps such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek, and by dedicated clients such as Claude Code and Codex, and repackage it as standalone APIs. The authors define such use beyond a provider's permitted access, allocation, or redistribution conditions as "LLM service freeloading" (LSF) and measure it systematically: 513 open-source projects and 1,588 inference paths across 64 upstream LLM services, covering how the projects are developed, what they depend on, and how they work. They also analyze the terms of 62 upstream services to see which practices are prohibited.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33218
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33218
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33218

### Contribution
- A dataset of open-source LSF projects (513 projects, 1,588 inference paths, 64 upstream services).
- An analysis of project growth, code reuse, development activity, popularity, and upstream dependencies, plus five categories of provider restrictions (automated access, redistribution, limit circumvention, account sharing, and multiple accounts).
- A grouping of implementations into seven functional groups, with case studies showing how credential management, product workflow reconstruction, protocol adaptation, and account scheduling combine into an independent API.

### Evaluation
- Data collection: 100 candidates gathered by hand from GitHub, Google, Reddit, and LINUX DO; two researchers independently labeled them and selected 76 seed projects (Cohen's κ of 0.919 before adjudication). The set was then expanded with 24 GitHub queries and source-code analysis.
- Results: 93.0% of the repositories were created in 2026; the most-starred one has 75,841 stars and the top ten account for 89.0% of all stars. 1,828 shared commits were found across 90 projects. 264 projects (51.5%) use at least one of DeepSeek Web, Gemini Web, Qwen Web, or ChatGPT Web.
- Mechanisms: 83.2% of paths (1,322) are direct HTTP or WebSocket requests that reproduce product protocols, 50.0% reconstruct prompts or conversation histories, 210 emulate tool calls by turning generated text into structured responses, and account selection or rotation appears on 304 paths (19.1%).
- Terms: 58 of the 62 services with available terms explicitly prohibit at least one examined practice; automated access is restricted by 57 (91.9%) and redistribution by 50 (80.6%).
- Credibility: as a measurement study, the reliability of the collection process matters more than baselines; the authors report inter-annotator agreement for seed selection and say they manually reviewed every included item. They also acknowledge possible omissions, since only public GitHub and three downstream API families are covered.

#### Comment
- Because this comes from a Chinese institution, I checked further. All authors are listed with HUST institutional email addresses, and the last author Haoyu Wang's personal homepage turned up in a search. Inflated performance metrics are not a concern for a measurement study, and the paper states its agreement figures and limitations concretely. However, no dataset link is given; the authors only say release will follow a review of sensitive operational details, so the work cannot yet be reproduced.
- The authors say they disclosed their findings to the affected providers. The pattern of draining free allowances through account rotation is directly relevant to how providers design abuse detection.
- It is a preprint with no venue yet.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# FlashProve-Next: Verifiable Inference for Production LLM APIs
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Independent researcher (sole author)
> 2026

Clients of closed-weight LLM APIs have no way to check whether a provider actually ran the advertised model, precision, hidden prompt, and reasoning budget. Existing approaches either trust hardware or third parties, or re-prove every layer of an integer surrogate model, which is expensive and diverges from native floating-point serving. FlashProve-Next claims to produce zero-knowledge-masked proofs for individual API responses without changing how serving works (bf16/fp8/mxfp4, random sampling, FlashAttention, CUDA graphs, batching, prefix caching) and without revealing weights, hidden prompts, hidden reasoning, or activations. What the proof attests is not "which GPU ran what" but "the provider holds a computation trace consistent with its committed configuration", and cost-weighted spot-checking guarantees probabilistically that the fraction of computation outside floating-point tolerance stays below a public threshold.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33741
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.33741
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33741
- Author repository (code, specifications, Lean 4 modules, Rust verifier): https://github.com/s-JoL/FlashProve-Next
- Paper PDF in the author repository: https://github.com/s-JoL/FlashProve-Next/blob/main/paper/flashprove-next.pdf

### Contribution
- A versioned API response relation covering request encoding, hidden prompt injection, tool calls, and multi-turn state.
- A static model commitment that commits deployed weights once with Reed–Solomon column trees and opens hidden columns inside proofs, giving zero knowledge that does not wear out with use.
- Per-operator rounding contracts that remove the integer surrogate, which the author argues avoids cases where an honest provider cannot produce a proof because of numerical drift (a failure that would itself leak information about hidden activations).
- Machine-checked proofs of key properties such as soundness in 28 Lean 4 modules (696 declarations, no sorry, standard axioms only), according to the author.

### Evaluation
- Calibration: across 1.85M GEMM/MoE blocks and 1.96M non-GEMM rows from five models on Blackwell GPUs, the author reports zero false rejections of honest execution.
- Proving cost: Qwen3-0.6B (ExtSeed-153 mode, 8 MiB proof) is proved in 450.2 s on GPU, all 14 cheating scenarios are caught, and the system scales to the 36-layer Qwen3-4B and a live vLLM Qwen3-30B-A3B-FP8 (1.49 s to prove and 23.4 ms to verify in Rust for one relation). The Rust verifier checks an 8 MiB proof in 10.1 ms on CPU.
- Credibility: every number was measured by the sole author, with no independent replication or peer review. Some of the core theorems are conditional on the assumption that kernels meet their rounding contracts and on an empirical approximation hypothesis, and the author states that certain cheats such as prefix collapse, as well as checkpoint backdoors, are out of scope. The paper cites the earlier FlashProve as taking 71–93 times an 8×H100 FP8 prefill, but within what I read I did not find a head-to-head comparison table under matched conditions.

#### Comment
- This tackles the practical question of whether a closed-model API really ran the advertised model, using public verification instead of a TEE, and releasing code, specifications, and machine-checked proofs is a real strength for reproducibility.
- It is a dense 36-page manuscript by a single independent researcher, with many self-coined terms and mode names (NI-315, ExtSeed-153, and others), so outside review is especially needed. No institutional affiliation is listed.
- I confirmed that the paper PDF in the author repository matches the arXiv abstract in title, author, and key figures (450.2 s, 14/14, 10.1 ms).
- Full text source: paper PDF in the author repository (I read the abstract, introduction, contributions, parts of the body, and the conclusion).
