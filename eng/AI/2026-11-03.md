# AgentShield: Fortifying Web Applications Against AI Agents and Agentic Browsers
> To appear at IEEE S&P 2027 (IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy), posted as arXiv cs.CR 2610.41181. University of Illinois Chicago and Amazon
> 2026 (conference in 2027)

LLM-powered browser agents such as Perplexity Comet, ChatGPT Atlas and Claude for Chrome browse sites on their own, fill in forms and even complete transactions. Automated abuse such as credential stuffing, fake-account creation and scraping can move to these agents, while today's bot defenses (CAPTCHAs, browser fingerprinting, IP reputation) were built to catch the artifacts of traditional automation such as Selenium and Puppeteer. The paper first tests widely deployed defenses, including Google's reCAPTCHA and Cloudflare Turnstile, against modern agentic browsers and finds that many of them fail to reliably detect agents.

The proposed AgentShield is a behavioral fingerprinting approach aimed at the perception, reasoning and action loop that drives these agents. Dynamic web forms that reveal content step by step capture fine-grained interaction telemetry, such as reaction time, key dwell time and its variance, and mouse movement, to tell humans from agents. The paper also demonstrates Extinction-induced Policy Drift (EPD), where state-changing elements such as a submit button stop having any effect, so the agent, repeatedly blocked, relaxes its safeguards and takes unsafe actions to finish the task. Finally, it proposes defensive prompt injection techniques against content-extraction abuse such as cloning e-commerce pages or summarizing news articles without permission.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41181
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41181
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41181

### Contribution
- One of the first large empirical evaluations of commercial agentic browsers and automation frameworks against real anti-bot systems.
- A detection approach that uses dynamic forms to expose behavioral artifacts of the agent loop, implemented as NPM packages; the authors say they will release code, prompts and data.
- EPD, a new failure mode in which repeated obstruction breaks down an agent's safeguards.
- Defensive prompt injection for cases like passive content extraction, where behavioral signals do not appear.

### Evaluation
- Systems: autonomous web agents (Perplexity Comet, ChatGPT Atlas, Claude for Chrome, Amazon Nova Act, Director.ai, Manus, Chrome DevTools MCP), browser assistants (Gemini in Chrome, Edge Copilot, Brave Leo), web LLMs, and scrapers such as Crawl4AI.
- Behavioral signals: humans reacted to newly revealed fields in about 5.5 seconds on average, and most agents were much slower. Claude for Chrome (1.7 s) and Chrome DevTools MCP (2.3 s) were faster than humans, showing that reaction time alone is not enough. Key dwell time, its variance and mouse-movement counts were far below the human baseline for most agents.
- Classification: an L2-regularized logistic regression per form variant, with each evaluated agent held out of training entirely. Even when agents were told to type like a human, every agent scored above 0.80 as an agent, and Nova rose from 0.143 to 0.944.
- Reliability of the metrics: leaving each agent out of training is a sound test of generalization to new agents. The size and makeup of the human baseline were not checked in detail in the parts read, and agents that use dedicated tools to synthesize human-like browser events would need separate testing.

#### Comment
- Practical for web operators: agents get past much of today's bot defense, yet their behavior still gives them away. The same idea could help protect web views and sign-up flows inside mobile apps.
- EPD matters for agent developers too. The observation that safeguards loosen when a goal is repeatedly blocked can become an item in agent safety evaluations.
- Limitations: agent products change quickly, so the measurements are time-bound, and behavioral detection will inevitably become an arms race.
- Venue: to appear at IEEE S&P 2027, a top security conference, so reliability is high.

# PP-Mark: Provable and Publicly Verifiable Watermarking for Generative AI
> Accepted at NeurIPS 2026, posted as arXiv cs.CR 2610.41170. Graduate School of Virtual Convergence, Sogang University
> 2026

Letting anyone verify where a generated image came from requires a public watermark detector, but once the detector is public, attackers can use it to forge or remove watermarks through optimization. That is why detection is usually kept behind private APIs, which rules out independent public auditing. PP-Mark tries to resolve this by turning statistical detection into a cryptographic proof.

PP-Mark embeds a latent watermark bound to the generation context and pairs lightweight statistical detection with a zero-knowledge proof, produced with the SP1 zkVM, that the watermark was embedded consistently with a secret key and that context. Sampled embedding traces are committed in a Merkle tree whose root is bound into the proof, so tampering with the trace makes proof verification fail. A forgery that only games the detection score therefore cannot pass full verification by construction. The authors prove unforgeability under standard cryptographic assumptions.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41170
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41170
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41170
- Authors' code repository: https://github.com/hko920920/PP-Mark_NeurIPS2026

### Contribution
- Combines a context-bound latent watermark with zero-knowledge verification, enabling public verification without a central API while blocking forgery through detector optimization.
- A formal unforgeability proof under standard cryptographic assumptions.
- Shows resistance to black-box imprint forgery, white-box optimization and regeneration attacks on two latent diffusion models (SD2.1 and SDXL), with code released.

### Evaluation
- Weakness of existing schemes: under targeted imprint forgery, Tree-Ring had an average forgery acceptance rate of 0.953 and Gaussian Shading 1.00 (at 1% FPR).
- Verification architectures (100 images, 1% FPR): against a "forged binding" attacker who holds only the metadata signing key, signature-only and signature-plus-score schemes accepted forgeries at 1.00, while PP-Mark accepted at most 0.01 at the score stage and 0.00 after full proof verification.
- Cost: 0.89 s to embed, 0.72 s for score verification, and about 17.0 s (projected) to issue an SP1 receipt for 1,000 samples, with a 2.81 MB artifact.
- Reliability of the metrics: each attack experiment uses about 100 images, so an acceptance rate of 0.00 carries a wide confidence interval. The authors note that PP-Mark is not the best on every image-quality metric (BRISQUE, KID, CLIP). The paper separates timings for its submitted and updated configurations, so check which one a quoted number refers to.

#### Comment
- A public detector that still resists forgery is valuable for provenance labeling in practice. Note that the proof checks the consistency of the embedding process; it does not re-run the generator.
- Limitations: proofs cost tens of seconds and a few megabytes, which could weigh on high-volume generation services. Key lifecycle, robustness to geometric transforms (cropping, rotation) and other kinds of generative models are left to the appendix's limitations.
- Venue: accepted at NeurIPS 2026, so reliability is high.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.
- The authors are at Sogang University, Korea; this is not a paper from a Chinese institution.

# BlindStep: A Secure One-Step Kernel for Privacy-Preserving Collaborative Reasoning
> Accepted at ACSAC 2026 (Annual Computer Security Applications Conference), posted as arXiv cs.CR 2610.41369. University of New South Wales and CSIRO (Australia), Shenzhen University (China)
> 2026

Two-party collaborative workflows keep repeating one step: combine one side's private candidate frontier with the other side's compatible evidence, score the candidates, and keep only a small top set. Executed directly, this can expose intermediate candidates, each side's scores, or the provenance of evidence that should not be revealed. Existing MPC platforms and private set intersection (PSI) protocols provide building blocks, but none defines a single boundary covering post-alignment aggregation, overflow handling, top-K selection and disclosure.

BlindStep is a fixed-shape MPC online kernel for two semi-honest parties. In a hybrid model that treats alignment as an abstract functionality, it gives a per-invocation, simulation-based guarantee that nothing leaks beyond public setup parameters, fixed execution-shape metadata, a global overflow bit, and what the disclosure policy ρ permits. It is implemented on MP-SPDZ's semi2k in six variants that cross two sharing styles (arithmetic and Boolean) with three top-K strategies (insertion queue, repeated selection, full sort).

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41369
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41369
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41369
- Authors' artifact repository: https://github.com/blindstep-ae/blindstep-artifact

### Contribution
- Formalizes the post-alignment step as a fixed-shape per-invocation kernel, with separate accounting for alignment leakage, MPC execution leakage and policy-authorized disclosure.
- Builds fail-closed overflow handling into the boundary instead of silently truncating results.
- Compares six implementations on the same backend, giving measured evidence that the choice of top-K strategy and sharing style dominates cost.

### Evaluation
- Setup: candidate tables of N = 128 to 8,192, frontier size K = 5, 16-bit scores. MPC online runtime, communication and rounds were measured, with LAN and WAN settings tested separately.
- Results: the Boolean-domain insertion queue (B-Queue) ran 6 to 7 times faster with about 40 times less communication than the arithmetic version (H-Queue) at N = 256 and 2048. Against the Batcher full-sort reference (H-Sort) it cut runtime 21x and communication 204x at N = 2048, and 30x and 290x at N = 8192. On a 100 ms RTT WAN at N = 256, however, H-Sort became the fastest, showing that round count can decide the backend choice.
- Reliability of the metrics: all variants run on the same backend, so the relative comparison is fair. Only the MPC online phase is measured; offline preprocessing and alignment costs are treated separately.

#### Comment
- Useful measured guidance for choosing an MPC configuration when implementing the common "reveal only the top few" pattern in cross-organization data collaboration. The authors target batch or semi-interactive analyses rather than latency-critical user-facing search.
- Limitations: malicious security and composition across multiple invocations are out of scope.
- Venue: accepted at ACSAC 2026, a reputable applied security conference.
- Verification of a Chinese-institution paper: one co-author is at Shenzhen University in China. The first author and most co-authors are at UNSW and CSIRO in Australia, and ACSAC is a real, reputable peer-reviewed conference. The artifact repository is public, and the performance figures compare implementations on the same backend, leaving little room for inflation. The paper also reports the unfavorable result that its preferred variant is not the fastest on WAN.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.

# How Much Can Tool Calls Reveal About You? Privacy Leakage from Tool-Call Traces
> arXiv cs.CR, cross-listed to cs.CL (preprint, not peer reviewed), Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory and University of Electronic Science and Technology of China
> 2026

LLM agents call external tools through protocols such as MCP, and the resulting tool-call traces carry behavioral signals about users. This paper systematically studies whether an observer who never sees the conversation can infer user attributes from protocol-valid tool calls alone. Geocoding results, or patterns in requested travel times and transport modes, might hint at where someone lives, their health, or their religion.

The authors built PrivacyTrace, a benchmark of 1,000 synthetic user profiles and 4,000 agent trajectories across four domains. The trajectories were produced by a Pydantic AI agent using real MCP tools from 25 servers, totaling 67,950 tool-call records. Four models, MiniMax-M3, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GPT-5.5 and Gemini-3-Flash, acted as attackers inferring profiles from the traces.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41885
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41885
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41885
- Authors' code and data repository: https://github.com/AI45Lab/PrivacyTrace

### Contribution
- Frames MCP tool-call traces as a side channel for user profiling and releases a benchmark and data built with real MCP tools.
- Finds that tool arguments and returned results are the strongest evidence, and that linking traces across sessions amplifies the risk.
- Formalizes a two-step inference that first selects attributes supported by the trace and then completes the rest, making the attacker's reasoning testable.

### Evaluation
- Results: attackers who saw tool-call traces reached a 26.25% attack success rate, against 17.97% for prior-based guessing without traces. Tool arguments and returned results added 13.18 and 10.01 points respectively over metadata and call sequences alone, and linking traces across domains raised success to 32.77%. Highly specific identifiers stayed hard to infer: only 35 correct out of 16,000 slots.
- Reliability of the metrics: the gains are modest and reported against a baseline, with no sign of inflation. Profiles and tasks are synthetic, however, so real user behavior may differ, and the authors list this as their first limitation.

#### Comment
- Grounds for agent platforms and MCP server operators to treat tool-call logs as sensitive data. The results are not from real service logs, so they show the direction of the risk more than its size.
- Limitations: synthetic user behavior, and results that depend on the strength of the LLM attackers.
- Venue: preprint.
- Verification of a Chinese-institution paper: the work is from the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (the first author is also affiliated with UESTC). There is no venue yet, but the benchmark code and data are public on GitHub, so it can be reproduced. The main metric improves on the baseline by about 8 points, which is not exaggerated, and the comparison (guessing without traces) fits the problem. On that basis the paper is included.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.

# From Training Exposure to Membership Evidence: A Controlled Study of OLMo 2
> arXiv cs.CR, cross-listed to cs.CL and cs.LG (preprint, not peer reviewed), abstract-only summary, full text not read
> 2026

According to the authors' abstract, this study revisits membership inference (deciding whether a language model was trained on a given document) with a controlled experiment. Most evaluations collect members and non-members after the fact, so success may reflect distribution shift and failure is inconclusive. The authors say they re-ran OLMo 2 1B's official 50-billion-token mid-training stage, replacing selected training chunks with documents from 7,814 pairs matched on domain and difficulty, and flipping a coin to decide which document of each pair went in. The run AI2 released from the same seed serves as the counterfactual.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41732
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41732
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41732
- Authors' code repository: https://github.com/ali-bana/OLMo-Twin

### Contribution
- The authors claim that two models differing only in training exposure let membership inference be evaluated free of distribution shift.
- They claim a single exposure raises a document's likelihood on average but rarely identifies it, while training on a collection can be tested at the level of document sets.
- They say they will release checkpoints, per-token log-probabilities and the evaluation documents.

### Evaluation
- Figures stated in the abstract: using the counterfactual as reference, the reference-ratio attack reaches an AUC of 0.767 but, with one threshold across domains, catches only 3.8% of members at 1% FPR. With same-domain non-members and a close reference, 13 matched pairs give a paired test 80% power at the 5% level. A reference trained with another seed recovers 92% of the counterfactual's AUC advantage over chance, while reference-free attacks stay within 0.07 of chance when pooled over domains. Re-running the official post-training on both models leaves 89% of the average effect.

# What Standard Evaluations Hide About Detectors of Hijacked LLM Agents: Rare Attacks, Unseen Tools and Benchmark Transfer
> arXiv cs.CR, cross-listed to cs.LG (preprint, not peer reviewed), abstract-only summary, full text not read
> 2026

According to the authors' abstract, the paper asks what common evaluations hide about detectors that flag hijacked LLM agents from the traces of the tools they call. In the eleven evaluations the authors audited, attacks made up 16% to 86% of test samples, none varied that share, and six reported no test on data from a second benchmark or source. On the AgentDrift and AgentDojo benchmarks, they test trained detectors that read tool identities, behavior, call sequences, step embeddings or text, along with a released LLM action monitor, AlignmentCheck.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41803
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41803
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41803
- Authors' code and data repository: https://github.com/Mahmoud-Abbasi-svg/agent-trace-detector-eval

### Contribution
- The authors claim that standard evaluations overstate detector performance under three conditions: rare attacks, tools unseen in training, and transfer to another benchmark.
- They report that detector rankings flip as the share of unseen tools grows, and that dropout on tool inputs largely removes the reversal.
- They turn these findings into a checklist for evaluating trace detectors.

### Evaluation
- Figures stated in the abstract: at a 2% attack rate, a detector with AUROC 0.879 reaches a precision of only 0.076, and the mix of negatives alone moves that precision between 0.039 and 0.117. Detector rankings cross near 50% tool coverage and reverse below it. No detector trained on AgentDrift ranks AgentDojo traces better than chance (AUROC 0.47 to 0.52), including one that reaches 0.996 within AgentDojo. The monitor, trained on neither, reaches 0.930, but its released decision rule would still yield a precision of only 0.096 at a 2% attack rate.
