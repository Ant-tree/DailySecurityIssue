# Securing Multi-Agent Systems by Design
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Duke University
> 2026

In a multi-agent system, a manager agent splits a task into subtasks, delegates them to agents and aggregates their responses into a final answer. This paper studies what happens when some of those agents are malicious and send crafted responses to steer the result, and how to defend against it. On the attack side, ReMA (response manipulation attacks) iteratively refines malicious responses using feedback from a surrogate multi-agent system, either to make the final answer wrong (untargeted) or to make it match an attacker-chosen answer (targeted). The response generator, the surrogate manager and the attack critic are all LLMs, and the procedure is adjusted for eight threat models depending on whether the attacker knows the benign agents' responses, the aggregation rule and the manager's backbone LLM. On the defense side, SecMAS is an aggregation rule that embeds the responses within an agent group and selects the one most similar to a sufficiently large subset of the others, on the intuition that benign responses cluster around the correct answer when benign agents are the majority.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17883
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17883
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17883

### Contribution
- Formulates response manipulation in multi-agent systems as an optimization problem and proposes ReMA, an iterative attack that works under black-box conditions. Unlike the existing optimization-based attack (ObliInjection), which needs white-box access, ReMA still works when the attacker knows nothing about the system.
- Proposes SecMAS, which picks the majority-consistent response by embedding similarity, and proves probabilistic security guarantees against untargeted and targeted attacks, assuming a bound on malicious agents per group and that benign response embeddings concentrate near the ground truth.
- Evaluates the defense against adaptive variants of ReMA designed specifically against SecMAS.

### Evaluation
- Tasks: in the five application domains from prior work TAMAS (education, finance, healthcare, legal, news), the authors generated tasks with ground-truth solutions using GPT-5.6-Sol and verified the solutions with Claude Opus 5, keeping 100 per domain, 500 in total.
- Systems: one manager plus four role-specific groups per domain, 10 agents per group by default. Each agent is randomly assigned AutoGen or CrewAI and one of Gemma-4-31B, Qwen-3.8-27B, Llama-4-Scout or three GPT-5.6 variants.
- Baselines: attacks TAMAS, MAST, AiTM and ObliInjection; aggregation rules LLM summarization, two CP-WBFT variants, DecentLLMs, and DeSummary (prompt injection detection used as an aggregation rule).
- Metrics and results: attack success rate (ASR, judged by GPT-5.6-Sol) and an embedding-cosine attack success value (ASV). Against existing rules, ReMA reaches ASR up to 1.00 when the attacker knows everything, and still 0.61 untargeted and 0.49 targeted against summarization in the news domain when it knows nothing. SecMAS keeps ASR near 0.00, comparable to the no-attack level, for every attack and threat model. Without attacks its utility matches the existing rules (about 0.89 averaged over five runs; all Welch t-test p-values above 0.05).
- Reliability: the weak spot is that both ground truth and success judgments come from LLMs, and the same model family (GPT-5.6-Sol) generates tasks, solutions and attacks and also judges success, which leaves room for bias.

#### Comment
- The guarantee only holds when malicious agents are a minority within a group, so it weakens if agents come from a single provider or share a vulnerable model and the benign ones fail together. The authors themselves note that stronger adaptive attacks may succeed when benign responses vary widely in quality.
- The defense assumes tasks with one convergent answer, so it does not carry over directly to creative or open-ended tasks. For open agent ecosystems that pull in third-party agents, though, "majority vote in embedding space" is a practical defense.
- The corresponding author, Neil Gong, is known for work on robust aggregation in federated learning and on prompt injection, and SecMAS can be read as Byzantine-robust aggregation carried over to agent responses. No code link was found in the paper.

# Forget the Lineage: Forward-Secure, Lineage-Complete Cryptographic Erasure for LLM-Agent Memory
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), submitted to the Journal of Information Security and Applications. Efrei Research Lab (Université Paris-Panthéon-Assas), Cédric laboratory at Conservatoire national des arts et métiers (Cnam)
> 2026

This paper proposes a way to cryptographically erase what a user asked an LLM agent to forget, including every summary, extracted fact and embedding derived from it. Because the model itself writes the agent's long-term memory, deleting the original message leaves its derivatives, storage residue and backups behind; reading the code, the authors found that mem0 2.2.1 even keeps the old text of a deleted memory in its history table. They define lineage-complete forward-secure erasure: after a forget request, an adversary who later obtains all storage and the agent's current keys learns nothing about the forgotten input or anything derived from it. Their system, LinEra, encrypts each memory under a key derived from its parents' keys along the derivation graph, with source keys coming from a GGM-tree puncturable pseudorandom function. Forgetting is a single puncture, and every descendant becomes unreadable without having to be located.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18133
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.18133
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18133
- Authors' code and LLM-call log repository: https://github.com/yulliwasameur/linera
- Archived code (Zenodo): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24166476

### Contribution
- A formal security goal for agent memory deletion combining lineage completeness and forward security, against an adversary holding all storage and current keys, such as an honest-but-curious cloud host or backup operator.
- A key graph and an encrypted vector index in which keys follow the derivation graph, so one puncture erases a whole closure. A sound lineage rule records everything the writer saw as parents, which confines a source's influence to its descendants. Security and lineage integrity are proven.
- Integration into mem0 through an adapter of about 140 lines, measured on memory written by real LLMs.
- A simple law that predicts the forgetting radius (the share of derived memories erased) from the writer's fan-in, and the observation that writing memory in short windows keeps both deletion cost and answer quality in check.

### Evaluation
- Data and models: 128 LoCoMo sessions written by Qwen2.5-1.5B (4-bit, CPU), two conversations replayed with Llama-3.2-3B, all ten LoCoMo conversations (272 sessions, 5,953 turns) with Qwen2.5-7B on one H100, and a LongMemEval-S subset. Secrets (canaries) were planted in conversations, forgotten, and then attacked.
- Baselines: logical deletion, per-object keys (Holepunch-style), epoch keys, and a rule that records only part of the lineage.
- Results: on the ten conversations written by the largest model, the attacker recovers 70 of 71 forgotten secrets after logical deletion, 67 with per-object or epoch keys, and none with LinEra. The same holds for every writer tested.
- Cost: with session-level summaries, forgetting 1% of turns erases 73.0% of derived memories (the law predicts 77.2%). With four-turn windows only 3.8% is erased, and answer quality (token F1) is actually higher. Lost memories can be restored by re-summarizing only the affected sessions or windows.
- Reliability: models are 7B or smaller, and some experiments use small question samples (60 and 16), so the authors ask readers to interpret only the direction of some results. Code and LLM-call logs are public, which helps reproducibility.

#### Comment
- The experiments make it clear that deleting the original message is not enough when the right to erasure meets agent memory. Small models in particular tend to copy secrets verbatim into summaries, so encryption without lineage leaks most of them.
- Limitations: model weights, RAM at the time of deletion and answers already sent out are out of scope. The GGM puncturable PRF is only selectively secure, as the authors note. The sound lineage rule treats everything retrieved into context as a parent, which can make the forgetting radius large.
- The paper is under submission; JISA is a mid-tier information security journal.

# Binarization as an Attack Surface in Weightless Neural Networks
> Published at ARTMAN 2026 (Trustworthy Machine Learning Workshop co-located with ACSAC 2026); arXiv version. Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro, Technion, Universidade Federal Fluminense, Elytron Cybersecurity
> 2026

This paper analyzes how robust weightless neural networks (WNNs) are to adversarial examples. WNNs binarize their inputs and learn by storing binary patterns in lookup memories, which makes them light and easy to implement in hardware, and they are used in resource-constrained settings such as intrusion detection. Since a small perturbation that crosses no binarization threshold leaves the input unchanged, they are easily assumed to be naturally robust. The authors study the canonical WiSARD and two variants that replace its memories with Bloom filters, Bloom WiSARD and BTHOWeN. They formalize the path by which a continuous perturbation crosses a threshold, flips a binary tuple, redirects a memory lookup and changes the prediction, and then test attacks on MNIST and three intrusion detection datasets while switching between linear and data-driven Gaussian thermometer encodings.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17646
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17646
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17646
- Authors' code repository: https://github.com/lucassenosc/adversarialwnn

### Contribution
- A bound on how many thermometer bits a perturbation can flip (a threshold-crossing bound) and a robustness certificate for standard WiSARD based on the discriminator response margin.
- Controlled experiments that change only the encoding show that binarization resolution largely determines robustness: finer encoding barely changes clean accuracy but sharply raises attack success.
- Numbers from intrusion detection showing that accuracy alone says little about risk, and that missed attacks (malicious classified as normal) and false alarms (normal classified as malicious) must be reported separately.

### Evaluation
- MNIST: raising thermometer bits per input from 1 to 6 moves clean accuracy by less than half a point (94.5% to 95.0%) while the score-based Simulated Annealing attack's success rate rises from 62.0% to 95.0% (first 200 test images). BTHOWeN with Gaussian encoding falls from 95.2% clean accuracy to 10.1% under random L∞ noise, but with linear encoding it stays at 94.2% clean and 94.3% under the same noise.
- Intrusion detection: FGSM and random L2 and L∞ perturbations on Bot-IoT, CICIDS and UNSW-NB15, binary and multiclass. Under FGSM, standard WiSARD loses 35.89 accuracy points on average and misses 47.9% of malicious traffic. On CICIDS, the miss rate reaches 85 to 88% while accuracy stays high, and for Bloom WiSARD on CICIDS multiclass accuracy even rises after perturbation while 87.6% of attacks are missed.
- Reliability: perturbations are applied directly in normalized feature space, without checking whether the result corresponds to packets or flows that could actually be sent (problem-space constraints). The three intrusion detection datasets are old and have known quality issues, so generalizing to production traffic calls for caution.

#### Comment
- The paper overturns the intuition that binarization buys robustness and shows that encoding resolution itself is an attack surface. For lightweight intrusion detection with WNNs or FPGA-based classifiers, the practical lesson is to evaluate misses and false alarms separately rather than accuracy.
- This is a workshop paper co-located with ACSAC, not a main-conference paper. Code is public.
