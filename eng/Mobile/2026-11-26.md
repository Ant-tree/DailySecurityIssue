# Aftershock: Yesterday's Linux Kernel Bugs, Tomorrow's Android Exploits
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). UC Riverside
> 2026

Android kernels have always lagged behind upstream Linux, because a fix has to travel through LTS releases, the Android Common Kernel (ACK) and vendor firmware before it reaches a phone. The authors argue that this lag is no longer just a patch-management issue: with capable LLM agents, a public upstream patch becomes a blueprint for a working n-day exploit. They test the claim end to end. Their pipeline screens upstream patches for memory-safety bugs, finds affected ACK branches by patch bisection, reproduces each bug from an ordinary app (the untrusted_app SELinux domain) on a Cuttlefish virtual device, and then has an off-the-shelf LLM agent build a local privilege escalation (LPE) exploit. Every submitted APK is verified independently on a freshly booted instance with production mitigations such as KASLR, PAN, PXN and CFI enabled, and without agent access.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31547
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31547
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31547

### Contribution
- An end-to-end measurement pipeline for Android kernel n-days, from upstream patch screening and app-context reproduction to independent verification. No pre-built reproducer or exploitation hints are supplied; the prompt only describes the environment, the tools and the goal.
- Measurements of per-exploit reliability and of how well exploits adapt across kernel revisions and from virtual devices to real Pixel phones.
- A trace of how long upstream fixes take to reach GKI branches and Pixel firmware, which puts numbers on the defender's timescale.
- A code-level analysis of all 17 agent-written exploits compared with known Linux kernel techniques. The authors report two new strategies and three new techniques, for example disabling SELinux first and only then using debugging interfaces such as tracefs to locate targets.

### Evaluation
- Scope: 152 CVEs from a 33-month window starting January 2024 that entered runtime reproduction. The model was OpenAI's gpt-5.6-sol reasoning model at xhigh effort, with a $1,250 cost cap per experiment.
- Results: 38 of the 152 were reproduced from an ordinary app, and 17 produced independently verified LPE exploits. Successful cases took a median of 14.3 agent-hours and $321.07 in API cost from reproduction to verification.
- Reliability: across 170 fresh-instance trials the median per-exploit success rate was 90%, and 12 of 14 trials (85.7%) succeeded on physical Pixel 9 and Pixel 10 devices.
- Patch delay: for 201 patched CVE-branch pairs the median GKI delay was 21.5 days (at least 71.4 days once unpatched pairs are included), and fixes reached Pixel firmware after a median of 181 days. On their release dates, 64.1% of the evaluated Pixel factory images contained at least one studied vulnerability whose CVE was already public. Only 3 of the 17 exploited CVEs appeared in the Android Security Bulletin.
- Reliability of the numbers: success is not judged by capturing a flag alone; a separate LLM reviewer checks that the exploit really used the assigned vulnerability. Most runs ended by exhausting their budget, so 17 is a lower bound, and all results come from one model and one prompt design.

#### Comment
- The key result is the measured asymmetry: hours to turn a public patch into an exploit versus months to ship the fix. For mobile security teams it is a strong argument against judging risk from the Android Security Bulletin alone.
- The authors note that defenders can use the same pipeline to assess which upstream patches matter for Android. Whether and how much of the exploit code will be released was not stated in the paper.
- Preprint with no venue yet. Co-author Zhiyun Qian has a long record of top-tier papers on kernel and network security.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).

# Harvesting Apple's Orchard: A Security Analysis of On-Device CoreML Models Across the Apple Ecosystem
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). VIA Cybersecurity Lab, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Ghana
> 2026

Apple ships hundreds of CoreML models inside iOS, iPadOS, macOS and tvOS for features such as SMS spam filtering, charging prediction, suggestions and photo analysis. The authors treat these models as a security boundary on a par with system daemons: they consume untrusted input such as messages and media, and other components trust their output. The paper presents what it calls the first systematic analysis of this surface. The authors harvested 1,137 CoreML artifacts from six firmware images and two devices (an iPhone X on iOS 16.7 and a jailbroken iOS 26.1 virtual research device), then ran static analysis, recovered compiled bundles into loadable form, and tested accuracy and adversarial robustness, latency, weight tampering and crashes.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31688
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.31688
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31688
- Author repository (reproduction code and measurement records, no weights): https://github.com/jayluxferro/on-device-coreml-security

### Contribution
- A census of 320 model files across 54 framework groups on the research device, and of 1,137 artifacts from eight source groups.
- A toolchain that converts undocumented .mlmodelc bundles back into loadable .mlpackage archives, recovering 155 models.
- Eight findings. Among them: 368 artifacts store learned weights in plaintext with no encryption (369.61 MiB), all 166 MIL bundles leak internal model-database tokens, and 11 bundles carry isUpdatable=1 update hooks.

### Evaluation
- Adversarial testing: across 82,092 records (221 model paths, 77,396 prediction calls), worst-case flip rates reached 100%. Inserting zero-width spaces alone flipped 13 of 20 messages (65%) for the Indian-English SMS filter and 14 of 20 (70%) for the US-English variant (the latter using a surrogate embedding in place of the unshipped production tokenizer).
- Integrity: on the research device a 16-byte weight edit changed 7 of 8 probe inputs of the interaction-prediction model, and no runtime integrity check was observed.
- Crash: loading Apple's hand-gesture model through the public CoreML API crashed libBNNS 4 out of 4 times on production M2 hardware. Apple reproduced it but classified it as a self-crash that does not cross a security boundary, and the authors agree it is an availability and robustness issue rather than a vulnerability.
- Phishing: the SMS classifiers labeled 8 to 9 of 10 hand-written phishing messages as transactional. This is the classifier's label, not an observed inbox-routing outcome.
- Reliability: the authors acknowledge that the SMS benchmark has only 20 messages and the phishing probe 10 templates, so the percentages are not population estimates. The plaintext figure is a lower bound from scanning the first 64 KiB of each artifact, and latency was measured on a jailbroken virtual device.

#### Comment
- The novelty is the focus on models the OS runs with system privileges rather than models bundled in apps. Apple declined all three reported issues (the crash, build-token leakage and internal path disclosure) as not crossing a security boundary, so the results are best read as robustness findings rather than attack paths.
- On ethics, the authors used only public firmware and devices they own, touched no user data, and do not redistribute the weights.
- Preprint with no venue yet. The repository describes itself as an artifact for an anonymous submission, so the paper appears to be under review.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).
