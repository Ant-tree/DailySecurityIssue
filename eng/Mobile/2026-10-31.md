# Investigating Data Flows from Android Games
> Extended version (with appendix) of a paper accepted at NordSec 2026, posted as arXiv cs.CR 2610.39730. University of Copenhagen, Denmark
> 2026

Free-to-play mobile games make their money from ads, and along the way they send data to a long list of ad and analytics companies. To find out what is collected and where it goes, the authors captured and analyzed the network traffic of the top 50 free puzzle games. Each app was run ten times, with the authors checking by hand that ads were actually shown, yielding 500 traces and 16.14 GB of traffic, which they released as an open dataset.

On average a game talked to about 20 domains per run. Across all 500 runs the apps sent 125,979 HTTP(S) requests to 239 domains, 836 hosts and 12,582 endpoints. Ad and analytics networks such as AppLovin were the most frequent destinations, and request payloads carried device attributes like time zone, device model and manufacturer, OS version and battery information. The authors then picked three apps and compared user-side countermeasures: flight mode, refusing consent for personalized ads, an ad-blocking DNS server, and AdGuard. Ad-blocking DNS and similar measures cut data flows but hurt usability, while refusing consent or opting out produced inconsistent behavior, in some cases even more outgoing traffic.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39730
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.39730
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39730

### Contribution
- A traffic-level snapshot of the 2026 ad ecosystem in free mobile games, with runs manually checked to make sure ads were really triggered.
- Analysis goes beyond contacted domains, hosts and endpoints to the attributes inside request payloads.
- Shows that consent banners split refusal into dozens of items (one app lists 8 purposes and 82 vendors) that must be toggled one by one, and that "legitimate interest" has to be switched off separately for vendors such as TikTok Ad Network and Google Advertising Products.
- Releases the 500 traces (16.14 GB) as an open dataset.

### Evaluation
- Scope: the top 50 free puzzle games, each run ten times after setup, with traffic captured through mitmproxy.
- Countermeasure study: three apps (one with all three ad formats, the one with the most requests at 440.9 per run, and the one with the fewest at 78.0) compared against a baseline on the LDPlayer emulator.
- Reliability of the metrics: request and domain counts are direct measurements and easy to interpret. The scope is narrow, though: 50 apps from one genre, and only three apps in an emulator for the countermeasure study. The authors say the smaller sample is what allowed manual verification of ad display. They attribute the small gap between requests and responses mainly to certificate validation failures, so connections that could not be intercepted may be missing from the analysis.

#### Comment
- A ready-to-use public dataset for anyone checking mobile data flows and whether consent refusals are honored. The observation that refusing consent can increase traffic is a concrete reason to audit how consent management platforms are implemented.
- Limitations: 50 puzzle games and only three apps in the countermeasure study make generalization hard. The paper does not assess legal compliance.
- Venue: the authors describe it as the extended version of a NordSec 2026 paper. NordSec is a mid-sized peer-reviewed Nordic security conference.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.
- The authors are at the University of Copenhagen, Denmark; this is not a paper from a Chinese institution.

# The Privacy and Security Risks of Edge LLM Optimization
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), University of South Florida, USA
> 2026

Running large language models on edge devices requires quantization, pruning, distillation and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT). This position-style paper argues that preserving task accuracy does not mean preserving security. The author treats optimization as a transformation of the information inside the model; the resulting shifts in representations, decision paths and trust boundaries are called "security drift," and the new attack points they open are called "optimization-induced attack surfaces."

The paper walks through quantization (lower precision moves decision boundaries), pruning (parameter removal), distillation and PEFT (knowledge concentrated in small modules that become targets for theft and tampering), and activation exposure, and argues that routing and state in newer efficient designs such as mixture-of-experts and state space models are attack points too. To back the argument, it includes a small experiment on six instruction-tuned models from 1B to 8B: after INT8 and INT4 (NF4) quantization and 10% and 20% pruning, how often does a model accept a harmful prompt that its FP16 version refused?

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40543
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2610.40543
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40543

### Contribution
- Ties the main edge optimization techniques together under a single "information transformation" view and maps each to the security problems it creates.
- Proposes three measures: Decision Boundary Flip Rate, Safety Refusal Erosion Rate, and the entropy shift at the refusal decision.
- Offers practical guidance to treat small but influential components, such as PEFT modules and MoE routers, as assets that need protection.

### Evaluation
- Experiment: six models, including sub-4B models such as Llama-3.2-1B, Gemma-2-2B and Phi-3.5-mini plus 7B to 8B models, each given the same 100 prompts sampled from the public onepaneai/harmful-prompts set.
- Example results: Llama-3.2-1B lost 5% of its safety refusals at INT4 and 14% at 20% sparsity, with decision boundary flip rates of 12% to 24%. Gemma-2-2B lost only 0% to 4%, so models differ widely.
- Reliability of the metrics: each prompt was generated once (greedy decoding, at most 64 tokens), and refusals were judged by keywords such as "I cannot" and "sorry." Keyword matching can miscount answers that look like refusals and vice versa, and one-percentage-point differences carry little statistical weight. The experiment is best read as an illustration of the argument.

#### Comment
- A useful checklist reminding teams to re-run safety evaluation after quantizing or pruning an on-device LLM. It does not introduce a new attack.
- Limitations: most of the paper is literature synthesis and concepts; the experiment is small and its judging method is simple. There are no measurements on real mobile hardware.
- Venue: preprint with no announced venue.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.
- The author is at the University of South Florida, USA; this is not a paper from a Chinese institution.
