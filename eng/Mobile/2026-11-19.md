# Is 4-Bit Quantization More Than Noise to a Chat Model's Safety Behaviour? Attributing Behavioural Shifts with Matched Perturbations
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), code and labels released. University of British Columbia
> 2026

The chat models people run on phones and laptops are usually not the models that were evaluated, but 4-bit copies that a third party produced with a single command. Their capability gets checked; whether they still refuse the same requests is mostly taken on trust, and earlier audits disagree on whether 4-bit quantization lowers refusal, leaves it alone, or raises it. The author starts from the observation that a 4-bit build is just one of many weight perturbations of the same size, and designs a pre-registered study to tell whether a behavioural shift comes from the specific rounding error or would appear under any perturbation that large. Three 3-4B models (Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen3-4B) are tested in a "forced start" setting, where the opening of a compliant reply is prefilled, and a "free start" setting with held-out in-the-wild jailbreak prompts. Matched control perturbations are built from the real error by shuffling it, replacing it with Gaussian noise of the same size, or rescaling it.

### Contribution
- An experimental design that uses non-quantization perturbations matched in size to the quantization error (shuffled, Gaussian, rescaled real error) to separate the effect of the error's structure from the effect of its size.
- An 8-bit build, whose likelihood is unchanged even though most of its text differs, serves as a "no change" reference, and every result is reported as a paired shift against the original, which controls for label instability.
- A practical conclusion: under 4-bit quantization, refusal depends on the specific rounding error, not on bits per weight or likelihood, so each build has to be compared with its own original.
- Code, the pre-registration, and every judge and human label are released.

### Evaluation
- Results: two shifts with opposite signs. In the forced start, 4-bit Qwen2.5-3B continued harmful replies more often than the original. In the free start, 4-bit Llama-3.2-3B complied with held-out jailbreaks 10.3 points less often. Qwen3-4B showed no significant shift in either setting.
- Controls: shuffled and Gaussian errors averaged close to zero, while scaling the real error from its mirror image to twice its size changed behaviour almost linearly. At half the real error, Llama's likelihood was nearly unchanged but it already complied 5.8 points less often. llama.cpp's Q4_K_M, at the same bits per weight, moved neither model.
- Llama's extra refusals were not better discrimination: it also refused more benign requests that were phrased like jailbreaks.
- Reliability: paired tests, blind human labels and an 8-bit null make the design solid. As the author notes, though, it covers only three 3-4B models from two vendors, replies were cut at 128 tokens before judging, and for the same contrast the judge reported +6.2 points while human labels reported +19.4 points (same sign, very different size). Other implementations such as imatrix GGUF builds, bitsandbytes and quantization-aware training are not covered.

#### Comment
- The study shows, with proper controls, that you cannot assume an on-device copy is as safe as the original model. Since builds at the same bit width behaved differently depending on the quantizer, it is a good argument for per-build safety regression tests in the deployment pipeline.
- It is a single-author study, and because the effects go in opposite directions across models, the author is explicit that it should not be read as "4-bit quantization makes models less safe" in general.
