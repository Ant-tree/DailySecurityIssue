# Revisiting Privacy Risks in Quantized Deep Neural Networks
> Accepted to the 2026 IEEE International Conference on Big Data (per the authors), public version on arXiv cs.CR. Stevens Institute of Technology (Hoboken, USA)
> 2026

Quantization shrinks weights and activations to 8 or 4 bits so that deep learning models fit on phones and IoT devices. Because a lower-precision model should memorize less of its training data, some have hoped it would also resist membership inference attacks (attacks that tell whether a given sample was used in training), and earlier work even proposed quantization as a defense. This paper tests that hope systematically by pairing six quantization methods with eight membership inference attacks. The answer is that the 4- to 8-bit quantization used in real deployments barely reduces the privacy risk; meaningful reductions appear only at extreme settings such as 2 bits, where accuracy collapses.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37488

### Contribution
- Covers post-training quantization (PyTorch's built-in quantization, AdaRound, QDrop), quantization-aware training (PACT), and mixed-precision methods (BSQ, HAWQ), and compares six black-box attacks (shadow-model, entropy-based, and the per-sample RMIA, among others) with two white-box attacks (WB-S, WB-P) in one framework. The authors tabulate how earlier studies each covered only part of the space of quantization types, threat models, and granularity.
- Draws a practical conclusion: quantization should not be treated as a privacy safeguard. A model quantized for on-device deployment needs the same privacy review as the original.
- Shows that lower attack success is not simply a side effect of lower accuracy but depends on how quantization reshapes the output distributions of members and non-members. On ResNet-18 the two distributions stay apart and attacks keep working; on MobileNetV2 they move closer and attacks weaken.
- Shows that existing defenses (adversarial regularization, dropout, differentially private training) add little protection to quantized models while compounding the accuracy loss. Code and data are public.

### Evaluation
- Data and models: ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2 trained on CIFAR-100 and CINIC-10, and a five-layer MLP on the Location and Texas tabular datasets. Weights and activations use the same bit width.
- Metrics: attack accuracy alongside the true positive rate at a 1% false positive rate (TPR@1%FPR). On CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 at 4 bits, for example, the white-box WB-S attack reaches 81.42% TPR@1%FPR on the full-precision model and still 68.40% under PyTorch quantization.
- At 2 bits attack performance drops meaningfully, but classification accuracy falls by as much as 95% in some settings.
- The defense experiments use ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100 and compare differential privacy budgets of 5 and 1, dropout at 15% and 20%, and adversarial regularization.
- Reliability of the metrics: reporting a low fixed false-positive-rate metric follows current best practice for membership inference. However, a white-box TPR@1%FPR above 80% means the target models are heavily overfit, so the same level of risk should not be assumed for well-generalized production models. Real mobile inference runtimes (such as TensorFlow Lite) and quantized large language models are out of scope.

#### Comment
- For mobile, the key point is that "it was shrunk to run on the device, so it must be less risky" does not hold. A quantized model bundled in an app is also easy for an attacker to extract and analyze with full white-box access, which makes the white-box results the most realistic threat model here.
- Limitations: small datasets and models, mostly classic benchmarks. The conclusion says quantization "can even amplify leakage," while the abstract and the body read closer to "little effect," so the framing is inconsistent.
- Venue: IEEE BigData is a large, long-running data conference and reasonably reputable, but it is not a security venue. Acceptance is as stated by the authors; the proceedings are not yet out.
