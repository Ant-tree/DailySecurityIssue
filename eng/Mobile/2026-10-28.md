# A-GE: Privacy-Preserving Age Assurance via Fine-Grained Motion
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), industry research from Aerendir Mobile Inc. (Mountain View, USA)
> 2026

Online age checks tend to sit at one of two extremes: they ask for far more than they need, such as an ID document or a face photo, or they rely on self-declaration, which guarantees almost nothing. This paper hypothesizes that the tiny tremors picked up by a phone's accelerometer and gyroscope while it is held in the hand reflect neuromuscular and physiological traits, and it presents A-GE, which uses this signal alone to make a binary call on whether a user is at or above an age threshold. Raw sensor data never leaves the device; only the decision goes to the service, and the threshold can be set anywhere from 14 to 21. The authors stress that it works even at the roughly 60 Hz sampling rate that web browsers allow, and report up to 93.8% accuracy at a 16-year threshold.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33206

### Contribution
- Proposes deriving age evidence from inertial sensor signals during ordinary device use, with no ID document, face image or credential from an identity provider. The signal is involuntary micro-motion while holding the phone, not a deliberate gesture like a signature.
- Adds a signal quality gate that checks sample count, whether the phone is resting on a surface, and whether there are excessive impacts, and simply discards poor windows rather than filtering them. The reasoning is that aggressive denoising could also erase age-related signal.
- Fully separates development and evaluation cohorts by participant, so one person's habits cannot appear in both training and testing and inflate results.
- Also measures how well exact age, gender and handedness can be inferred from the same signal, reporting the residual privacy risk alongside the main result.

### Evaluation
- Data: a development cohort of 273 participants (ages 4 to 83) and a separate held-out cohort of 359 (ages 4 to 69). Accelerometer and gyroscope readings were collected with dedicated Android and iOS apps while participants held the phone; the only recorded attributes were gender, age and dominant hand.
- Model: XGBoost (300 trees, depth 6) over statistical, higher-order moment, time-domain and frequency-domain features.
- At the reference setting (16-year threshold, 5-second window, 500 Hz) accuracy is 89% with an F1 of 88.7%, rising to 91.1% when the probabilities from two 3-second observations are averaged. The best figures per threshold are 95.2% at 14, 93.8% at 16, 91.6% at 18 and 82.9% at 21; the web implementation reaches 86.2% at a 20-year threshold.
- Side inferences: exact-age regression is of little practical use (mean absolute error of 8.53 years), but gender is inferred fairly well (72.6% accuracy, AUC 0.807). Handedness barely leaks (AUC around 0.60).
- On-device cost: inference takes 0.65 to 3.75 ms on a Pixel 3a with under 3 MB of memory.
- Credibility of the metrics: the participant-disjoint protocol is solid. However, the "best observed" numbers in Table 6 come from choosing the observation length and sampling rate separately for each threshold, so they were tuned on the same evaluation cohort and should be read as optimistic. Only 73 participants are under 16, and the authors themselves note wide confidence intervals for subgroups. There is no head-to-head comparison with existing approaches such as face-based age estimation.

#### Comment
- From a mobile perspective, the fact that a browser's motion sensor API is enough to make an age decision cuts both ways. Services get a frictionless age check, but the same signal reveals gender with 72% accuracy, a reminder of what web pages can learn when they read sensors without a permission prompt.
- The threat model assumes sensor input cannot be forged, so it does not cover bypasses such as replaying recorded motion or using a mechanical shaker. The authors acknowledge that a deployment would need an architecture protecting everything from sensor capture to the decision, ending in a verifiable certificate.
- The full feature list is withheld as proprietary and neither data nor code is released, which makes independent reproduction hard. Every author works for the company commercializing the technology, a conflict of interest worth keeping in mind.

# From Speech Tokens to Stolen Voices: Attacker-Chosen-Text Speaker Impersonation via Representation Inversion
> arXiv cs.SD, cross-listed to cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), School of Cyberspace Science, Harbin Institute of Technology
> 2026

When a speech language model is split between a phone and a server, the device converts audio into tokens or features and sends those, keeping the raw recording local, and it is tempting to assume this protects the speaker's voice. This paper presents STIA, an attack showing that a stolen 3-second fragment of those frontend representations is enough to synthesize any sentence the attacker chooses in the victim's voice, without the original audio or its transcript. A conditional flow-matching model is trained on pairs of different utterances by the same speaker from public speech data; at attack time it is conditioned on the leaked representation and the attacker's text. No victim-specific training is needed.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34688

### Contribution
- Where prior work (SpInv) stopped at recovering speaker embeddings from frontend representations, this paper claims to be the first to turn a leaked representation directly into the victim's voice saying new content.
- A single conditioning encoder accepts discrete tokens (Moshi), mixed discrete and continuous representations (Kimi-Audio) and continuous features (Qwen3-Omni), so the attack does not depend on the frontend format.
- Control experiments that generate without the leak, without the text, or without either isolate the fact that speaker identity really does come from the leaked representation.

### Evaluation
- Data: the public Emilia dataset, split by speaker so training and evaluation share no speakers, with separate English and Chinese experiments.
- Metrics: speaker similarity (CAM++ and ECAPA cosine), word error rate on the target text measured with a fixed speech recognizer, and predicted naturalness (UTMOS).
- The baseline is a two-stage pipeline that recovers a speaker embedding with SpInv and synthesizes with SpeechT5. With a 3-second Moshi leak, STIA reaches a CAM++ similarity of 0.78 and a word error rate of 3.80%, well ahead of the baseline's 0.22 and 13.37%. It scores 0.82 on Kimi-Audio and 0.75 on Qwen3-Omni.
- Growing the leak from 2 to 8 seconds lifts similarity only slightly, from 0.76 to 0.80. Applying McAdams anonymization to the input drops similarity to 0.44 but does not eliminate it. Removing the leak entirely drops it to 0.25.
- Credibility of the metrics: the baseline is one the authors assembled themselves around an older synthesizer (SpeechT5), which may exaggerate the gap. As the authors admit, similarity scores are not pass rates against a real speaker verification system, and there is no listening test.

#### Comment
- As more voice assistants split processing between an on-device frontend and cloud inference, this is useful evidence that "we never send raw audio" is not enough to rule out voice theft. The recommendation to manage access rights and retention on every server or relay that receives the representations, not just to encrypt them in transit, is a practical one.
- Limitations: a single dataset (Emilia), a separate attack model trained per target frontend, and a single output codec (Higgs). Figure 1 uses "Hey Siri, open the door" as an example, but there are no experiments against real speaker verification systems or voice assistants.
- Verification of a Chinese institution: the authors are affiliated with Harbin Institute of Technology, a real university, and the corresponding author's email domain (hit.edu.cn) matches. Only public datasets and models are used and the training configuration is tabulated, but no code link is given. The numbers are not suspiciously good and the authors are fairly candid about limitations, though the weak baseline and the lack of verification pass rates are clear weaknesses. We judged that it meets what can be checked from public information and included it, noting that it is an unreviewed preprint.

# Phantom Forgetting: How BatchNorm State Fakes Deletion in Edge Split Machine Unlearning
> arXiv cs.CR (preprint, not peer reviewed), SUTD (Singapore), SRM University-AP and Manipal Institute of Technology (India)
> 2026

In split learning, a phone or IoT device runs the first layers of a network and a server runs the rest. When such a system processes a deletion request, the usual check is whether accuracy on the data to be forgotten has dropped. This paper shows that re-estimating only the running means and variances of Batch Normalization on the forget data, without touching a single learnable weight, is enough to make accuracy on that class collapse. In other words, an accuracy drop alone cannot tell you whether data was actually removed or whether normalization state merely shifted. The problem is worse on edge devices, whose state is easily disturbed by data drift, over-the-air updates and checkpoint rollbacks.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34800

### Contribution
- Defines a state-only diagnostic that separates changes in learnable parameters from changes in BatchNorm buffers, and measures whether the effect comes from the device side, the server side or both.
- Proposes SIFG (state-induced forgetting gap), a metric that subtracts the accuracy loss on retained data and keeps only the loss on the forget set.
- Lays out how the effect can arise in real deployments, such as non-atomic over-the-air updates, models left in training mode during validation, and integrity checks that hash only weights, and tabulates minimum verification requirements, including versioning weights together with device and server buffers.

### Evaluation
- Data and models: CIFAR-10 with a small BatchNorm convolutional network. The split model puts 32- and 64-channel blocks on the device and a 128-channel block on the server. Each of the 10 classes is used in turn as the forget class, and means and standard deviations are reported.
- In the centralized model, BatchNorm recalibration alone drops forget accuracy from 0.7357 to 0.1207, a 61.5-point fall, with exactly zero weight change. Recalibrating on retained data instead raises forget accuracy, confirming that the direction of the effect depends on the calibration distribution.
- In the split model, device-only recalibration cuts forget accuracy by 37.27 points, server-only by 41.71 and both together by 51.90. Server-only recalibration is the most selective, lowering retained accuracy by just 2.09 points, while recalibrating both sides changes about 42% of test predictions.
- Credibility of the metrics: the authors verify weight equality directly instead of assuming it, which is good. However, the standard deviations reflect differences between classes rather than random seeds, and with one dataset and one small model, the evidence for generality is thin.

#### Comment
- This is a short, five-page diagnostic paper. It does not propose a new unlearning method; it is closer to a warning not to accept an accuracy drop as proof of deletion. Its suggestion that TEE-based device attestation on mobile hardware should hash BatchNorm buffers along with weights is practically useful.
- Limitations: no comparison with retraining or gradient-based unlearning, no membership inference audit and no experiments with heterogeneous device fleets, all of which the authors leave as future work for establishing generality. No code is released.
