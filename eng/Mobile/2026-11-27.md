# Onnx2TEE: Automated Graph-Aware Model Partitioning for TEE-based Edge AI Protection
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Tennessee Technological University, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)
> 2026

Once privileged software such as the operating system is compromised, an AI model deployed on an edge device can have its decrypted weights lifted straight out of memory at inference time. A TEE such as TrustZone can protect those weights, but secure-world memory is too small to hold a large model in full. The authors present Onnx2TEE, a pipeline that takes an ONNX model, cuts it into units that preserve the computational graph's dependencies (single-entry, single-exit segments), proposes partitions that minimize the tensors crossing the boundary between the rich execution environment (REE) and the TEE, and then automatically generates a GlobalPlatform-compliant client application (CA) and trusted application (TA). Two design choices stand out. Instead of putting a general-purpose inference engine inside the TEE, it uses emmtrix's ONNX-to-C translation to emit C code for only the operators it needs, so the trusted computing base does not grow. And it estimates the TEE heap requirement of every candidate partition up front, filtering out configurations that cannot be deployed.

### Contribution
- A graph-based partitioning algorithm that keeps dependencies intact even in non-sequential feed-forward networks with branches, merges, and skip connections. Unlike DarkneTZ and EffTEE, which are tied to layer-wise partitioning and Darknet models, it accepts ONNX models exported from PyTorch, TensorFlow, and Keras as they are.
- End-to-end automation from the partitioning decision through TA/CA code generation to memory-based deployability checks, which removes the burden of hand-writing a trusted application for each model.
- Measurements showing that, for weight protection, placing the parameter-heavy segment in the TEE matters more than the number of segments placed there, and that TEE memory depends heavily on boundary tensor sizes rather than segment count.

### Evaluation
- Setup: NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin developer kit, Arm TrustZone, OP-TEE 3.19 as the trusted OS. Two models: a small CNN-based MNIST classifier and YOLO26n.
- Parameter distribution: in MNIST, 97.06% of the parameters sit in segment 8 of 10; in YOLO26n, 96.96% sit in segment 17 of 19.
- TEE heap: every valid MNIST configuration fit in TEE memory, but many YOLO26n configurations exceeded the budget. For example, (P7, P11) is deployable while (P7, P10), which has one segment fewer, is not, because moving the output boundary past a downsampling step shrank the duplicated output buffer from 6,400 KiB to 1,600 KiB.
- Latency: REE-only execution was fastest for both models. For MNIST, even the TEE partition with the fewest parameters took 93% longer than the REE baseline. The relative gap was smaller for YOLO26n.
- Credibility: the only baseline is REE-only execution; there is no quantitative comparison with earlier partitioning schemes such as DarkneTZ or EffTEE, and only two models are tested. The authors also note that the measured latency cannot separate world-switch overhead from computation inside the TEE.

#### Comment
- The most telling result is that in YOLO26n the largest deployable partition protected only 1.51% of the parameters (segment 10), not segment 17 with its 96.96%. In other words, the paper itself shows that its own advice to "put the parameter-heavy part in the TEE" is hard to apply to a YOLO-scale model under a real Jetson TrustZone budget.
- The threat model targets weight exfiltration (stealing an exact copy). Model extraction through the intermediate tensors that cross the TEE boundary, and side channels against the TEE itself, are out of scope.
