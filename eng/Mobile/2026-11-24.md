Subcategory: TEE and side channels (speech eavesdropping through camera metadata)

# Your Camera Can Hear You: Side-Channel Acoustic Eavesdropping via Android Camera2 Metadata
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). State Key Laboratory of Blockchain and Data Security, Zhejiang University; Electric Power Research Institute of Guangdong Power Grid
> 2026

Android puts several layers of protection around SensorManager so that motion sensors cannot act as hidden microphones. Since Android 12, the gyroscope and accelerometer are capped at 200 Hz by default, higher rates need the HIGH_SAMPLING_RATE_SENSORS permission, and turning off the system-wide microphone switch throttles them back to 200 Hz even when that permission is granted. The authors show that the Camera2 subsystem gets around all of this. While a camera session is open, the optical and electronic image stabilization (OIS/EIS) pipelines in the Camera HAL record high-rate gyroscope readings and hand them to apps through vendor-defined CaptureResult metadata, and speech played through the loudspeaker shakes the chassis enough to show up in those readings. Of 50 phones from 10 vendors released between 2020 and 2026, 37 expose this metadata, 26 exceed the 200 Hz cap, and six recent flagships stream it at 800 to 1000 Hz. The channel needs neither RECORD_AUDIO nor HIGH_SAMPLING_RATE_SENSORS, only CAMERA. On top of this, the authors build CamEar, a speech recognizer trained purely on leakage synthesized by a physics model, so it works on phone models it has never seen.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27964
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.27964
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27964

### Contribution
- Measurements show that switching off the microphone does not slow the Camera2 metadata, and switching off the camera only blanks the video frames while the 800 to 1000 Hz metadata keeps flowing. A correlation analysis (R² of at least 0.9993) confirms that Camera2 metadata and SensorManager read the same MEMS gyroscope.
- Because the sensor hub does not isolate rates per client, opening a camera session also lifts other SensorManager listeners on the same device to 400 to 500 Hz without any permission, a "rate spillover" the paper also reports.
- Earlier learning-based attacks needed tens of hours of data from the target phone. Source-to-Leakage Learning (S2L) separates speech content from each phone's mechanical response and synthesizes training data with a physics-constrained forward simulator, so the attack generalizes without any target-device data.
- Lowering the metadata rate sharply cuts accuracy, which gives a concrete fix; the authors say they disclosed the issue to the affected vendors.

### Evaluation
- Targets: six phones that export 800 to 1000 Hz metadata (vivo X300s, X300 Pro, X200 Ultra, Xiaomi 17T Pro, HONOR Magic8 Pro Air, Redmi K90 Max, all on Android 16). Training used only LibriSpeech train-960 passed through the simulator. Testing used 816 utterances from 16 unseen speakers played through each phone's loudspeaker, 4,896 measured traces in total.
- Results: with no target-device data, mean word accuracy was 52.1% (up to 61.8%) and character accuracy 74.3% (up to 80.7%), rising to 60.1% word accuracy with text-only LLM correction. The prior method ISpyU, fine-tuned on 24 hours of data from one phone, reached only 25.9% on other phones and 36.6% even on its own phone, so CamEar came out ahead on every device.
- Mitigation: capping the metadata at 400 to 499 Hz dropped word accuracy to 32.4%, and the 200 Hz cap dropped it to 2.3%.
- Reliability: the default setup places the phone on a rigid table playing speech at full volume, which is kinder to the attacker than a real call. ISpyU's code is not public, so the authors reimplemented it, and the baseline may therefore underperform the original. Still, test speakers do not overlap with training and accuracy sits around 50%, so the numbers do not look inflated.

#### Comment
- A clear case of inconsistent mediation across APIs in the mobile permission model. Third-party libraries inside apps that legitimately use the camera, such as video calling, AR or vision SDKs, could be the attacker, which matters for app review and SDK supply chains.
- Because this paper comes from a Chinese institution (Zhejiang University), it was checked more closely. The corresponding author, Bingsheng Zhang, is a real researcher at Zhejiang University, and the devices, measurement conditions and evaluation splits are described in detail. No code or data link was found in the paper, so reproducibility is limited. It is a preprint with no venue yet.

# Correct Precedents Induce Policy Violations in On-Device LLM Agents
> arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence (MBZUAI)
> 2026

In local-cloud agent systems, a cloud model plans and a small on-device model carries out actions on private data. The on-device agent, which the authors call the guard, is the last checkpoint before irreversible actions such as deleting a file or issuing a refund. The paper reports, for the first time, that a guard can be pushed into a violation by its own correct decisions earlier in the same session. After it rightly deletes a file whose legal hold was lifted, it becomes more likely to delete a similar file a few items later whose hold is still active. Since every earlier decision was correct, an audit has no faulty step to point at. The authors call this correct-precedent generalization and fix it with Paired Decision Distillation (PDD), which distills the decisions of a weakly affected teacher into a LoRA adapter.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27216
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.27216
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.27216

### Contribution
- Defines a new failure mode in which an agent breaks a restriction after a history with no faulty step or injected instruction. Prior work only looked at histories containing something wrong.
- Isolates the effect by comparing two queues that differ in a single earlier item (a released look-alike versus an ordinary item), and shows that the risk compounds when a refused request is simply resent in a new session.
- History interventions show that the guard copies its action on the look-alike to the restricted item instead of checking that item's status. Writing each item's status next to it removes most of the effect.
- PDD restores the safe rate to nearly 100% with no extra prompt text, tool calls or model calls at inference time.

### Evaluation
- Setup: 13 open-weight models from nine developers, each small enough for a single GPU (the seven largest as 4-bit checkpoints). Three generated task families (file cleanup, refunds, package cleanup), ten-item queues with the restricted item at position 7 and the comparison item at position 3. Violations are detected by parsing the JSON tool call directly, with no LLM judge.
- Results: one precedent lowered the safe rate in all 13 models, roughly halving it in the most affected one (Nemotron-3.5). Over ten repeated requests, at least one release became more than four times as likely as without the precedent (48% versus 6% in the illustrated case). The effect also showed up in some models on τ²-bench, in an adaptation of the Minions setup, and with thinking enabled.
- Mitigation: PDD brought the safe rate back to nearly 100% in every distilled model while staying non-inferior to each base model on MMLU-Pro and XSTest. Five inference-time fixes, including a status-lookup tool and a clean-context gate, also helped but all add overhead at deployment.
- Reliability: the design and analysis of each confirmatory test were fixed in a time-stamped commit before data collection, with bootstrap intervals clustered by family and instance and permutation tests. The main protocol, however, runs with thinking off, JSON-text actions, generated queues and a scripted cloud side, so effect sizes in real deployments may differ.

#### Comment
- For hybrid agent designs that rely on an on-device model as the final safeguard, the message is that checking one request at a time is not enough and guards need to be evaluated over whole sessions. This applies directly to mobile assistants where a small local model handles personal data.
- The statistics are careful, but no code link was found in the paper. It is a preprint with no venue yet.
