# On the Same Track: Privacy Leaks in Electric Rail Transport via Magnetic Fields
> Published at ACM CCS 2026 (the paper carries the CCS '26 proceedings header, DOI 10.1145/3830454.3846554); arXiv version. TU Berlin, BIFOLD
> 2026

This paper shows that a smartphone magnetometer picks up the magnetic field generated by an electric train's traction current, and that this is enough to tell, without any location permission, whether a user is on a train and whether two users are on the same one. Railways in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Norway and Sweden run on 15 kV, 16.7 Hz AC; the current flowing through the overhead line, rails and car body produces a field with a clear 16.7 Hz component inside the passenger cabin. Neither Android nor iOS gates the magnetometer behind a permission, so any installed app can record this signal silently. The authors build two attacks on top of it: a transport mode detector that decides whether the user is riding an electric train from spectral analysis alone, and a colocation attack that compares the 16.7 Hz traces of two devices to decide whether they share a train.

### Contribution
- First demonstration that the magnetometer, usually treated as a low-risk sensor that mostly measures the Earth's field, reveals train rides and co-travellers on electrified lines.
- Transport mode detection uses no learned model: a simple detector looks only at the spectrum around 16.7 Hz. It matches multi-sensor machine-learning approaches while being cheap enough to run continuously in the background.
- Colocation is implemented as a 1D CNN that takes two traces stacked as input channels. It is more accurate than a DTW-distance approach and on average 16 times faster (43 times on the longest recordings), which makes one-to-many comparisons practical.
- A public dataset of 217 journeys that, unlike existing transport mode datasets, distinguishes electric trains.

### Evaluation
- Data: regular commuters recorded 217 journeys while using their phones normally (calls, messaging, phone in pocket, bag, hand or on a table), giving 357 segments with at least two colocated recordings. Participants logged stops and departures to create labels and consented to the release.
- Transport mode detection: a single 10-second decision reaches MCC 0.83 (F1 0.94); a majority vote over the first 300 seconds after departure reaches MCC 0.93 (F1 0.98). The authors compare against prior work that reports F1 0.70 to 0.93 on other datasets (HTC and others), and note that the datasets differ, which limits a direct comparison.
- Colocation: the prior approach (Nguyen et al., field magnitude with DDTW) scores MCC 0.09, close to chance, in this paper's harder setting where all compared traces have the same length. Extracting the 16.7 Hz component and then using DTW gives 0.50, and the CNN gives 0.79 (F1 0.89). Both work best on the first 300 seconds; with only 60 seconds the CNN drops to 0.66. There is no clear difference between long-distance and regional trains.
- Reliability: train and test data are split by journey so that no journey appears on both sides, and results are averaged over 10 independent splits. For colocation there is only one baseline, and its reproduction conditions differ from the original study, which partly explains its low score.

#### Comment
- The key point is that blocking location permissions is not enough: a permissionless sensor still reveals when someone took a train and who they travelled with. If the same app or ad SDK is installed on many phones, this extends to inferring social ties.
- Limitations: the attack is confined to 16.7 Hz railways in central and northern Europe. The authors expect 25 Hz systems to be reachable with today's 100 Hz sampling, but 50 Hz systems coincide with the public power grid frequency, so the signal stops being railway-specific. They also point out that seeing a 16.7 Hz component at all already reveals the region the user is in.
- The proposed countermeasure is an explicit permission for magnetometer access in mobile operating systems. Limiting background sensor sampling rates is worth considering alongside it.
- CCS is a top-tier security venue, and the code, data and artifact are all public.
