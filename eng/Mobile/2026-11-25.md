Subcategory: Other (defending live calls against voice impersonation)

# Ultrasound Watermark: Real-time Acoustic Watermarking against Voice Scams on Smartphones
> arXiv preprint (not peer-reviewed). University of Maryland, Baltimore County (UMBC), Indiana University Bloomington, University of Massachusetts Amherst, Bloomberg L.P., Amazon
> 2026

Modern text-to-speech and voice conversion make phone scams that impersonate friends or institutions far more convincing. Existing defenses tend to analyze recordings after the fact, break down once audio passes through telephony codecs, or fall to adaptive attacks. The authors propose Ultrasound Watermark: during a call, the phone's speaker emits ultrasound, the microphone picks up reflections from the caller's moving lips and jaw, and features of that motion are fused with the outgoing speech to form a watermark. Because the watermark is tied in time to actual articulation, speech that is synthesized or replayed without that motion should not be able to carry a matching watermark. The generator runs on the caller's phone and the detector on the callee's, with no extra hardware.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30106
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.30106
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.30106

### Contribution
- Ties the voice watermark not to "who is speaking" but to "is this person physically speaking right now", using only the phone's built-in speaker and microphone for both ultrasonic sensing and embedding.
- Trains the generator and detector jointly while enforcing cross-modal consistency between speech and ultrasonic motion, so the detector can separate replay, synthesized speech and articulation-mimicking attacks.
- Applies call distortions (band filters, resampling, MP3/AAC, noise, echo, Encodec compression and more) during both training and evaluation.
- Ships iOS and Android apps that process 200 ms segments in real time.

### Evaluation
- Data: more than 2.5 hours of synchronized speech and ultrasound recordings from 22 speakers, 8 phones and 4 real-world environments. Metrics are accuracy, TPR, FPR, attack success rate and AUC.
- Results: audio-only attacks and attacks that add random ultrasound (both replay and synthesis) were rejected with AUC 1.00 and FPR at or below 0.01. The hardest case, attaching synchronized ultrasound taken from another person's real speech, dropped AUC to 0.84 (replay) and 0.88 (synthesis), with FPR rising to 0.30-0.37.
- In a user study spanning several days, both call modes (speaker and earpiece) and varied phone positions, the watermark was detected in every session. On recent devices the generator took 15-24 ms and the detector under 8 ms; even older devices stayed under 200 ms per segment.
- Reliability: as the authors admit, band-pass and high-pass filtering, AAC and low-bitrate Opus compression weaken the watermark substantially. No end-to-end test over real cellular voice codecs (AMR, EVS and the like) was found in the paper. Twenty-two speakers is a small sample for generalizing across individual articulation patterns.

#### Comment
- Adding a proof of live speech with nothing but phone hardware makes this a practical line of work against voice scams. Both caller and callee need the app, though, so a real deployment hinges on how users are told that a call carries no watermark when the scammer simply calls without one.
- An FPR above 0.3 under synchronized-ultrasound attacks means the representation does not separate speakers well, and the authors leave speaker-conditioned detection to future work.
- No code or data link was found in the paper. It is a preprint with no venue yet.

# MobileGrail: Learning Compact Guardrails for On-Device deployable Mobile GUI Agent Safety
> arXiv preprint (not peer-reviewed). UC Davis, CodeDroid
> 2026

Runtime guardrails check each action a mobile GUI agent proposes before it runs, but they have relied on large cloud-hosted multimodal models, which means every screen, including payments, messages and medical records, goes to a remote server. The authors propose MobileGrail, a roughly 2B-parameter guardrail that runs on the device. It keeps a recurrent task state holding only the evidence later safety decisions depend on (what the user authorized, how far the task has progressed), so that this evidence survives a short context window. It also learns two separate labels, unsafe (the action that commits a violation) and block (any action inside the intervention window), to reduce interventions that come too early or too late.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29847
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.29847
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29847

### Contribution
- Points out that existing datasets fold "looks risky", "violation committed" and "should intervene" into a single unsafe label, which blurs the safety boundary, and splits the labels around an intervention window that runs from the first action the user's authorization no longer explains to the action that commits the violation.
- Builds a training environment of 32 apps whose backends verify the effect of every action, providing labels for supervised learning and rewards for reinforcement learning.
- Releases MobileVeto, an offline benchmark of 210 unsafe trajectories, mostly on held-out apps, each paired with a benign counterpart.
- Runs the 4-bit quantized model on a Snapdragon 8 Elite and measures real latency.

### Evaluation
- Offline (MobileVeto): MobileGrail on Qwen3.5-2B (SFT plus GRPO) intercepted 76.2% of violations (64.5% for the same model prompted), had a premature-intervention rate of 5.2% (58.6% for the prompted model) and a benign false-positive rate of 15.7% (75.7%). The cloud-based OS-Sentinel (Kimi-K2.6) intercepted 39.3% with a 57.1% benign false-positive rate, worse than MobileGrail on all five metrics. Gemini 3.8 Flash, used as the labeling teacher, remained the strongest at 95.5% interception.
- Online (110 GhostEI-Bench tasks): guarding a Qwen3.8-27B agent cut fully successful attacks from 13 to 3 and raised task completion from 48 to 58.
- Performance: with Q4_0 quantization the median latency was 4.70 s with 3.67 GiB resident memory, close to cloud-hosted Gemini 3.8 Flash at 5.11 s.
- Reliability: Gemini 3.8 Flash produced the training labels and rationales and also serves as a reference point, so the teacher's judgment is baked into the results. The 210 benchmark trajectories come from the same kind of environment the authors built, so generalization to commercial apps at large is not yet shown.

#### Comment
- A guardrail that never sends the screen off the device is a meaningful framing for mobile privacy. At 4-5 seconds per action check, though, it still weighs on how responsive an agent feels.
- No code or model release link was found in the paper. It is a preprint with no venue yet.

# NRSnooper: Enabling Real-Time Bidirectional Traffic Decoding in Commercial 5G NR Networks
> arXiv preprint (not peer-reviewed). KAIST, Politecnico di Milano
> 2026

Decoding both uplink and downlink lower-layer 5G NR traffic exposes protocol behavior, unprotected signaling, and the size and timing of encrypted higher-layer packets, well beyond what scheduling metadata alone reveals. In commercial networks this is hard: per-device configurations are hidden in encrypted RRC messages, the identifiers of ongoing connections are unknown, and wideband signals are expensive to process in real time. NRSnooper has the attacker attach an open-source UE (OAI UE) with a legitimate SIM, replay the capabilities of commercial phones, and learn the physical-layer configurations the network hands out so they can be reused. It then recovers connection identifiers (C-RNTIs) from ongoing transmissions, picks the matching configuration through CRC-validated decoding, and decodes uplink and downlink data in real time.

Links:
- arXiv abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29439
- arXiv HTML full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2611.29439
- arXiv PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29439

### Contribution
- First demonstration on commercial networks of a passive 5G NR sniffer that decodes traffic after encrypted configuration updates and from devices that were already connected.
- Combines syndrome-guided C-RNTI recovery, control-channel candidate pruning, polar decoding and session-specific DMRS identity recovery, with a hybrid CPU-GPU pipeline that keeps up with 100 MHz channels.
- Replays 21 commercial UE capability sets and 83 mutated variants to collect per-network configuration profiles. The authors describe the sniffer as open source, but no release link was found in the paper.
- Argues that 6G should make lower-layer confidentiality an explicit requirement.

### Evaluation
- Testbed (srsRAN gNB): using gNB logs as ground truth, every CRC-valid transport block matched the gNB payload byte for byte. NRSnooper recovered 74.98-98.61% of downlink and 87.87-97.16% of uplink transport blocks.
- Two commercial networks (country K on n78 and country U on n41, both 100 MHz SA): compared with using only configurations from unencrypted signaling, it decoded 1.3x and 6.3x as many control messages and 9.0x and 17.7x as many downlink data blocks. It also decoded traffic from a device in the middle of a phone call.
- Hardware: a four-antenna USRP N310, an i7-14700K and an RTX 3080 Ti.
- Reliability: commercial networks have no ground truth, so the figures compare CRC-valid block counts with and without each technique rather than coverage of all transmitted traffic. Target-device experiments used only the authors' own phones.

#### Comment
- From Yongdae Kim's group at KAIST, this extends the LTESniffer line of work to 5G standalone commercial networks. The point that protecting RRC reconfiguration alone is not enough matters for operators and standards bodies.
- The authors mention that configuration profiles could narrow down phone models but do not evaluate identification. The paper includes a separate ethics section.
- It is a preprint with no venue yet.
- Full text source: arXiv PDF (downloaded on the user's computer).
