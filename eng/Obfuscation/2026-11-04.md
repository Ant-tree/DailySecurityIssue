# Rethinking Neural Decompilation: Candidate Selection as the Hidden Bottleneck
> Published at ICICS (International Conference on Information and Communications Security), posted as arXiv cs.CR 2611.02788. Institute for IT Security, University of Lübeck, Germany
> 2026

LLM-based neural decompilers produce more readable and functionally accurate code than rule-based tools like Ghidra and IDA Pro, but there is a large gap between their best-of-many performance and the accuracy of the single output they actually return. The gap widens under obfuscation such as control-flow flattening (FLA) and bogus control flow (BCF), to the point that the original LLM4Decompile paper concluded its released checkpoints were unsuitable for obfuscated inputs.

This paper splits decompilation into candidate generation and candidate selection. Analyzing the LLM4Decompile architecture, the authors find that the ground-truth token is among the model's top five next-token predictions at 95.6% of positions on average, and that sampling many outputs reaches a pass@100 of 93% on HumanEval-Decompile. In other words, the correct reconstruction is usually already in the model's sampling space; the problem is picking it out. They systematically compare a generate-then-select pipeline that ranks 100 candidates per input with text, syntax, embedding, consensus, likelihood and execution-based selectors. They also build and release ExeBench-Obfuscated, a dataset of 362,647 samples obfuscated with OLLVM.

### Contribution
- A token-level analysis showing that correct reconstructions are mostly already in the model's sampling space. Errors concentrate in identifier names and qualifiers such as const and static, which compilation discards.
- A systematic comparison of selectors that establishes negative perplexity (the model's own likelihood) as the strongest static selector on HumanEval-Decompile.
- Release of the 362,647-sample ExeBench-Obfuscated dataset, and a demonstration that one epoch of fine-tuning on it, combined with the selection pipeline, brings pass@1 on obfuscated binaries to 88.8%, refuting the earlier claim that the released checkpoints are unusable on obfuscated inputs.

### Evaluation
- Setup: LLM4Decompile-Ref-9B-v2 for Ghidra pseudo-code input and LLM4Decompile-End-6.7B-v1.5 for assembly input, across four configurations combining HumanEval-Decompile and ExeBench. Candidates were sampled at temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, 100 per input, with results averaged over five seeds.
- Results: the gap between random selection and the oracle (success if any candidate passes) was 16.3 to 33.4 points. Among static selectors, negative perplexity was best in three of four configurations, reaching 73.3% pass@1 on HumanEval-Decompile with pseudo-code input. Execution-guided selection, which runs tests to choose, reached 86.3% and 75.4% on ExeBench, within 0.5 points of the oracle. Generating candidates adds about 12.6 seconds per input.
- Reliability of the metrics: the candidate pool is fixed before any selector runs, so comparisons between selectors are fair. Inputs with no compilable candidate were excluded from the selection analysis, and execution-guided selection needs input-output tests, which real reverse engineering may not have. The 88.8% figure comes after fine-tuning on OLLVM-obfuscated data, so generalization to obfuscators not seen in training needs separate study.

#### Comment
- Worth noting for anyone building obfuscators: classic OLLVM transformations such as flattening and bogus control flow can be largely undone by a neural decompiler fine-tuned on those transformations and paired with candidate selection. When assessing obfuscation strength, the attack model should be "generate many candidates and select," not "run a decompiler once."
- Limitations: the unit is a single C function; cross-function structure and whole-program recovery are not covered.
