# A Model Obfuscation and Controllable Authorization Scheme for Deep Learning as a Service
> arXiv preprint (not peer-reviewed). School of Artificial Intelligence, Beijing Normal University; City University of Hong Kong
> 2026

When deep learning models are shipped to edge or third-party devices, reverse engineering can leak their weights and architecture. Existing protection either depends on special hardware (such as TEEs) or heavy computation (such as homomorphic encryption), and even when it protects the model it does not control who may use it. The authors propose KeyObf, which hides parameters and architecture through model obfuscation and ties the compensation step that restores the obfuscated parameters to a user key, so that only authorized users get correct inference. Selected layers get a coupling transformation that multiplies their outputs by a scaling factor and a key-derived factor, and the inference library recovers these factors from the user key, user identifier and authorization credential to compensate.

### Contribution
- Merges model obfuscation and usage authorization into one design: even if authentication is bypassed, the model cannot produce correct outputs without the key-derived compensation factors.
- Derives key factors deterministically with HMAC-SHA256 and assigns an independent factor to each output feature (each output channel in convolutional layers), and compares how channel-grouping granularity affects key-factor recovery.
- Presents a theorem showing that compensation after the coupling transformation restores the original model's output.
- Runs on TFLite models without special hardware.

### Evaluation
- Models: VGG11, MobileNetV2 and ResNet20/32/44/56 on CIFAR-100, with 10 coupling positions per model.
- Accuracy and cost: with the correct key and credential, Top-1 accuracy changed by -0.01 points on average (71.39% to 71.38%). Average inference time over 10,000 test images grew from 208.65 ms to 391.31 ms, about 1.9x, and ResNet56 went from 293.66 ms to 632.77 ms.
- Authorization: with a wrong key or no credential, the inference library rejected the request at authentication. When authentication was bypassed under controlled conditions, a variant without key binding still inferred normally while full KeyObf could not compensate correctly.
- Reverse-engineering resistance: using the dynamic instrumentation tool DLModelExplorer, the weight extraction rate was 48.9% for KeyObf and 51.6% for the earlier DynaMO. Baselines are ModelObfuscator and DynaMO.
- Reliability: the baselines are published work on the same problem, which is appropriate, but the evaluation is limited to small CIFAR-100 models with no large models and no measurements on real mobile devices. As the roughly 50% extraction rate shows, the scheme focuses less on preventing extraction than on keeping extracted weights from producing correct inference.

#### Comment
- This fits the practical need to protect on-device models inside apps and gate their use with license keys. Because the key factors are recovered on the user's device, though, it does not fundamentally stop an authorized user from dumping the compensated weights from memory with their own key, and the paper does not evaluate this threat.
- Extra checks were made because the authors are at Chinese institutions. Co-author Xiaohua Jia is a professor at City University of Hong Kong whose lab page and CV are public. The results are not suspiciously strong (the paper reports the inference slowdown and the 48.9% extraction rate as they are), and the baselines are prior work on the same problem. There is no code link and no venue yet, so reproducibility could not be confirmed.
