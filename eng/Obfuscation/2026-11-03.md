# Evaluating Structural and SAT Attack Resilience in Cryptographic Dynamic Logic Locking via Keystream Capping
> arXiv cs.CR 2610.41083 (preprint, not peer reviewed), Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, UNC Charlotte, USA
> 2026

Logic locking is a hardware obfuscation technique that inserts key gates into a circuit design so that the circuit produces wrong outputs without the correct key. It protects design intellectual property in a supply chain where design and fabrication pass through many outside parties. Conventional static locking keeps a fixed relationship between key, input and output, which makes it vulnerable to SAT attacks that use a working chip as an oracle. STREAMLOCK is a dynamic scheme that breaks that assumption by changing the output masking every clock cycle with a stream-cipher keystream.

This paper identifies and fixes two weaknesses in STREAMLOCK. First, if masking sits only at the primary outputs, the combinational logic inside the netlist is left intact, and an adversary can tap the signals before the mask at gate level and recover functionality without solving for the key (a structural removal attack). The authors use fault-propagation analysis to pick internal nodes with high output impact and spread key-dependent masking across them. Second, moving the mask inside is not enough: in wide circuits many keystream bits are exposed per cycle, and SAT solvers could extract the key algebraically. To stop this, they cap the number of distinct keystream bits used per cycle at K (7 in this paper).

### Contribution
- Shows that dynamic locking with output-only keystream masking falls to structural removal regardless of cipher strength. The issue is where the mask is placed, not how the keystream is generated.
- Proposes distributing masking across internal nodes selected by fault-propagation analysis.
- Shows experimentally that internal placement alone does not prevent algebraic key extraction, and introduces a keystream reuse cap that bounds per-cycle exposure independently of circuit width.

### Evaluation
- Targets: three ISCAS'85 benchmark circuits (including C17 and C432). The original and hardened designs were tested against structural removal and a bounded SAT-style attack that unrolls the circuit over several cycles.
- Results: internal distribution grew the uninformed attacker's brute-force space for the largest circuit from 6 candidate node subsets to more than 10^45. With the reuse cap, the SAT attack went straight from fast solving to timeouts without revealing the key.
- Reliability of the metrics: only three small classic benchmarks were used. The SAT attack is a bounded unrolling setup built by the authors, not a comparison against the full range of recent attacks on dynamic locking. "Timeout" depends on the chosen time limit, so the security level cannot be stated quantitatively.

#### Comment
- There is a lesson for software obfuscation too: protection placed only at the output boundary leaves the internal logic exposed, much like an app that wraps only its verification code and leaves the core logic untouched.
- Limitations: the evaluation is small, and from the parts read, the quantitative comparison of area and power overhead is thin. It is a short eight-page paper.
- Venue: preprint with no announced venue.
- Full-text source: the official arXiv PDF, downloaded on the user's computer.
